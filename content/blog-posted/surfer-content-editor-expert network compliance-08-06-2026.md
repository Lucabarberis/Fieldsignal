## Insider Trading Law, MNPI, and Why Expert Networks Are Scrutinized

Regulators started paying close attention to expert networks after a wave of enforcement actions between 2009 and 2012. Cases like [SEC vs. Broadband Research / John Kinnucan](https://www.sec.gov/enforcement-litigation/litigation-releases/lr-22261) showed how experts connected through networks were tipping hedge funds with nonpublic information. The SEC regulates expert calls for insider trading in the US. The FCA enforces market-fairness rules in the UK. These regulatory actions forced large funds to demand strict compliance processes from every network they used.

The core legal framework sits in [Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act](https://www.sec.gov/about/about-securities-laws) of 1934 and Rule 10b-5. Trading on material non public information obtained in breach of a duty is illegal. Regulatory frameworks include securities laws and market abuse regulations. Insider trading can trigger fines in the millions for individuals and firms involved. Fines for non-compliance can reach tens of millions.

Three legal theories drive most enforcement. The classical theory covers a corporate insider who trades on their own company's secrets. The tipper-tippee theory applies when someone passes confidential information in breach of duty and the recipient trades on it, which is the scenario most relevant to expert calls. The misappropriation theory applies when someone misuses information owed to another party. If a board member shares non public earnings guidance during an expert call and the client trades, that's a textbook tipper-tippee or misappropriation violation under insider trading laws.

Mosaic theory offers a defense. Combining many pieces of public or non-material information into a useful picture is permissible, as long as no duty is breached and no single piece crosses into nonpublic information territory. This is how legitimate primary research works. You're synthesizing specialized knowledge from subject matter experts, not soliciting secrets.

## Key Risks in the Use of Expert Networks

Risk extends well beyond insider trading charges. Legal exposure, reputational damage, and bad data quality all create problems for expert networks clients. Here's how each one shows up in practice.

### MNPI and Insider Trading Exposure

Material non public information can lead to insider trading charges even when neither side intended to cross a line. Common scenarios include:

* An expert currently employed at a public company mentions unreleased quarterly results during a call about industry trends.

* Someone involved in clinical trials discloses interim data before the sponsor's public announcement.

* A former executive shares details of an unannounced tender offer or M&A activity they learned about through ongoing board relationships.

Trading after such a call can trigger SEC investigations. Both expert networks and clients are expected to show they had controls to prevent and respond to MNPI incidents. In the Choi/Nvidia case, a current accounting manager shared upstream earnings guidance through expert network pathways. Profits for connected funds ran into millions.

### Reputational and Regulatory Risk

A breach can damage client trust and reputation overnight. Being named in an SEC order or press article involving improper use of expert network services can immediately scare LPs, customers, and acquirers.

* SEC examiners look for failures in tracking expert calls during compliance exams of funds and advisers. The SEC's [FY 2026 Examination Priorities](https://www.troutman.com/insights/sec-division-of-examination-announces-fiscal-year-2026-priorities/) emphasize strong compliance programs, governance, and record-keeping.

* Weak network compliance, meaning no training, no audit trails, inconsistent firm's policies, is itself a red flag for regulators and any institutional investor conducting due diligence.

* Even unproven allegations create "headline risk" that shows up in LP DDQs and board conversations.

If you're a junior associate or analyst, assume your expert calls may be reviewed years later. Plan your documentation accordingly.

### Data Protection, Privacy, and Cyber Risk

Expert networks hold sensitive information: expert identities, employment details, consultation histories, and sometimes call transcripts. That makes them attractive targets. Expert networks must comply with GDPR for data protection in Europe, and data breaches can lead to significant GDPR penalties.

Cross-border calls create specific risk. A U.S. fund speaking with an EU-based expert must handle data under international data transfer rules. Compliance frameworks focus on lawful data processing and retention limits. Expert networks implement encryption and access controls for data security. Expert networks must adapt to international data transfer rules as calls increasingly span jurisdictions.

When evaluating vendors, ask about encryption in transit and at rest, role-based access, incident-response plans, and data retention policies for call notes and transcripts. This isn't an IT side topic. It's a core part of ensuring confidentiality in your compliance processes.

### Information Quality and Conflict-Driven Bias

Poor vetting introduces experts whose incentives skew the relevant information you receive. This creates decision risk that can corrupt investment decisions, IC decks, and board materials.

Concrete examples of potential conflicts: a current supplier hoping to secure contracts, a former executive with an unresolved legal dispute against the target company, or a consultant trying to sell their own consulting services. Networks that take conflicts seriously tend to deliver more reliable integrity research. Compliance and quality are linked.

## Core Components of a Compliant Expert Network Program

Any serious expert network should be able to demonstrate these controls on request. They need to be repeatable, documented, and auditable. Periodic compliance monitoring includes testing systems and spot-checking interactions. Here's the full lifecycle.

### Expert Vetting and Onboarding

Expert vetting includes identity verification through public records, employment history, and current role confirmation before any project is offered.

* Networks check for high-risk affiliations: current officers of public companies, employees of regulators, anyone under strict non disclosure agreements or trial confidentiality.

* Vetting processes assess experts' employment status and conflicts of interest.

* Experts sign clear terms covering MNPI, proprietary information, and contractual obligations before their first call.

* Experts are required to annually re-sign terms and conditions regarding MNPI and NDAs through the network's internal database.

* FieldSignal prioritizes ex-employees, customers, and partners when appropriate, which reduces MNPI exposure on many projects by design.

### Client Project Review and Scoping

Compliant networks review client briefs before sourcing certain experts. The goal is to identify MNPI-sensitive topics and flag banned issuers.

* Clients should supply their own restricted lists, employer preference lists, and any internal expert call rules.

* Conservative scoping avoids questions on non public financials, unannounced transactions, and embargoed clinical or regulatory results.

* FieldSignal supports tailored scoping for PE/VC, corporate, and consulting clients with different compliance rules per project, not just a one-size-fits-all process.

### Pre-Call Disclosures and Attestations

Pre-call attestations confirm experts won't share material non public information or breach any fiduciary duty to current or former employers. Experts must attest not to share confidential information at both booking and call start.

* Clients receive a clear pre-call briefing about what they must not ask, including examples of forbidden questions about strategic plans or unreleased financials.

* Click-through attestations are logged by the network platform, forming part of the audit trail.

* This protects both sides: the expert, by clarifying limits under applicable laws, and the client, by showing good-faith compliance intent and informed consent.

### Conduct During the Call

Expert networks should avoid discussions that involve unpublished financial results or other information barriers that protect nonpublic information.

* Experts should refuse to answer if a question touches on MNPI or other sensitive information. This is a core part of the expert network's policies.

* Clients should be trained to pause and escalate to their legal team or compliance teams if a response might have crossed into non public information.

* Acceptable topics include market structure, historical roles, customer behavior, and public information synthesis.

* Some networks spot-monitor calls or use random sampling review to reinforce strong compliance standards.

### Post-Call Documentation and Audit Trails

Documentation of expert interactions is essential for compliance. Every interaction must be logged.

* Records include date, time, participants, company topics, expert background check results, and fees.

* Expert networks log call metadata and maintain audit trails as standard practice.

* Transcripts or detailed notes should be retained where allowed, plus stored copies of expert terms and call attestations.

* Typical retention expectations range from 3 to 7 years. Check current regulatory guidance for exact requirements in your jurisdiction.

* Regular audits and exception reports enhance compliance credibility and support follow up during LP DDQs or regulatory exams.

* FieldSignal maintains audit-ready records so clients can respond to internal audits and external reviews.

## How to Vet an Expert Network Vendor for Compliance

You're an associate, analyst, or founder. You need primary research fast. You also can't afford to create legal or reputational problems for your firm. Here's how to pick an expert network vendor that won't put you at risk. These questions apply equally to GLG, AlphaSights, Third Bridge, Guidepoint, Tegus, ProSapient, Coleman Research, Atheneum, Mosaic Research Management, Inex One, and FieldSignal.

### Essential Compliance Questions to Ask Any Expert Network

* How do you screen experts for MNPI risk, including current employer status, non disclosure agreements, and clinical trial confidentiality?

* What compliance training do experts undergo, and how often? Is compliance training mandatory for all employees understand in expert networks?

* What are your pre-call attestations? Are they click-through and logged?

* What records can you provide if the SEC or exchange commission asks about a particular case or call?

* How do you handle experts currently employed by public companies?

* What project approval or scoping controls do you use? Can clients supply restricted lists or topic bans?

* What dashboards or tools do clients have to monitor project activity and expert profile data?

* Have you ever declined or terminated certain experts for compliance reasons? How are those decisions documented?

* Can you share sample T&Cs, sample compliance attestations, or redacted compliance reports?

* How customizable are your compliance rules for different business units or client types?

Push for clear, written answers. Verbal reassurances aren't enough.

## Designing Your Internal Controls for Use of Expert Networks

Even with a strong expert network vendor, your firm still needs its own internal policies for ethical use of expert networks. The vendor handles their side. You handle yours.

### Pre-Approval and Scoping Process

Firms should require written pre-approval from compliance or your legal team before any expert call is booked, especially when public companies are involved.

* Use a simple checklist: purpose, target company, topics, any known MNPI sensitivities, and the client name or deal associated with the request.

* Maintain your own internal lists of prohibited companies, industries, or expert types.

* Assign clear ownership and deadlines so the process doesn't bottleneck deal timelines.

### Employee Training and Conduct Expectations

New hires who might use expert network services should receive specific training on MNPI, question framing, and what to do if a call feels "over the line." Compliance training is mandatory for all employees in expert networks and for firms that use them.

* Run periodic refresher training, at least annually, with scenario-based examples tailored to your work. Pre-IC research calls and commercial due diligence are good contexts.

* Make clear that employees understand they can pause or end a call and escalate to compliance without penalty.

* Cover the Sarbanes Oxley Act and its interest in corporate governance controls where relevant to your firm's policies and compliance programs.

### Documentation, Monitoring, and Escalation

Employees should log each call in a centralized system: date, expert, company discussed, key topics, and any potential issues flagged.

* Compliance should periodically sample call notes, invoices, and email threads related to expert network use for consistency checks.

* Suspected MNPI incidents need a defined escalation path, including temporary trading freezes if necessary and documented outcomes.

* Documentation is what allows firms to show regulators and LPs they took network compliance seriously. It's also what proves your competitive edge in governance over firms that skip this step.
