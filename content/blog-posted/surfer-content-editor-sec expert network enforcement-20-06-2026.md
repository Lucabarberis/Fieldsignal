## Timeline of SEC enforcement actions involving expert networks

SEC scrutiny of expert networks is cyclical but persistent. It started with a cluster of high-profile investigations around 2009, produced convictions and civil penalties through the mid-2010s, and then shifted toward examinations of controls and compliance programs at investment advisers and private funds. The SEC's enforcement program targets financial professionals and corporate insiders, and expert networks sit at the intersection of both.

Here's the key chronology:

**2009.** The Galleon Group insider trading investigation begins. While not exclusively about expert networks, it exposed how hedge fund managers obtained "edge" through consultants and tippers. It reshaped the SEC's posture toward research channels that could carry confidential information.

**2010.** The [SEC charged](https://www.sec.gov/spotlight/insidertrading/cases.shtml) Dr. Yves Benhamou, a French doctor, with allegedly tipping negative clinical trial data to a hedge fund manager who used it to avoid more than $30 million in losses. Insider trading can involve illegal tipping of information, and this case showed that expert network consultants in specialized fields like pharmaceuticals were squarely in scope.

**February 2011.** The SEC filed charges against four hedge fund portfolio managers and analysts in a [$30 million insider trading scheme](https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2011/2011-40.htm). The SEC alleged they traded on material nonpublic information obtained from consultants working for the expert network Primary Global Research. Issuers involved included AMD, Seagate Technology, Western Digital, Fairchild Semiconductor, and Marvell. The SEC charged both tippers and tippees under Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5.

**Early 2011.** The SEC filed charges against several Primary Global Research employees, accusing them of tipping hedge funds in return for sham consulting fees. These cases framed expert networks themselves as potential conduits for MNPI leaks.

**2012 to 2014.** Continued civil and criminal enforcement. High-profile parallel cases like SAC Capital, Level Global Investors LP, Barai Capital Management, and the Raj Rajaratnam / Rajat Gupta prosecutions shaped the SEC's broader posture toward hedge fund research practices. While not solely expert network cases, they reinforced the message that "edge" sourced from insiders at publicly traded companies is a federal securities laws violation.

**Mid to late 2010s.** Enforcement emphasis shifted from prosecuting blatant MNPI leaks to examining the controls at investment advisers and fund managers. The SEC's OCIE (now EXAMS) began listing alternative data and expert network oversight in its annual examination priorities.

**2020.** The SEC's OCIE issued a risk alert on private fund adviser examinations, identifying failures in policies and procedures relating to MNPI misuse, including from expert networks and value add investors.

**2022.** The [SEC EXAMS risk alert](https://www.sec.gov/files/code-ethics-risk-alert.pdf) on Code of Ethics compliance explicitly called out deficiencies in tracking expert network calls, inadequate policies around expert conversations, and failures to monitor or enforce codes of ethics.

The pattern is clear. Early 2010s enforcement targeted blatant MNPI leaks through expert networks. By the late 2010s, the SEC was examining whether investment advisers had adequate policies, procedures relating to expert network use, and trading surveillance, even without proving actual insider trading occurred.

## Legal framework: how the SEC builds insider trading cases

SEC expert network enforcement isn't a novel legal theory. It's standard insider trading law applied to a specific research channel. Understanding the relevant law helps you design clean written policies and spot problems before they become enforcement actions.

### What counts as material nonpublic information

The SEC defined materiality as significant information for investors, specifically information that a reasonable investor would consider important in making investment decisions. Common examples include pre-release earnings data, undisclosed M&A talks, clinical trial results, major customer losses, or changes to a company's securities offering terms.

Information is nonpublic until it's broadly disseminated through press releases, public filings, or equivalent channels. A tip shared on an expert call about an upcoming tender offer or a board member's decision doesn't become public just because five people heard it.

### Three theories of insider trading liability

The SEC builds cases under three main theories:

1. **Classical theory.** Corporate insiders (officers, directors, employees) trade their own issuer's securities while possessing MNPI, breaching their fiduciary duty to shareholders.

2. **Tipper-tippee theory.** An insider leaks material non public information to an outsider who trades on such information. The tippee must know or should know about the breach of duty. Most expert network cases fall here, with the consultant as tipper and the hedge fund analyst or portfolio managers as tippees.

3. **Misappropriation theory.** The misappropriation theory applies to non-insiders trading on confidential information obtained in breach of a duty to the information source. A technology company employee who moonlights through an expert network and discloses product roadmap data breaches their duty to their employer, not to the company's shareholders. This theory captures many expert network scenarios.

### Statutory and rule hooks

Insider trading violates Section 10 of the Securities Exchange Act and Rule 10b-5, which prohibits fraud in connection with securities trading. The SEC also cites Section 17(a) of the Securities Act for misrepresentations. For criminal, willful violations, Section 32 of the Securities Exchange Act and the Sarbanes Oxley Act provide additional enforcement tools and the rules thereunder.

Rule 10b5-2 defines "duty of trust or confidence." Employment agreements, NDAs, and consulting contracts all create duties. When an expert breaches those duties during an expert network call, both the expert and the recipient can face insider trading liability.

Individuals who disclose material nonpublic information can face criminal prosecution and civil penalties. The DOJ often works in parallel with the SEC, layering on conspiracy and wire fraud charges. Courts can impose disgorgement of ill gotten gains, injunctions against future violations, and industry bars.

The SEC investigates anomalous trading patterns near significant corporate announcements to detect insider trading, meaning your trading activity around expert calls is visible to regulators.

### Mosaic theory and its limits

Hedge fund managers and research teams often rely on mosaic theory, combining public information with small, immaterial nonpublic fragments to form an investment thesis. This is legal. But the line is firm: if any single piece is material nonpublic information obtained in breach of a duty, trading on the same information crosses into insider trading violations. Mosaic theory protects diligent research. It doesn't protect sloppy sourcing.

This is why serious fund managers and institutional investors treat expert networks as a compliance topic, not a casual research tool.

## What SEC exams and Risk Alerts say about expert networks and alternative data

Recent SEC exams of private fund advisers, hedge funds, and PE firms have repeatedly flagged controls around expert networks and alternative data. The SEC's division of examinations treats these as high-priority areas. Advanced market surveillance and data analytics are employed by the SEC to monitor expert networks, making weak controls more detectable than ever.

The core expectations from [recent Risk Alerts and exam findings](https://sec.gov/files/Private%20Fund%20Risk%20Alert_0.pdf) include:

* Investment advisers must implement written policies to prevent the misuse of material nonpublic information. Over 36 percent of registered investment advisers manage private funds, and EXAMS staff have observed MNPI policy deficiencies among many of them.

* Advisers must enforce those policies, not just write them. The SEC penalizes firms for compliance failures even without proving actual insider trading.

* Advisers must document how they test and implement adequate policies.

### Typical SEC exam questions on expert networks

When examiners show up, expect these questions:

1. Which expert networks are you using?

2. How are experts vetted for conflicts, current employment, and restrictive covenants?

3. Are expert network calls recorded or transcribed? Are topics pre-cleared?

4. Is there trading surveillance around consultant interactions?

5. Who reviews detailed notes or call memos after engagements?

### Alternative data scrutiny

For alternative data, the SEC asks about internet search data, web-scraped e-commerce data, credit card transaction feeds, and geolocation datasets. The concern is whether data is "too close" to customer-level or otherwise nonpublic information. Firms must explain the data's original source, any contractual or privacy restrictions, and whether aggregation is sufficient to avoid potential receipt of MNPI.

### Expanded scope beyond hedge funds

Private equity and venture capital firms are now in scope, not just public equity hedge funds. The SEC focuses on potential mnpi obtained from board seats, observation rights, and customer/supplier interviews. A PE associate conducting due diligence by calling a target company's former employees is subject to the same MNPI rules as a Wall Street analyst calling through GLG.

## Common failure modes in expert network use that trigger enforcement risk

The SEC's expert network cases usually share a few patterns. You can design against each of them.

**Moonlighting insiders.** Current employees of public companies earn consulting fees through an expert network while disclosing confidential information about earnings, product decisions, or customer contracts. In the Primary Global Research cases, consultants were current employees of technology firms who passed nonpublic information to hedge funds. Expert networks may face liability if they facilitate the passing of inside information this way.

**Weak pre-call scoping.** Hedge fund managers or analysts let conversations drift into earnings guidance, nonpublic customer loss data, or pending transactions, with no real-time compliance oversight or logging calls. Without documented guardrails, there's no way to rebut SEC allegations later.

**Inadequate expert vetting.** Some networks accept any self-identified "expert" without independent checks for current employment, restrictive covenants, or access to MNPI. Exam findings note this as a recurring deficiency.

**Sloppy communication channels.** Follow-up by text, encrypted apps, or personal email where compliance can't review context. This creates a potential risk that the SEC will infer concealment.

**Orphaned compliance policies.** Investment adviser manuals that mention expert networks and MNPI but aren't enforced in practice. The SEC views this as evidence of conscious disregard of the firm's own controls.

**Reputational fallout.** Firms risk severe reputational harm and loss of investor trust due to insider trading scandals. Other investors and LPs watch enforcement actions closely. Even an investigation without charges can damage fundraising.

## Building a defensible expert network program (what the SEC wants to see)

Your goal isn't zero risk. It's a program that looks credible and disciplined when an SEC examiner, outside counsel, or a law firm's clients review it. Firms must implement policies addressing insider trading risks and expert interactions. Here's a seven-step framework.

### 1. Written policy

Your policy must cover expert networks, one-off expert introductions, and alternative data vendors. Define material nonpublic information and nonpublic information clearly. List prohibited topics (undisclosed earnings, pending M&A, product launch dates). Include decision trees for edge cases, such as when an expert is a current employee of a relevant public company. Firms must implement written policies to provide guidance across the organization.

### 2. Expert network due diligence

Vet your expert network providers. Review their template contracts, consultant onboarding questionnaires, and compliance training materials. Ask whether the network records calls or provides transcripts. Check if they screen for restrictive covenants and current employment at public companies.

### 3. Pre-approval workflow

Before each call, require an intake form with: expert identity, issuer names, sector, call objective, whether the expert is a current or recent employee of a relevant issuer, and compliance sign-off. Expert network compliance programs should document approval processes for every engagement.

### 4. Real-time controls during calls

Use approved conferencing tools. Have a trained moderator on calls. Pre-clear the call agenda. If the expert says something like "I can't share this publicly, but..." or starts discussing unannounced financial metrics, stop the call. Firms should establish information barriers between public and private sides of the organization to prevent cross-contamination.

### 5. Post-call documentation

Firms should document all discussions with expert network consultants. Take short call memos reviewing detailed notes on time, topics, attendees, and key statements. Require supervised persons to acknowledge that no MNPI was shared. Firms must track and log all consultations to comply with SEC regulations.

### 6. Trading surveillance

Maintain a restricted list of public issuers under coverage. Monitor trades around expert interactions by access persons, portfolio managers, and analysts. Pre-clear personal trades when necessary. Link expert calls to subsequent trading activity to catch suspicious patterns.

### 7. Training and attestations

Employees must receive training on insider trading laws regularly, at minimum annually. Use scenario-based training drawn from past enforcement actions. Cover such matters as tipper-tippee liability, misappropriation, and the limits of mosaic theory. Require attestations from supervised persons about adherence to MNPI policies. Training should reference real cases like Primary Global Research, the York regional office investigations, and relevant SEC filed charges to make the lessons concrete.

Following this structure meaningfully reduces enforcement risk. It also gives you a defensible record if the SEC ever sends a detailed analysis request or exam letter.

1. Confirm whether the expert is a current employee of any relevant public company.

2. Confirm the expert's restrictive covenants or NDA status, past or present.

3. Document purpose and scope before each call: issuer(s), topics, intended output.

4. Don't ask for unpublished financial metrics, earnings guidance, product launch dates, customer contracts, or undisclosed mergers.

6. Get compliance sign-off before engaging any new expert network or alternative data source, even for a single call.

8. Ask any alternative data provider to explain the data's original source and whether any contractual or privacy restrictions apply, including internet search data or credit card feeds.

9. Keep all expert interactions on recorded or logged channels. Don't follow up via personal WhatsApp, Signal, or private email.

10. Conduct training and attestations with your team: scenario-based insider trading and expert network examples, enforced in practice, not just on paper.
