# Sample Size for Expert Interviews: How Many Is Enough

You don't need 50 expert interviews to make a good decision. For most B2B research projects, the right number falls between 8 and 30, depending on how narrow your question is and how many segments you're covering. This article gives you concrete ranges, the research behind them, and a practical process for determining sample size without overspending.

## Answer first: how many expert interviews do you actually need?

The recommended sample size for expert interviews typically ranges from 5 to 30 participants. For a narrow competitive intelligence question, like understanding one competitor's pricing or go-to-market motion, 8 to 12 interviews with well-recruited experts will usually get you there. For broader market theses spanning multiple geographies or customer types, plan for 15 to 25. If you're running focus groups instead, 4 to 6 groups of 5 to 10 participants each covers most bounded topics.

In depth interviews typically require 5 to 30 participants, with 12 often sufficient for a single-segment question. That's not a guess. It's backed by decades of published qualitative research showing that interview saturation usually occurs within 12 to 15 interviews when your sample is focused.

Determining sample size in qualitative research isn't about hitting a magic number. It's about information sufficiency. You stop when additional interviews confirm what you already know instead of revealing something new. Saturation is a key factor influencing sample size decisions, and it depends on your scope, expert quality, and the complexity of what you're studying.

For most PE/VC, M&A, and product strategy projects, thematic saturation is reached well before 30 experts, provided your recruitment is precise. You don't need former employees from every department at Competitor X. You need the five to seven who sat close enough to the decisions you're researching.

Expert interviews differ from consumer UX sessions or general population surveys. Each expert, whether a former VP of Sales, a churned enterprise customer, or a supply chain manager, carries higher "information power." They've lived the topic you're studying. That means smaller sample sizes are not just defensible but often preferable, because you avoid redundancy and keep costs contained.

Homogeneous samples often reach saturation with fewer interviews. If you're talking to six former account executives from the same company about the same sales process, you'll start hearing repeated patterns by interview four or five. The math works differently when those experts come from different roles, regions, or competitor sets. That's when you need more interviews.

## Why sample size in qualitative research works differently for expert interviews

Sample sizes in qualitative research follow a fundamentally different logic than quantitative research. You're not chasing statistical significance or confidence intervals. You're chasing saturation and decision confidence. Qualitative research focuses on depth over breadth, which means the bar for "enough" depends on how much signal each interview delivers, not how many data points you collect.

Here's why expert interviews produce that signal faster:

**Experts carry concentrated knowledge.** When you interview a former regional director at a competitor, a procurement lead at a target customer, or a channel partner who's worked both sides of a market, you're extracting specific process details, pricing logic, and strategic rationale that simply aren't available in surveys or public data. One well-placed expert can surface what 50 consumer respondents can't. The richness of data collected from these experts directly influences the required sample size, pushing it lower.

**Information power reduces required sample size.** The concept of [information power](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1049732315617444), introduced by Malterud et al. in 2016, formalized what practitioners already knew: if your experts are highly experienced and provide rich insights, you need fewer participants. Five dimensions drive this, including study aim specificity, sample relevance, use of theory, dialogue quality, and analysis strategy. Score high on all five, and you can defend a sample of 8 to 12 with confidence. Quantitative research typically requires larger sample sizes for reliability because individual responses carry less unique information.

**Practical constraints force deliberate scoping.** Budget, deal timelines, and the limited availability of niche experts mean corporate and investment teams can't casually plan for 40 interviews. A PE associate working a deal has two to three weeks before the investment committee meets. A product manager planning a market entry has a quarter, not a year. These constraints aren't weaknesses. They're design parameters that should be stated explicitly in your research plan. Practical considerations such as time and budget can dictate sample size, and pretending otherwise doesn't help.

**Academic norms don't fit applied work.** A recent review of 562 qualitative interview studies across top journals found a median of 46 interviews per study. That's fine for multi-site ethnographies and theory-building dissertations. It's not realistic for a two-week commercial due diligence sprint. FieldSignal clients typically need to make calls in two to three weeks, which means scoping for 10 to 20 high-quality interviews, not 46. Web-based interviews may require different sample sizes than in-person interviews, but in applied B2B work, most expert calls happen over video or phone, and the information power per call remains high.

## Key concepts: data saturation, thematic saturation, and theoretical saturation

Saturation is the main principle for determining when you have enough qualitative data. It's not a single concept but a family of related ideas, each relevant at different stages of the research process. Understanding the differences helps you set realistic expectations, build credible stopping rules, and avoid both underspending and overspending on data collection.

Saturation is a key criterion for determining sample size adequacy. But saturation can differ based on the study's population and topic complexity, so the specific type of saturation you target matters.

### Data saturation and code saturation

Data saturation is the point at which additional interviews stop producing new codes, categories, or discrete observations. In practice, this is the first milestone you hit. Code saturation is often reached after 9 interviews in a focused, homogeneous sample. Guest, Bunce, and Johnson's widely cited 2006 study found that [by interview 12, approximately 92% of all codes had emerged](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/1525822x05279903) from the dataset. Data saturation is often reached after 9 interviews when experts share similar professional backgrounds or organizational contexts.

Data saturation is reached when no new themes emerge from interviews. If you're running a competitive analysis on one vendor's enterprise sales process and you've talked to eight former account executives, you'll likely see the same pricing tiers, discount structures, and objection-handling patterns repeating by interview six or seven. Saturation occurs when no new themes emerge from data, and at that point, you can confidently shift focus to the next segment.

### Thematic saturation and meaning saturation

Thematic saturation goes further. It's not just about discrete codes but about whether the themes, patterns, and mechanisms relevant to your research question have stabilized. Can subsequent interviews still surprise you with a new competitive dynamic or customer objection you haven't heard? If not, you've hit thematic saturation.

Meaning saturation typically requires 16 to 24 interviews, particularly when you need to understand not just what happens but why it happens and what it means for your thesis. A [systematic review of saturation in qualitative research](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0277953621008558) confirmed that many studies reach saturation with surprisingly small sample sizes when the group is uniform, but thematic saturation typically requires 16 to 24 interviews for rich understanding in more diverse samples. Saturation in qualitative research often occurs around 12 to 15 interviews for narrower questions.

### Theoretical saturation

Theoretical saturation is the most demanding standard. It's the point where further data collection no longer improves or refines your explanatory model, deal thesis, or strategic framework. If you're building a multi-region market entry thesis or evaluating a fragmented competitive set, theoretical saturation requires 16 to 24 interviews for depth, and often more when scope expands across customer types and geographies.

Theoretical saturation is most relevant in theory based interview studies, grounded theory work, and complex commercial due diligence. For a single-competitor teardown, you probably don't need it. For a nine-figure acquisition spanning three continents, you do.

### Where focus groups fit

Focus groups usually need 4 to 6 groups with 5 to 10 participants each. They're efficient for capturing group norms, customer language, and comparative reactions. They work well for customer satisfaction themes or product concept testing. They don't work well for extracting proprietary competitive intelligence or insider process knowledge, where one-on-one expert interviews are superior.

| Saturation Type        | Definition                            | Typical Range        | Best For                                               |
|------------------------|---------------------------------------|----------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| Code saturation        | No new discrete codes or observations | 9 to 12 interviews   | Narrow CI questions, single-competitor analysis        |
| Thematic saturation    | No new themes or patterns             | 16 to 24 interviews  | Market entry, product strategy, multi-segment research |
| Theoretical saturation | No refinement to explanatory model    | 20 to 30+ interviews | M&A due diligence, multi-region theses                 |
| Focus group saturation | No new group-level dynamics           | 4 to 6 groups        | Customer satisfaction, concept testing                 |
Operationalising data saturation means defining it before you start, tracking it during the project, and documenting it when you report findings. It's not something you declare after the fact. It's something you monitor interview by interview.

## Factors that drive expert interview sample size in practice

Determining sample size is an analytic and practical judgment. It should be documented before you conduct research, not rationalized afterward. Sample size in qualitative research varies based on study design, and several concrete factors push the number up or down.

### Research goal

The nature of your question changes everything. Exploratory landscaping, where you don't know what you don't know, demands broader coverage and more interviews to capture a range of expert views. Broader exploratory studies require more interviews than confirmatory work. A deal thesis test ("Is this company's NRR really 130%?") is more focused. A detailed product teardown sits somewhere in between. Match your sample size to the ambiguity of the question.

### Population heterogeneity

Participant diversity can impact the required sample size for expert interviews. If you need perspectives from US enterprise buyers, DACH mid-market accounts, and APAC distributors, you need interviews in each cell. Five to seven per segment is a reasonable minimum. One geography with one buyer type might need 8 to 12 total. Three geographies with three buyer types might need 25 to 30. Studies with broad scopes require larger sample sizes for saturation.

A sample size under 5 may lack validity if it does not capture diverse perspectives within any single segment. Two interviews with former employees of Competitor A isn't a pattern. It's two anecdotes.

### Method mix

The research methods you combine affect how many interviews you need. In depth interviews provide the richest qualitative data per unit of effort. Focus groups capture group dynamics efficiently. Short expert surveys can validate patterns at scale. If you're pairing 12 interviews with a 50-person survey, those 12 interviews can focus on depth while the survey handles breadth.

Qualitative methods and quantitative data serve different purposes. Use them together, and you can defend fewer interviews because the survey provides triangulation.

### Topic complexity and risk

Complex and specialized studies may require 15 to 30+ participants to map the full picture. Biotech supply chains, regulated fintech, or enterprise infrastructure with multiple integration layers all demand more data to reach saturation. The stakes also matter. If you're writing a $200M check, you can justify a larger sample. If you're sizing a seed-stage TAM, you can't, and you shouldn't need to.

Focus on the research scope to determine appropriate sample size for expert interviews. A smaller sample allows for deeper exploration of complex issues within a single domain. Don't spread too thin across too many questions.

### Resource and timing constraints

Investment committee dates, product sprint cadences, and fundraise timelines create hard deadlines. You have to decide: do you want 25 interviews spread over eight weeks, or 12 interviews in two weeks? Often the 12 are more valuable because they arrive while the decision is still live.

### Expert quality and information power

Information power requires fewer participants if interviewees are highly experienced and provide rich insights. A former CRO at the acquisition target carries more weight than a junior AE who left after six months. When recruitment is precise and expert quality is high, you can defend a smaller sample and still extract meaningful insights. Most qualitative researchers working in applied settings already operate this way, even if they don't always name the framework.

## Practical ranges: how many expert interviews for common B2B research scenarios?

Here are concrete ranges for the most common projects we see. These aren't rules. They're evidence-informed starting points you can adjust based on the factors above.

For each scenario, the number of interviews per "cell" (segment, geography, expert type) matters more than the grand total. Five interviews across five segments is weak everywhere. Fifteen interviews across three segments, with five each, gives you enough to see patterns within each.

**Market entry assessment.** If you're evaluating entry into one or two new markets, plan for 10 to 20 expert interviews per country. Split them across two to three customer types, and include one to two supplier or channel partner conversations. Qualitative studies may require 20 to 40 interviews for cross cultural research where you need to capture themes across distinct markets with different buyer behaviors. Cross cultural research pushes sample sizes higher because you can't assume patterns transfer across regions.

**Competitive analysis and vendor benchmarking.** To understand one competitor's product, pricing, and go-to-market approach, 8 to 15 interviews with former employees, partners, and power users will typically achieve saturation. You'll reach thematic saturation on product strengths and pricing patterns around interview 10 to 12 if recruitment quality is high. This is one of the most common scopes for qualitative interviews in B2B intelligence.

**Customer satisfaction and churn drivers.** A mix of 12 to 20 in depth interviews with current and lost customers, or 3 to 4 focus groups, captures the main themes and most edge cases. Budget for two to three additional interviews beyond your expected saturation point, because churn drivers often have a long tail of less common but important reasons. Five to eight participants are sufficient for usability testing rounds, but satisfaction and churn analysis demands more because the reasons are more varied and emotionally loaded.

**Commercial due diligence.** This is the most ambitious scope. You're covering customers, competitors, ex-staff, and value chain participants. For single-geography, single-vertical deals, 15 to 20 interviews is standard. For multi-region theses or fragmented markets, expect 25 to 30. A [B2B International case study](https://www.b2binternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Competitive-Intelligence.pdf) documented a competitive intelligence project spanning multiple sectors and geographies that required 95 interviews to cover all segments. That's an outlier driven by scope, not a benchmark for every project.

Maximum sample sizes can yield diminishing returns after reaching data saturation. If you've heard the same answer to the same question from the last five experts, booking five more is spending money without gaining new insights.

## A step-by-step approach to determining sample size for your expert study

Here's how to turn the concepts above into an actual research plan. This process works whether you're running your first qualitative inquiry or your fiftieth.

**1. Define your primary decision question in one sentence.**

Write it down. "Should we acquire Company X before Q4?" "Can we price our product 20% above Competitor Y in North America?" "What are the top three reasons mid-market customers churn from this platform?" Then classify it as exploratory (you're mapping unknown territory) or confirmatory (you have a thesis to validate). Exploratory questions need more interviews. Confirmatory questions need fewer but more targeted ones.

**2. Map required perspectives with specific expert types.**

Don't write "we'll interview 15 experts." Write "5 to 7 former sales leaders from Competitor A, 4 to 6 enterprise customers in North America, 3 to 5 key distributors in EMEA." Each group is a segment. Each segment needs its own mini-sample. The target population for each segment should be defined before recruitment starts, not improvised during fieldwork.

This is where how many participants you need per segment becomes the real question. Plan for 4 to 7 per segment as your initial analysis sample, with room to add if saturation hasn't been reached.

**3. Choose your methods.**

In depth interviews give you the richest data per conversation. They're ideal for sensitive competitive intelligence, pricing detail, and internal process knowledge. Focus groups work for customer perception research where group interaction surfaces additional data you wouldn't get one-on-one. Short targeted surveys supplement interviews with quantitative data across a broader set. Decide which combination gives you the best shot at both depth and coverage for your question.

Each method contributes differently to saturation and systematic analysis. Interviews surface mechanisms. Surveys quantify prevalence. Focus groups reveal social dynamics.

**4. Set an initial sample size plan with stopping rules.**

Use the evidence-based ranges from this article as starting points. For each segment, plan 8 to 10 interviews with a stopping rule: "Once three consecutive interviews per segment produce no new themes or codes, we stop recruiting for that segment." Document this in your research plan before a single call is scheduled.

This approach to sample size determination is explicit, auditable, and defensible. It beats the alternative, which is picking a round number and hoping it's enough.

**5. Conduct research in waves.**

Run your first three to five interviews. Pause. Do an initial round of qualitative data analysis. What themes emerged? What's still unclear? What segments need more coverage? Then run the next wave. This iterative approach to data collection lets you adjust recruitment in real time.

After each wave, ask: "Did the last batch of interviews reveal new data, or did it confirm existing patterns?" If you're still seeing new insights, keep going. If the themes are stable, shift to a different segment or stop entirely. Most qualitative researchers who work under time pressure already operate in waves, even if they don't formalize it.

**6. Decide on your stopping rule and document how you'll evidence it.**

Pick one: thematic saturation (no new themes), theoretical saturation (no refinement to your model), or a fixed cap tied to your decision timeline. State your choice in advance.

When you report findings, include language like: "We reached code saturation on competitive pricing structure by interview 9. Interviews 10 through 13 confirmed earlier patterns. No new themes emerged in subsequent interviews." This makes your qualitative analysis credible to IC partners, board members, and clients who don't have qualitative research backgrounds.

## Documenting and defending your sample size in qualitative studies

Your sample size means nothing if you can't explain why it's appropriate. Investment committees, deal memos, academic review boards, and client presentations all require justification. The good news: a clear, honest explanation of your approach is more convincing than a large number with no rationale behind it.

Validity in qualitative research is linked to the depth and richness of data, not the size of the sample alone. A well-executed study with 12 interviews and documented saturation is more credible than a sloppy study with 40.

### Three justification strategies

**Saturation-based justification.** State which type of saturation you targeted (data, thematic, or theoretical), how you monitored it, and when you reached it. Report thematic saturation explicitly, e.g., "By interview 14, no new competitive positioning themes had emerged. Interviews 15 through 17 served as confirmation." This is the strongest approach when your audience values rigor.

**Design-based justification.** Show your sampling frame. Explain which expert types, geographies, or roles you intentionally varied (maximum variation sampling, typical case sampling, or critical case sampling in expert work). Show that your design required a minimum number per cell and that you met it. Justifying sample size sufficiency through design means connecting sample sizes to your sampling strategy, not picking a number out of the air.

**Pragmatic justification.** Be honest about time, cost, and access constraints. "We had 18 business days before IC. We completed 14 interviews across three expert segments. Thematic saturation was reached in two of three segments; the third segment had adequate coverage for our decision but not full saturation." Transparency about trade-offs is more convincing than pretending constraints don't exist.

### Why this matters more than you think

A [review of 562 published qualitative studies](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10944281261424516) found that approximately 84% of papers did not specify how they determined sample size. That's in academic journals where methodology is theoretically the whole point. In applied work, the bar is even lower. Which means that if you provide clear, auditable reasoning for your sample size, you immediately stand out.

Include specific language in your qualitative report and IC memos. Sample size sufficiency should be demonstrated, not assumed. Phrases like "We used conceptual depth criteria to assess saturation" or "Theoretical sampling guided our recruitment across waves" show methodological literacy.

Existing literature in qualitative health research, phenomenological research, interpretative phenomenological analysis, and field methods all provide frameworks for this justification. You don't need to reinvent the wheel. You need to adapt these frameworks to your commercial context.

Secondary analysis of previously collected data can also supplement your primary interviews, extending your coverage without additional recruitment cost. If you have access to prior transcripts or research from related projects, use them.
