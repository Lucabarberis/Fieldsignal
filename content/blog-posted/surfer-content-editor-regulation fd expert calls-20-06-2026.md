# Regulation FD and Expert Calls: What's Permitted

Expert calls are legal under Regulation FD. The rule doesn't ban one-on-one conversations with industry specialists. It bans selective disclosure of material nonpublic information by issuers to favored investors. If your expert calls don't involve MNPI, you're operating within the law.

## Quick Answer: Are Expert Calls Allowed Under Regulation FD?

Yes. Expert network calls connect investors with industry specialists to discuss market trends within regulatory boundaries. The two risks you need to manage are Reg FD selective disclosure (if the expert is an issuer representative) and insider trading liability (if anyone on the call shares or trades on MNPI).

Regulation fair disclosure applies to issuers and their communications, not to PE/VC firms or expert networks directly. But buy-side firms can still get pulled into enforcement action if they trade on improperly disclosed material nonpublic information. The securities and exchange commission doesn't care whether the tip came through a formal analyst meeting or a casual expert call. What matters is whether MNPI was shared and whether someone traded on it.

Properly structured expert calls, with clear ground rules, compliance-trained experts, and real-time monitoring, fit within SEC expectations and historic enforcement patterns since October 2000. ## Regulation Fair Disclosure (Reg FD): Core Rules You Must Know

Reg FD was implemented on [October 23, 2000](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/2000/08/selective-disclosure-insider-trading). The SEC adopted it to stop publicly traded companies from giving material nonpublic information selectively to securities analysts, selected institutional investors, and other market professionals before making the same information available to the general public.

Regulation FD aims to ensure all investors have equal access to material information. The rule aims to prevent selective disclosure to favored investors. Here's how it works in practice.

**Timing rules:**

* If an issuer intentionally discloses material information to covered recipients, it must publicly disclose the same information simultaneously

* If the disclosure is unintentional, the issuer must correct it promptly, typically within 24 hours or before the next trading session

**Who counts as a "covered recipient":**

* Securities market professionals: broker-dealers, investment advisers, institutional investment managers

* Securities analysts and stock analysts

* Rating agencies

* Shareholders reasonably expected to trade on the information

**What counts as "material":**

A reasonable investor would consider the information important when making investment decisions. Think earnings forecasts, revenue guidance, significant departures in executive leadership, or major contract wins and losses. If it would change analysts estimates or move the company's securities price, it's material.

**What counts as "nonpublic":**

Information that hasn't been widely disseminated. A comment in a private meeting doesn't become public just because someone took notes. Public disclosure methods include Form 8-K filings, broadly disseminated press releases, and open-access conference calls or webcasts with prior notice and public replays. Companies must make recordings of analyst calls publicly available to satisfy Reg FD requirements.

Reg FD requires simultaneous public disclosure of material information. Section 10 of the Securities Exchange Act prohibits manipulative trading practices, and Section 32 of the exchange act criminalizes willful violations of insider trading laws. These rules work together to keep financial markets fair.

## How Reg FD Changed Analyst Behavior and Public Disclosures

Before Reg FD, analysts routinely got incremental guidance in private meetings and calls with management. This created information asymmetry: investment professionals with issuer access had advance warnings about earnings results before individual investors saw anything. The playing field was tilted.

Since Regulation FD, analysts cannot rely on insider tips from company management during private calls. The shift was measurable.

* Analysts' forecast revisions increased significantly after Regulation FD, because analysts had to update models faster based on public events rather than waiting for back-channel tips

* Regulation FD enhanced analysts' reliance on public disclosures as the primary input for their models

* Analysts responded more quickly to earnings announcements post-Reg FD, with shorter delays between an earnings announcement and the next round of forecast updates

* Forecast dispersion among analysts decreased after Regulation FD, meaning analysts converged faster on similar estimates when all were working from the same information

## Where Expert Calls Sit Relative to Reg FD and Insider Trading Law

Expert networks and one-on-one expert calls aren't banned by Reg FD. But they must operate within both Reg FD (for issuer employees) and general insider trading restrictions (for everyone).

* If an expert is a current issuer insider, or regularly speaks for the company, they're constrained by Reg FD. They can't share internal sales data, unreleased earnings forecasts, or other material information without the issuer making a simultaneous public disclosure.

* Former employees, customers, suppliers, and channel partners typically aren't "issuers" for Reg FD purposes. But they're still subject to insider trading laws if they share confidential information obtained through a duty of trust or confidence. Experts employed by public companies must not disclose material nonpublic information to investors. Experts are legally barred from sharing material nonpublic information about public companies they worked for or consult with.

* Insider trading laws prohibit trading on material nonpublic information. There are three main theories of liability:

1. **Classical theory:** corporate insiders trade on or tip MNPI about their own company

2. **Tipper-tippee doctrine:** an insider shares non public information with someone who then trades on it

3. **Misappropriation theory:** someone with access to confidential information (a consultant, vendor, or adviser) misuses it for trading advantage

The mosaic theory is your friend here. Building an investment thesis by combining non-material public information and immaterial nonpublic information is allowed, as long as no single piece is material and nonpublic at the time of trading. Analysts use expert calls to gather high-level industry data rather than direct corporate information, and that's exactly where the mosaic theory applies.

Corporate insider bans prevent institutional investors from using expert networks to obtain nonpublic details about a specific company's internal operations.

### Regulation FD vs Insider Trading: Different Rules, Same Call

These are two separate regimes that can both get triggered on the same call.

* Reg FD is an issuer disclosure rule. It governs when and how a company must publicly disclose material information. Example: an investor relations officer selectively giving unreleased quarterly revenue ranges to favorite analysts violates Reg FD. The company discloses material information to a narrow group without making it public.

* Insider trading rules apply to trading and tipping. That same analyst who received the tip and then trades on it faces insider trading liability under the securities exchange act.

* A former sales director speaking on a FieldSignal expert call can share qualitative views on demand trends and competitor behavior. That's fine. But they can't disclose hard internal numbers from their former employer that haven't been publicly disclosed. During expert calls, discussions should focus on public information and avoid confidential data.

Your compliance goal on every expert call: prevent either regime from being triggered by ensuring no material nonpublic information is shared or used.

## Practical Compliance Framework for Expert Calls Under Reg FD

Treat every expert call as a regulated interaction. The structure should be similar to what banks and large funds expect from GLG, AlphaSights, Third Bridge, Guidepoint, Tegus, and similar platforms. Conducting expert calls requires strict protocols to prevent selective disclosure of material nonpublic information.

Here's a five-step process:

1. **Scoping:** define the research question and explicitly exclude topics that would reasonably elicit MNPI. No unreleased earnings, no internal KPIs by name, no confidential contract terms, no upcoming rating agency actions.

2. **Expert selection and vetting:** run conflict checks on current employment at an issuer under active review, litigation or regulatory red flags, prior enforcement action, and non-disclosure obligations. Compliance protocols include verifying expert employment status and checking restricted lists before calls.

3. **Pre-call briefing:** send written ground rules to experts and clients. No MNPI, no confidential customer data, no discussion of live deals or undisclosed product launches by specific dates.

4. **Live call monitoring and escalation:** a moderator or compliance-trained participant monitors in real time and can redirect or terminate if the conversation drifts into sensitive territory.

5. **Post-call documentation:** log date, participants, topics discussed, and any concerns. Firms should document all meetings with experts for compliance purposes.

### Information Barriers and Call Protocols

Firms must establish information barriers between public and private sides. If your firm has a "private side" team sitting on boards, deal teams, or information walls, they shouldn't be on the same expert calls as public side investment professionals.

* Public side staff should receive training on identifying potential MNPI in real time and be empowered to stop or redirect a call if an expert drifts into sensitive territory. Employees should be trained on insider trading laws and compliance policies.

* All expert calls should be conducted on approved, recorded lines or platforms, with attendance logs and time stamps stored for internal review or regulator questions.

* Use a standard script at the start and end of every call where the expert confirms they won't share, and didn't share, material nonpublic information or breach any duty to current or former employers. Expert networks must employ compliance protocols requiring experts to sign agreements not to breach disclosure rules.

## Reg FD, Conference Calls, and Earnings Forecasts: What You Can and Can't Ask

Most Reg FD issues arise around earnings forecasts, internal KPIs, and forward-looking statements tied to specific time periods. Here's what to avoid and what's safe on an expert call.

**Off-limits question categories:**

* Explicit unreleased quarterly revenue or EPS figures (e.g., "What's Q4 2026 EPS going to be?")

* Non-public customer wins or losses above a materiality threshold

* Internal pipeline conversion data or undisclosed backlog numbers

* Unannounced restructurings, M&A activity, or leadership changes

* Such information falls squarely into the category of material non public information

**Safer alternatives:**

* Directional questions about demand trends in a sector

* Relative market share changes visible in the market

* Typical contract structures and procurement processes

* Observed competitor behavior based on publicly available signals

* General industry pricing trends or adoption patterns

Public issuer conference calls are different. When a CFO gives guidance or answers analyst questions on a public line with a transcript or replay, that's public disclosure. You can fully integrate it into your models. Reg FD was designed to ensure full and fair disclosure through these channels.

Even when an expert refers to a public disclosure, like a 10-K, 10-Q, earnings call, or press release, they shouldn't supplement it with unreleased "color" from internal decks or restricted data rooms.

### Analyst Reports, Rating Agencies, and Public vs Nonpublic Signals

Analyst reports, rating agency commentaries, and issuer press releases are all public disclosures. They fall cleanly outside Reg FD risk when you rely solely on what's been published. The SEC noted that reliance on publicly available information doesn't trigger fair disclosure concerns.

Private "whisper numbers" or off-the-record guidance that meaningfully changes published earnings forecasts or ratings can raise concerns if they can be traced to selective issuer communications. If an expert references a not-yet-public analyst report, treat it cautiously. Don't trade based on that information until it's publicly available or clearly non-material.

Trends in forecast dispersion, consensus shifts, and rating changes are all fair game for expert discussion, so long as the underlying drivers are themselves based on public or immaterial information. This is how fully informed market participants build models without crossing lines.

## Enforcement Trends and Legal Risks Around Expert Networks

While formal SEC enforcement action under Reg FD has been relatively rare since 2000, the cases that do surface often involve earnings guidance or internal metrics selectively shared with analysts ahead of public disclosure. The SEC adopted Regulation FD in October 2000 to prevent selective disclosure, and they've enforced it when companies cross the line.

The typical pattern looks like this: investor relations staff or senior executives hold small-group calls with sell-side analysts, provide nonpublic data like smartphone unit sales or channel inventory figures, analysts adjust their models, then the company "beats" consensus when earnings results are released. The SEC has treated this kind of sharing confidences as a violation.

In [expert network investigations](https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2011/2011-40.htm), the government pursued both civil and criminal actions against consultants who passed unreleased financial metrics to hedge funds. Individuals received multi-year [prison sentences](https://archives.fbi.gov/archives/newyork/press-releases/2011/expert-networking-firm-consultant-found-guilty-in-manhattan-federal-court-of-insider-trading-crimes) for sharing non public information about unreleased earnings and revenue figures. The legal risks are real, and they apply to experts, firms, and the entities that trade on the information.

If an expert is a current senior finance or IR officer and shares similar internal metrics on a supposedly "private" call, both they and trading clients face scrutiny. Well-documented procedures, clear no-MNPI ground rules, and immediate escalation when questionable disclosures occur are your best defense.

### What Regulators Look For in Expert Network Use

Regulators focus on specific patterns:

* Trading ahead of market-moving public disclosures, especially when repeated

* Using the same experts in close proximity to those trades

* Gaps or inconsistencies in compliance documentation

* Missing training records, unsigned expert contracts, or absent written policies around Reg FD and insider trading

**What you should maintain:**

* A central log of expert calls: date, time, participants, issuer or sector focus, high-level topics, who approved the call, and whether any concerns were escalated

* Periodic compliance review of a sample of call notes or transcripts to confirm there's no drift into earnings leaks or nonpublic contract news

* Best practices after expert calls include keeping detailed records and implementing periodic training for all staff involved in research

These records are commonly referred to as your "compliance paper trail," and they're often requested in the early stages of any regulatory inquiry.
