# Pension Fund Investment Research: A Practical Field Guide

Pension fund research isn't about beating a benchmark. It's about determining whether a plan can pay what it promised, whether the assets backing those promises are invested correctly, and whether you have enough information to act before a committee deadline. This guide gives you a repeatable framework for all three.

## What pension fund research actually needs to answer (and how this guide is structured)

Pension funds are specialized investment pools providing retirement income for long term savings. When you research one, you're not writing a stock pitch. You're assessing whether promised benefits are sustainable, sizing the risk that sits on a sponsor's balance sheet, and evaluating whether the people managing the money are making informed decisions fast enough.

This article helps you answer three questions:

1. **Is the pension plan affordable?** Look at the funded ratio, required contributions now and over the next decade, sponsor strength, and legal obligations.

2. **Are pension assets invested appropriately for long term investment obligations?** That means asset-liability matching, return assumptions, liquidity, exposure to alternative assets, and risk concentration.

3. **What information do you need to make decisions fast?** The right documents, quantitative diagnostics, red flags, manager insights, and scenario stress tests.

This guide is written for PE/VC associates, corporate M&A teams, and strategy consultants doing due diligence on pension funds or pension-heavy acquisition targets. Examples reference public pension funds in the US and Europe, defined benefit and defined contribution plans, and use specific historical dates and case studies.

## Core concepts: how pension funds, plans, and assets fit together

A pension fund is a pool of pension assets set aside and invested to back the obligations of one or more pension plans. The plan is the contract with members. The fund is the vehicle holding investments. They're distinct, and mixing them up leads to sloppy analysis.

* **Defined benefit (DB) vs defined contribution (DC):** DB plans promise a formula-based pension benefit (salary x years x accrual factor), and the sponsor bears investment and longevity risk. DC plans depend on contributions and investment returns, and the member bears the risk. Example: a US corporate DB plan closed to new entrants since 2012 vs a 401(k) DC plan with auto-enrollment since 2006.

* **Open vs closed:** Open DB plans still accept new entrants. Closed plans don't, but existing members still accrue benefits. Active to passive worker ratios indicate the structural foundation's health of a pension plan, and closed plans see that ratio decline over time.

* **Public vs private:** Public pension plans are managed by government entities while private plans are managed by employers. A public pension fund like CalPERS is governed by state statute. A closed UK corporate scheme falls under trust law and the Pensions Regulator.

* **Scale:** Public pension fund assets reached US$58.5 trillion in 2023. Public pension fund assets total approximately $58.5 trillion globally, with non-OECD jurisdictions adding another \~$2.9 trillion. Public pension funds primarily serve public sector employees.

* **Regulation:** Public pension funds are regulated under public sector law. Private pension funds are regulated under private sector law. Public pension funds in the US managed [$10 trillion in assets as of 2016](https://www.nasra.org/publicfundsurvey). Private pension funds in the US hold $9.1 trillion in assets. ERISA compliance provides regulatory protections for US private sector pension funds. Private pension funds often have fewer regulatory restrictions than public funds.

## Types of pension plans and why they matter for investment research

Plan design drives investment risk, funding strategy, and the questions you ask in diligence. Get the plan type wrong, and every number downstream is unreliable.

* **DB plans:** Benefits are based on final average salary and years of service. The sponsor bears investment risks, making these typical in older corporate and public sector plans. DB plans typically offer more predictable retirement income, which is why employees value them, but they create open-ended liabilities for employers.

* **DC plans:** Contributions are fixed as a percentage of base salary. In DC plans, members bear investment risks. Benefit outcomes depend on contributions and returns, with typical examples being US 401(k) plans and UK auto-enrolment schemes launched after 2012. DC plans allow for greater portability of benefits, which matters in economies with mobile workforces.

* **Hybrid plans:** Hybrid pension plans combine elements of both defined benefit and defined contribution schemes. US cash-balance plans credit a notional return to individual accounts. The Netherlands introduced collective DC reforms effective 2023, blending risk-sharing and individual accounts. These complicate modeling because you must track guaranteed minimums, interest crediting rates, and portability rules.

* **Research implications for DB:** Focus on funded status, liability duration (sensitivity to discount rate changes), deficit volatility, and contribution policy. Funded status evaluates the ratio of a plan's assets to its liabilities.

* **Research implications for DC:** Focus on default investment options (returns, glidepaths), fees, administration quality, and member behavior (withdrawal rates, leakage, retirement timing).

* **Cross-cutting:** Investment return is the primary metric for measuring pension fund growth. Investment earnings account for 60% to 65% of pension fund revenue. That means asset management quality matters more than contribution levels in most mature plans.

## Pension fund investment strategies and asset allocation

Most large pension funds use a strategic asset allocation policy anchored to long term investment return assumptions and liability profiles. Asset allocation impacts pension fund performance significantly, so getting this section right matters more than almost anything else in your research.

* **Common asset class ranges for mature DB funds (US public plans, FY 2024):** Public equities \~44%, fixed income \~21%, real estate \~9%, alternatives (private equity, hedge funds, infrastructure) \~24%, cash \~1%. These numbers come from [NASRA's public fund survey](https://www.nasra.org/content.asp?contentid=37).

* **Shift to alternatives:** Pension funds are increasingly investing in alternative assets like private equity. In the early 2000s, alternatives were under 10% of US public fund portfolios. By 2024, large sophisticated funds (above $25 billion AUM) averaged \~37% in alternative investments. As of 2023, many pension funds are diversifying into real estate investments as well.

* **Passive trend:** Pension funds are moving towards passive investment methods like index funds. As of 2023, many pension funds prefer passive investment methods for their public equities and fixed income sleeves to control costs.

* **Liability-Driven Investing (LDI):** Liability-Driven Investing aligns a fund's assets with expected future payouts to reduce volatility. UK schemes adopted LDI strategies heavily in the 2000s. The [2022 UK gilt crisis](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2023/an-anatomy-of-the-2022-gilt-market-crisis) exposed severe risks: leveraged LDI forced pooled funds into fire sales when gilt yields spiked, threatening more than £1 trillion in LDI products. High risk concentration in derivatives and insufficient collateral buffers were the root causes.

* **DC default funds:** Lifecycle and target-date strategies de-risk as members approach retirement. A typical glidepath starts at \~90% equities at age 25 and shifts to \~40% equities by age 60, with the rest in bonds and cash.

* **ESG integration:** Large European public funds (Dutch, Nordic) integrate climate risk, exclusions, and impact investing mandates while still meeting return targets. This is growing but uneven across markets and asset classes.

## How to research a pension fund: step-by-step framework

Here's a practical, repeatable process you can apply in due diligence, manager selection, or competitive research. Follow the steps in order. Skip nothing.

1. **Problem definition:** Clarify what you're assessing. Is it a single-employer DB plan in an acquisition? Benchmarking a public pension fund against peers? Evaluating a fund managers' fit for a pension mandate? The plan type, jurisdiction, and research purpose shape every question that follows.

2. **Document review:** Request specific documents. You need the most recent actuarial valuation report (with assumptions for discount rate, mortality, salary escalation), funding policy, investment policy statement (IPS), asset-liability study, and the latest annual report (e.g., a 2023 CAFR for a US public plan). For DC, get default fund fact sheets, fee schedules, and member take-up data.

3. **Quantitative diagnostics:** Run high-level checks. Funding ratio measures the health of a defined benefit plan by comparing total assets to liabilities. Track funded ratio trends over 5 to 10 years. Compare contribution history against actuarially required contributions. Analyze projected benefit payments and asset allocation vs peers. Key factors to analyze when researching pension funds include funded status and investment strategy. Comparing the investment performance of pension funds requires evaluating benchmarks and liabilities, not just raw returns.

4. **Qualitative interviews:** Talk to people who've sat in the room. Former CIOs explain risk culture and decision timelines. Ex-investment committee members reveal governance dynamics. External investment managers describe how the fund behaves during market stress. Consultants who advise the plan fill in gaps around assumption-setting and benefit reform debates.

5. **Scenario analysis:** Build stress cases. Model a 200 basis point drop in discount rates. Run an equity drawdown similar to 2008 (\~40%) or March 2020 (\~34%). Test mortality improvement beyond expected levels. Layer in an inflation shock. Each scenario should map to its impact on funded status, employer contributions, and a buyer's P&L.

6. **Synthesis and red flags:** Watch for these:

   * Required contributions consistently falling short of actuarial recommendations.

   * Overly optimistic return assumptions (anything above \~7% real is suspect in most jurisdictions).

   * Heavy allocation to illiquid alternative assets without a cash flow plan for benefit payments.

   * Frequent changes in actuarial assumptions with no disclosed rationale.

   * High manager turnover or lack of transparency in board minutes.

## Case studies: pension fund research in real transactions

Concrete case studies make abstract pension research tangible. Here are patterns that show up repeatedly in deal work.

* **US corporate carve-out (2010s):** A single-employer DB plan looked modest on paper. Early-stage research discovered that employees were retiring at age 60 with full benefits, not 65 as the buyer initially assumed. That single finding increased the expected period of benefit payments by \~5 years per retiree. Adjusted assumptions (updated IRS mortality tables, realistic retirement ages, annuity buyout pricing) increased liabilities by 30 to 60%, which changed purchase price discussions entirely.

* **US public pension fund alternative assets review (\~2015 to 2020):** A mid-sized state plan grew its alternative assets allocation from \~10% to \~30%. Research teams interviewing former staff and fund managers discovered that vintage selection bias and lagged private equity valuations were smoothing reported returns. Actual downside risk was higher than published data suggested. This matters if you're assessing the financial security of a plan sponsor or the capital needs of a public entity.

* **UK DC master trust acquisition (post-2015 pension freedoms):** A private buyer evaluating a DC master trust analyzed default investment options, fees, and member take-up rates. The deal nearly collapsed because expected member behavior diverged sharply from the seller's assumptions. Early withdrawals were higher than modeled, reducing fee income durability and the value of the business.

* **Using expert calls to test assumptions:** In each of these cases, conversations with ex-CIOs validated how realistic retirement ages and return assumptions actually were. Former actuaries provided data on mortality trend assumptions. Ex-board members confirmed whether the sponsor's willingness to meet future contribution obligations was real or political posturing. ## Evaluating investment managers for pension mandates

Many pension funds don't pick individual securities. They hire external investment managers across equities, credit, multi-asset, real assets, and infrastructure. Your job is to assess whether those managers are worth their fees and capable of performing under stress.

* **Core questions:** What's the manager's edge vs benchmark? Can they deploy capital at scale without harming returns? How stable is the team (turnover since 2008, 2020)? How did they perform in stress periods (2008, 2011, 2018, 2020, 2022 gilt crisis)? Are fees aligned with long-term outcomes (hurdle rates, clawbacks, carried interest)?

* **Manager search process:** Typical public funds run RFPs, build consultant shortlists, conduct on-site visits, then present finalists to an investment committee. Timeline: 6 to 12 months from initial sourcing to approval.

* **DB vs DC mandates:** For DB, assess whether a manager can handle both LDI (liability hedging) and growth portfolios. For DC or IRA mandates, look for target-date or multi-asset capabilities with low fees. High management and administration fees can reduce the ultimate value of a pension fund, so fee analysis isn't optional.

* **Cross-checking with experts:** Use interviews with ex-portfolio managers, ex-OCIO staff, or former consultant research heads to cross-check a manager's claimed process. Institutional investors and asset owners rely on these high conviction assessments to separate marketing from reality.

## Public pension funds: special research considerations

Public funds have unique transparency, political, and regulatory dynamics. The data is more available, but interpreting it requires more skepticism.

* US public pension funds are typically governed by state statutes and must publish annual reports. European public funds follow EU rules such as the [IORP II directive](https://www.oecd-ilibrary.org/en/publications/pension-markets-in-focus-2025_b095d0a0-en.html) effective from 2017 to 2019. Governments set the rules, and those rules shape investment behavior.

* Public pension fund assumptions (e.g., 7%+ nominal return targets) are often influenced by politics and can lag market reality. [NASRA data shows](https://grsconsulting.com/2025/06/25/nasra-updates-brief-on-public-pension-plan-investment-return-assumptions-4/) that median assumed returns have dropped from \~8% a decade ago to \~7% today. You should back-test these against 10 to 20 year asset class returns in financial markets to assess whether they're credible.

* Pension funds must have a governing body accountable to members. Governance structures influence pension fund stability and performance. OECD guidelines recommend clear separation of operational and oversight roles. Effective governance ensures transparency and accountability in pension funds. Many pension funds face governance challenges affecting member interests, especially when political appointees sit on investment committees.

* **Practical research moves:** Read board and investment committee minutes for debates over benefit reforms, discount rate changes, and contribution policy. Scan media coverage for political pressure or mis-reporting. Use datasets like [NASRA's Public Fund Survey](https://www.nasra.org/publicfundsurvey) to benchmark size, allocation mix, and use of alternatives.

* **Disclosure differences:** Public funds publish CAFRs and actuarial reports. Corporate plans disclose less unless required by SEC filings or transaction data rooms. This affects how you build your research priorities and interview guides.

## Integrating pension analysis into M&A and investment theses

Pension research should change pricing, structure, and sometimes whether you do the deal at all. Treat it as a core diligence workstream, not a footnote.

* **Enterprise value adjustments:** A PE or corporate buyer should translate funding gaps into value. Treat deficits like debt or model top-up contributions over 5 to 10 years. If a plan is 70% funded, the unfunded portion is real money the buyer will eventually be paying.

* **Covenant strength:** Assess the sponsor's credit quality, legal ability to change pension benefits, and history of contributions during downturns like 2009 and 2020. Strong employers in developed economies with steady profits can absorb pension volatility. Weak sponsors can't.

* **Scenario presentation:** Present 2 to 3 scenarios in your investment committee memo: base case, adverse market conditions (equity drawdown plus rate shock), and regulatory change (mortality table updates, discount rate floors). Tie each to IRR impact and show what happens to the retirees and beneficiaries under each.

* **DC platform deals:** For deals involving DC platforms or master trusts, integration focus shifts to admin quality, fee revenue durability, and member retention. Balance sheet risk is lower, but income risk and employment opportunities for retaining key admin staff are real.

* **Expert calls for refinement:** Use expert calls to quickly refine your downside case before investment committee. When the sponsor's report is incomplete or skewed, a 45-minute call with someone who managed that fund's money is worth more than another week of spreadsheet work.
