# Endowment Investment Research: A Practical Field Guide

Endowment research is the analysis of how institutions invest, spend, and govern their long term capital. This guide covers what you need to know to run a practical endowment research project in 2026, whether you're benchmarking peers, evaluating an OCIO platform, or preparing for a policy shift.

## What endowment research actually is (and why it matters in 2026)

Endowment investment research is the mix of market, manager, and policy analysis that informs how endowment funds at higher education institutions and private colleges in North America and Europe allocate capital, set spending rates, and structure governance. Effective endowment research involves evaluating financial, strategic, and governance components. It's analysis, management, and strategic allocation of endowment funds, not stock picks for retail investors.

This guide is for investment committees, CIOs, and strategy or M&A teams that need fast, compliant primary research on how peer institutions run their money. Not for individual investors.

The pressure points in 2024 through 2026 are real. Endowment taxes were overhauled under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act in 2025. Political scrutiny of large university endowments is intensifying. Donor intent disputes are hitting courts. ESG and divestment campaigns keep expanding. Most of the questions you'll need to answer boil down to: "How do peers invest and govern their endowment fund, what are they changing now, and what practices can we copy or avoid?"

**A properly done endowment research project beats relying on glossy annual reports. You get peer-normed spending rates, legal constraints, tax exposure, and insider governance practices that reports often obscure.**

## Core concepts: how modern endowment funds are structured

An endowment is a pool of donor capital invested to support an institution in perpetuity or over the long term. The principal amount is usually preserved while earnings fund operations. Typically, 4-6% of endowment assets are spent annually, with a common spending rate of 4%-5% of market value. The goal is intergenerational equity: support students and faculty today without eroding purchasing power for future generations.

Here's how the main types break down:

* Restricted endowment: used only as designated by donors. Restricted endowments must be used according to donor agreements. Example: an endowed chair in chemistry established in 1987 at a private college, restricted to support chemistry faculty salaries only. Donors can restrict endowment funds for a specific purpose, and ignoring those donor restrictions is termed "invading" the endowment.

* Unrestricted funds: can be used at the institution's discretion to support general operations or mission expenses.

* Term endowment: allows access to funds after specific conditions are met, such as a fixed time period. Example: a 10-year term endowment launched in 2022 to fund a Data Science Institute.

* Quasi endowment funds: flexible pools designated by the board as long term but not intended for permanent, perpetual use. They're treated like endowments but without binding donor restrictions.

Consider Harvard University as a reference point. Harvard's endowment was valued at over $53 billion in FY2024 and consists of approximately 14,765 individual donor-directed funds. Over 80% of those funds are donor-restricted. Harvard's endowment supports over 14,000 individual funds spanning scholarships, professorships, academic programs, and scientific research.

Gifts are typically booked with associated restrictions, then unitized into a common investment pool. Major US universities follow NACUBO/Commonfund norms for valuation and reporting. Endowments are often invested in diversified portfolios to minimize risk, and the size of an endowment is a strong predictor of performance.

Endowment spending primarily funds academic research, financial aid, and campus operations. Endowment income helps pay for endowed faculty positions and maintain campus facilities. Endowed professorships typically cost between $1 million and $5 million. A significant portion of endowment spending is dedicated to student financial aid programs. In FY2024, Harvard's endowment contributed $749 million in scholarships alone. Endowment distributions provided $2.5 billion in FY2025, representing [37% of Harvard's total revenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endowment_tax).

Endowments link past donations with future educational goals. They provide financial security to institutions during economic downturns, allow institutions to maintain operations despite decreased enrollment or unforeseen expenses, and help mitigate the impact of fluctuations in state funding and tuition revenue. Endowment payouts can reduce the reliance on tuition increases, creating a steady and reliable revenue stream for educational institutions. Endowments can also fund specific programs as directed by donors, from teaching fellowships to grants for charitable activities.

### Legal and policy framework: UPMIFA and the Institutional Funds Act era

The uniform prudent management of institutional funds, codified as UPMIFA, is the current legal standard in almost all US states governing the investment and spending of institutional funds, including endowment funds. It replaced the earlier institutional funds act framework (UMIFA).

* After the 2008 financial crisis, many endowments became "underwater," meaning the endowment's market value fell below the original gift principal. Under the old rules, institutions couldn't spend principal. UPMIFA allows boards to consider prudence in spending decisions even when market value dips below the original gift amount, provided they document their reasoning.

* Endowments must comply with donor agreements and state regulations. Donor intent is legally binding. Named chairs, department-restricted funds, or geographic restrictions hold up in court. When a donor is deceased or conditions can't be met, cy-près doctrine or court modification may apply.

* Spending policies should ensure endowment growth in real value over time, protecting against inflation.

* Practical research questions: How do boards interpret and document prudence? How do they justify spending rates in volatile markets? How often do they override published policy?

## Governance: boards, investment committees, and operating partners

Governance quality usually matters more than picking the right investment managers, especially for endowments above $100 million. Governance and fiduciary oversight are crucial in managing endowment funds. Strong oversight and disciplined decision-making differentiate high performers.

* The investment committee sets long term risk and return targets, approves strategic asset allocation, hires and fires OCIOs or external managers, and monitors the endowment's performance versus policy benchmarks. Analyses of liquidity risks are important for effective endowment research at this level.

* At a typical private college, the board of trustees sets the broad mission and approves the investment committee. Example: a 12-member investment committee at a midwestern liberal arts college overseeing a $600 million pool in 2026 might include trustees, faculty representatives, alumni, and external investment professionals.

* Internal CIO teams, OCIO providers, and consultancies interact in ways that matter for research. You need to ask: Who makes which decisions? Who monitors? Who authorizes rebalancing or manager replacement?

* This governance data isn't in annual reports. You get it through interviews with former committee members, CIOs, or OCIO staff. That's where expert research fits.

### Spending rules, payout policies, and long term discipline

Endowment payouts typically range from 4% to 6% annually. Common formulas include a fixed percentage of a three-year rolling market value or hybrid rules tied to inflation. These rules exist to support future generations while funding current operations.

* After the 2020 COVID shock, many large private universities reduced or delayed planned payout increases. Smaller colleges responded to 2022-2023 volatility by lowering effective spending rates or deferring capital projects.

* What your research should surface: Have peers ever overridden their published spending policy? Under what conditions? How do they communicate that to donors and auditors? How do rating agencies react?

* Restricted endowment segments often have fixed payout requirements, limiting flexibility. The unrestricted portion is more flexible and typically used to respond to fiscal stress. These decisions flow directly into the operating budget, affecting financial aid, faculty salaries, and program funding.

## Investment strategy: where endowments actually put the money

Most endowment portfolios mix public equities, fixed income, real assets, and alternatives. Allocation correlates strongly with size. In FY25, among institutions in the [NACUBO-Commonfund Study](https://www.nacubo.org/Press-Releases/2026/US-Higher-Education-Endowments-Report-Stable-Returns-Increase-Spending-to-33-4-Billion-in-FY25), private and alternative strategies accounted for roughly 54.5% of allocations, public equities about 31.5%, and fixed income about 11%. Endowments over $5 billion had approximately 62.5% in alternatives.

* Key questions for your research: What is the current and target asset allocation? How much money invested in private equity, venture capital, real estate, hedge funds? What liquidity buffers exist? Are there mission-related or impact portfolios?

* The "endowment model" popularized by Yale and Harvard University pushed high allocations to illiquid alternatives. But don't mythologize it. Use specific allocation bands from recent reports when benchmarking.

* Term endowments or quasi-endowments may be invested with shorter duration or lower risk profiles. Benchmark these separately.

* Peers in higher education often share high-level allocation data publicly. Details like manager concentration, lock-ups, co-investments, and fees almost always come from insider conversations with investment professionals.

### ESG, divestment, and impact investing in endowment research

From 2019 through 2026, ESG and divestment campaigns have become central to endowment debates. Targets include fossil fuels, private prisons, weapons, and AI surveillance. Donors, students, and faculty are all pushing for change in specific areas.

* [George Washington University](https://gwhatchet.com/2025/12/16/gw-meets-2025-fossil-fuel-divestment-goal-set-to-hit-interim-carbon-neutrality-target-by-year-end/) met its 2025 fossil fuel divestment goal, with exposure now under 2% of endowment assets.

* [Princeton reversed](https://paw.princeton.edu/article/princeton-reverses-course-fossil-fuel-divestment) its earlier fossil fuel divestment decision in June 2026, now aiming for net zero by 2046.

* [Middlebury College](https://www.middlebury.edu/energy-2028/whats-happening/divestment) committed to phase out direct fossil fuel investments by 2034. Sustainability-related investments now represent about 20% of their portfolio.

* Impact investments are often small carve-outs: local housing, clean energy, diverse managers. Research should ask about size, performance measurement, and how these interact with donor intent and risk-adjusted returns.

## Taxes and regulation: endowment tax, reporting, and public scrutiny

Under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, private colleges and universities with 500 or more tuition-paying students and net assets of at least $500,000 per student became subject to a 1.4% excise tax on net investment income. That changed in 2025.

* The [One Big Beautiful Bill Act](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2025/07/final-one-big-beautiful-bill-act) replaced the flat rate with graduated endowment taxes of 1.4%, 4%, and 8% based on endowment assets per eligible student. The threshold was raised to institutions with at least 3,000 tuition-paying students, effective for taxable years beginning after 2025.

* For each institution your research covers, clarify: Is it subject to the new tax? What's the endowment per student? What rate applies? How many dollars in tax per fiscal year? Has it shifted its asset mix or spending policy to reduce taxable net investment income?

* Related pressures: proposals to expand the tax further, state-level scrutiny of tuition and reserves, and transparency campaigns demanding more granular endowment disclosures.

* Public universities, private colleges, and independent foundations face different rules. Don't assume one framework fits all nonprofits or organizations.

### Endowment taxes and cross-border issues

Non-US institutions, including UK, EU, and Canadian universities, handle endowment investment income taxation differently. Research users should watch for withholding rules, unrelated business taxable income (UBTI), and treaty effects that change after-tax returns on money managed across borders.

Large US endowments often allocate to offshore funds or use blocker structures for alternatives. This surfaces in interviews with former tax or investment staff. The goal of research here is to understand after-tax return expectations and constraints, not to provide capital or tax advice.

## How to run an endowment research project with experts

Desktop research and annual reports from colleges and universities are useful but incomplete. The financial resources you need, such as governance practices, fee structures, and override decisions, come from speaking with former CIOs, investment committee members, OCIO staff, and portfolio managers who contribute directly to endowment management.

A simple 5-step process:

1. Define the decision you're informing (e.g., benchmarking spending rates, evaluating an OCIO).

2. Map the peer set by size, geography, type (private vs. public, small vs. large).

3. Design interview guides and surveys targeting governance, tax, and investment strategy questions.

4. Recruit vetted experts: ex-directors of university investment offices, former foundation committee members, OCIO partners, senior institutional consultants.

5. Synthesize themes and benchmarks, both quantitative (average payout rate, allocation to alternatives, fee median) and qualitative (override norms, ESG policies, donor restriction enforcement).

Compliance is non-negotiable. No MNPI. No current material nonpublic documents. Clear guidelines matching or exceeding what platforms like GLG or AlphaSights use.

### Interview and survey questions that actually work

* Governance and decision-making cadence: "How often does your investment committee meet? What criteria trigger a review of spending policy?"

* Current and target asset allocation: "Walk me through your current mix and what targets you're working toward. What would need to happen to shift allocation materially?"

* Manager selection and termination criteria: "What are your criteria for hiring or firing external managers in alternatives? How frequently do you review performance vs benchmark?"

* ESG and divestment policies: "Has your institution changed its fossil fuel exposure since 2020? How do you balance returns vs. mission demands?"

* Handling of restricted endowment funds: "How do you track restricted vs. unrestricted segments? How often do donor restrictions force deviation from your preferred policy?"

* Responses to endowment tax changes: "How are you modeling the 2025 tax law changes? Are you planning asset rebalancing or spending rule adjustments?"

Use structured surveys to quantify benchmarks (payout rate, number of managers, average fee load, endowment per student) across a dozen or more comparable institutions.

## Using endowment research in real-world decisions

Concrete use cases: a PE fund evaluating OCIO platforms to serve university endowment clients. A VC firm validating demand for software used in university investment offices. A bank structuring lending or derivative overlays tailored to large endowments. A university considering a policy shift on ESG, spending rates, or asset allocation after the 2025 tax law change.

* Good endowment research focuses on decisions with clear dates and numbers: a policy revision planned for 2027, rebalancing trigger levels, or changes to sustainable payout formulas. Not broad theory.
