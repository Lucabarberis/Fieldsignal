# Expert Call Income Tax: What US Consultants Need to Know

## Introduction: How Expert Call Income Is Taxed in the US

If you earn money from expert calls, paid surveys, or advisory work through expert networks, the IRS treats that as taxable self-employment income. There's no exception for "side hustle" amounts, occasional calls, or small payments. It's business income, and you owe taxes on it.

That applies whether you're getting paid by GLG, AlphaSights, Guidepoint, Third Bridge, FieldSignal, or any other expert network companies. The expert network industry is valued at $1.9 billion and facilitates over 1 million calls annually. If you're one of the consultants on those calls, the money hits your tax return.

Some platforms issue a Form 1099-NEC. Others don't. Either way, you're required to report every dollar on your tax return. The IRS doesn't care whether you received a form or not.

This article covers US federal income tax rules for tax years 2025 and 2026, with notes on state and local issues where relevant. Tax codes are dense and frequently updated, so treat this as a starting point. FieldSignal is writing from the perspective of a boutique expert network that works regularly with US-based consultants and investors. This isn't individual tax advice. Talk to your own CPA or enrolled agent for your unique situation.

## What Counts As Expert Network Income?

Expert calls are one-on-one consultations with industry experts, but income from expert networks goes beyond just phone calls. Here's what counts:

* One-on-one phone or video calls (the most common way experts earn through networks)

* Group calls and panel calls

* Paid surveys (expert network surveys typically pay between $40 and $80)

* Advisory fees, consulting honoraria, and research participation incentives

Common sources include GLG, AlphaSights, Third Bridge, Guidepoint, Tegus, ProSapient, Coleman Research, Atheneum, Mosaic Research Management, Inex One, AlphaSense, and FieldSignal. Expert networks connect clients with over 1.2 million vetted professionals, including former executives, c level executives, and subject matter experts across specific industries. Clients can gather insights from up to 100 experts through surveys alone.

Expert consultations can start at rates of $1,000 per hour for senior professionals, and even a standard phone consultation at a lower hourly rate still generates taxable income. Clients can schedule expert calls within hours of a request, and many expert consultations can be scheduled within 24 to 48 hours, which means payments can come in fast.

Expert calls typically last one hour and focus on specific topics. They help clients gain unique insights not found in reports. Expert networks help clients understand industry trends and competitive landscapes, and [expert networks arrange over 1 million calls annually](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p334) to do exactly that. GLG offers qualitative and quantitative research options beyond just calls.

Payments in USD via ACH, wire, PayPal, Wise, or prepaid card are all taxable. If you work with multiple expert networks, you can treat payments from all of them as one combined consulting activity when you file. You don't need a separate Schedule C for each network unless the activities are fundamentally different businesses.

## Is Expert Call Work a Business or a Hobby for Tax Purposes?

The IRS usually views recurring expert network consulting as self-employment, even if you only do a few calls per month. The key factors are profit motive, regularity, and whether you treat the activity seriously.

Factors the IRS considers:

* How often you do calls (monthly, weekly)

* Whether you maintain profiles on expert network platforms

* Whether you invest in equipment or tools for the work

* Whether you intend to earn a profit

Here's a concrete example. A PE associate earning $9,000 in 2025 from GLG, Third Bridge, and FieldSignal calls should treat it as business income. Private equity investors and other business professionals doing this work regularly have clear profit motive.

Business status means filing Schedule C and paying both income tax and self-employment tax on your net profit. If the IRS classifies your work as a hobby, you can't deduct expenses, and you lose the ability to reduce your taxable income.

Calling yourself a "consultant" or "independent contractor" in your expert network profiles supports the business classification. If your LinkedIn says the same thing, that consistency helps if the IRS ever reviews your return.

## Forms You'll See (or Not See): 1099s and Your Tax Return

Form 1099-NEC is issued when a US payor pays a US individual $600 or more in a calendar year for services, including expert calls. Starting in tax year 2026, the threshold for issuing 1099-NEC forms [increases to $2,000](https://glgmembersolutions.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360038733233-For-U-S-Network-Members-Will-I-receive-a-tax-form).

Some networks issue 1099-NEC reliably. Others don't, or they don't issue 1099-NECs to corporations. Even when a network doesn't send you a form, the income is still taxable. Don't wait for a form to decide whether to report it.

Track your total income yourself. Download annual statements or invoices from each expert network portal. Reconcile those against your bank deposits. Keep a simple spreadsheet with these columns:

* Date of call or survey

* Expert network name

* Client type (PE, corporate, hedge fund)

* Project ID (if provided)

* Amount paid

Where does the income go on your tax return? For most individuals, it goes on Schedule C attached to Form 1040. If you had a single, isolated call and aren't sure how to classify it, discuss with a tax preparer. But for anyone doing this regularly, Schedule C is the answer.

## Self-Employment Tax and Quarterly Estimated Payments

In addition to regular income tax, net profit from expert calls is subject to [self-employment tax](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employed-individuals-tax-center) covering Social Security and Medicare, at a combined rate of 15.3%.

For 2025, the Social Security portion (12.4%) applies to net earnings up to $176,100. For 2026, that cap increases to $184,500. The Medicare portion (2.9%) applies to all net earnings, with an additional 0.9% on income above $200,000 for single filers.

Here's a concrete example for tax year 2025:

* You earn $12,000 from expert networks

* You have $2,000 in deductible expenses

* Net self-employment income: $10,000

* Approximate self-employment tax: $1,530

* Plus federal income tax on that net income

Expert consultations can start at rates of $1,000 per hour, so even a few calls per month can push your earning potential into territory where quarterly estimated payments are required. You need to make estimated payments if you expect to owe $1,000 or more beyond W-2 withholding.

Quarterly due dates:

1. April 15

2. June 15

3. September 15

4. January 15 of the following year

Set aside 25% to 35% of each expert call payment into a separate account. That covers federal tax, self-employment tax, and most state income tax obligations.

## Typical Deductible Expenses for Expert Network Consultants

Expert call income is 1099 contractor income. Ordinary and necessary business expenses reduce your taxable profit, which means direct tax savings.

Common deductible expense categories:

* A portion of home internet and phone plan (allocated between personal and business use)

* Noise-canceling headset, webcam, microphone

* Note-taking software, scheduling tools, secure document storage

* Tax preparation fees and bookkeeping software

* Professional memberships related to your consulting expertise

If you meet in person with a client arranged by an expert network, travel expenses are deductible. The standard mileage rate for 2026 is $0.725 per mile. Hotel and meals (at 50%) count too.

Tax consultants help clients with tax savings strategies, and a good one will identify deductions you'd miss. But you need documentation: receipts, bank statements, invoices, and call logs. If you use your phone plan for both your W-2 job and consulting, only the consulting portion is deductible. The IRS is strict about allocation.

For 2025, 100% additional first-year depreciation is allowed for certain qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025. If you bought a new headset, webcam, or monitor for your consulting work, that's relevant.

## State Income Tax, City Tax, and Where the Call "Happens"

For most consultants, the state that matters is your state of residence on the date you perform the expert call. If you live in California, you pay California income tax on that income. If you live in Texas, there's no state income tax.

The client's location doesn't control your state tax. A New York PE fund hiring you through FieldSignal doesn't make your income New York-sourced if you're sitting in Austin. Similarly, the expert network's HQ (GLG in New York, Third Bridge in London) is irrelevant.

City income taxes apply in a few places. NYC, Philadelphia, and some Ohio cities tax residents on all income. If you physically sit in one of those cities for your calls, local taxes may apply.

Here's an example. You live in Austin, Texas and do calls for a Chicago-based investor through FieldSignal. Investment firms and hedge funds based in other states don't create state tax liability for you in Texas. Federal income tax and self-employment tax still apply, but you owe zero state income tax.

If you frequently travel to client sites in other states while doing consulting work, you may need to file nonresident returns. That's a situation where professionals helping with multi-state allocation, specifically a tax preparer familiar with various industries, becomes worth the cost.

## Handling Expert Network Income While Working a W-2 Job

Here's a realistic scenario. You're a full-time corporate strategy or PE associate. You do 5 to 10 expert calls a month on evenings and weekends. Your W-2 employer withholds taxes on your salary, but that withholding doesn't cover your 1099 consulting income.

Two approaches to avoid an unpleasant surprise in April:

1. Increase your W-2 withholding via Form W-4 at your employer. Add enough to cover the estimated tax on your consulting income.

2. Make separate quarterly estimated payments using IRS Form 1040-ES for the expert network income.

Failing to plan leads to an unexpected balance due and underpayment penalties. Once your expert call income crosses a few thousand dollars, run a mid-year estimate. Use the IRS Form 1040-ES worksheets or talk to a tax preparer. The process isn't complicated, but ignoring it is a potential risk.

## Recordkeeping, Compliance, and Working Within a Compliance Framework

Tax compliance and expert network compliance are different things. Tax compliance means reporting income accurately on your tax return. Network compliance means following rules about MNPI, insider trading, screening questions, employer conflicts, and research goals.

Expert networks like GLG, AlphaSights, Third Bridge, Guidepoint, and FieldSignal require you to accept a compliance framework that may reference your tax status as an independent contractor. These aren't optional.

Records to keep for 3 to 7 years:

* Expert network contracts and engagement terms

* Annual earnings statements from each network

* Call logs with dates, network, client type, and topic

* Invoices and payment confirmations

* Notes on whether calls were done in personal capacity or through your employer

Accurate records support your reported hourly rate, your expense deductions, and the fact that you weren't acting as an undisclosed agent of your employer. They also demonstrate that your consulting activity is a real business, not a hobby.

Align your profiles. If your expert network profile and LinkedIn headline say "independent consultant" or "advisor," that should match what you claim on Schedule C. Consistency matters if the IRS reviews your return or if you need to identify and support your business classification. Clients can access insights from various industry experts quickly through these networks, and as an expert providing access to your specialized knowledge, your records should reflect that.

## Special Issues: Foreign Expert Networks, Currency, and Treaties

Some expert networks are non-US entities paying US individuals. A London-based or EU-based firm may not issue US Forms 1099. That doesn't make the income non-taxable.

US citizens and residents must report worldwide income on their tax return. If you receive EUR or GBP payments, convert them to USD at a reasonable annual or transaction-specific exchange rate.

W-8BEN and W-9 forms relate to whether you're treated as a US taxpayer for withholding purposes. Filling out a W-8BEN doesn't make income non-taxable. It may only reduce or eliminate withholding under a tax treaty.

Tax treaty rules mainly apply when you're physically outside the US for long periods, working as a consultant for non-US clients. That's a situation for a cross-border tax preparer, not a blog post.

Here's a concrete example. You live in New York and do calls for a London hedge fund through a UK-based expert network. You physically perform those calls from your apartment in New York. The income is US-sourced, fully taxable on your US return, and reported on Schedule C. The network's location doesn't change that. You still need to connect it to your complete tax filing.

## Checklist: Getting Your Expert Network Taxes Right Each Year

Use this checklist each tax season to stay prepared:

* **January/early February:** Download annual income reports from every expert network you worked with. Don't rely on 1099 forms alone.

* **Reconcile income:** Match network statements against bank deposits. Flag any discrepancies.

* **Categorize expenses:** Sort receipts and invoices into deductible categories (equipment, internet, software, travel, tax preparation).

* **Decide on filing approach:** Schedule C for most taxpayers. If your income or structure changed, consult a tax preparer.

* **Review estimated payments:** Determine if quarterly estimated payments are needed for the new year, especially if your hourly rate or call volume increased and your earning potential grew.

* **Set quarterly reminders:** Put calendar alerts for April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 to update your income and expense log.

* **Don't reconstruct in March:** If you update your records quarterly, you won't spend hours rebuilding a full year of data during tax season.

* **File or extend by April deadline:** If you need more time, file an extension, but still pay estimated taxes owed.

## When to Reconsider Your Structure (LLC, S-Corp, or Staying Sole Proprietor)

Many consultants start as sole proprietors reporting expert call income on Schedule C. No formal entity is required. For most PE/VC associates and corporate strategists doing occasional expert calls, this is usually sufficient, and it delivers outstanding results from a simplicity standpoint.

Triggers to talk to a tax preparer or attorney about forming an LLC:

* Annual expert network income exceeding $40,000 to $60,000

* Signing larger advisory retainers with clients

* Building a broader consulting practice beyond expert network calls

* Wanting liability protection for your advisory work

An LLC doesn't automatically change your tax treatment. A single-member LLC is "disregarded" for federal tax purposes unless you elect otherwise. But it can provide liability protection and signal professionalism to clients.

Moving to an S-Corp changes how self-employment taxes are calculated. You'd pay yourself a "reasonable salary" and take remaining profit as distributions, potentially reducing self-employment tax. That's a conversation for a qualified tax preparer who can model whether the strategies and savings outweigh the added cost and complexity for your unique situation. Businesses at high volume consulting levels benefit most.

For most people reading this article, a simple sole proprietor setup works. Focus on clean records, timely payments, and a good tax preparer when things get complex.
