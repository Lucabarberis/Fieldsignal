## Understand How Expert Networks Operate (So You Can Position Yourself)

Understanding how expert networks operate helps you position yourself as the exact match recruiters want. Here's the expert network industry model, explained in plain terms.

**Core workflow:**

1. A client submits a project brief, for example, "US hospital adoption of robotic medical devices, 2022 to 2025."

2. The network does custom recruitment via LinkedIn and internal databases of vetted professionals.

3. Matched experts complete screeners (short questionnaires about role, dates, exposure).

4. The client picks an expert and schedules the call.

Recruiters search for very specific patterns: company names, titles, product lines, customer segments, and recent dates. "Former director of orthopedic implants in US hospitals, 2021 to 2024" beats "healthcare executive with 20 years experience" every time.

Most networks specialize by region or sector. Third Bridge is strong with global public market investors. Some boutiques focus on medical devices, software, or [biotechnology](https://www.nextyn.com/post/what-is-an-expert-network-complete-guide-2026?utm_source=openai). Expert network transcript libraries are used by public market investors for faster due diligence.

Beyond calls, some networks run expert surveys, panels, and longer consulting projects. They can conduct qualitative and quantitative market research and help identify market trends and competitive intelligence. But for gig income, the main opportunity is 30 to 60 minute expert calls. Major expert network platforms facilitate these paid short-term consultations, and clients can quickly gather diverse perspectives from various experts.

Expert networks often serve the financial industry and consulting firms, helping them refine strategies and identify trends faster. They utilize various business models for client engagement.

**Compliance basics:** You can't share MNPI (material non-public information), must respect confidentiality obligations, and typically sign a network-specific code of conduct before any calls.

## Make Your LinkedIn "Expert-Network Ready"

LinkedIn is a primary tool for finding new experts. Almost all custom recruitment starts there, not with CVs. Professionals should optimize their profiles to attract recruiters who are scanning for exact matches.

**Headline:** Replace "Experienced Healthcare Executive" with something like "Former VP of Sales, US Hospitals, Robotic Surgery & Capital Medical Devices (2015 to 2024)." Include function, sector, geography, and product keywords.

**Experience section:** Detail each role with exact employer names, titles, years (e.g., "2018 to 2023"), product categories, customer types, and regions. A strong profile includes biography and work history that screeners can copy directly.

**Role bullets that mirror expert calls:**

* "Set pricing strategy for orthopedic implants in US and EU"

* "Led due diligence on acquisition of a SaaS competitor in 2022"

* "Managed vendor selection for cloud infrastructure across EMEA"

**Keywords to insert:** "medical devices," "payer contracting," "B2B SaaS churn," "channel partners," "China automotive supply chain," "former executives" of a specific company. These should appear in your headline, About, and role descriptions.

**Contact info:** Include a direct email address in the Contact section and optionally in the About summary. Many recruiters still send direct emails instead of InMail.

**Open to work:** Turn on "Open to consulting" and list advisory services. This signals that short expert calls and project work are welcome. Creating this signal takes five minutes and makes you visible to technology tools that sourcers use.

## Write Screener Responses That Actually Get You Picked

This is where most experts lose opportunities. Engaging promptly increases your chances within expert networks.

**What a typical screener looks like:** 3 to 10 short questions about your role, time period, exposure to a product or company, and your ability to speak without breaching confidential or MNPI restrictions.

**How to respond well:**

* Mirror the client request. If a project asks about "US outpatient surgery centers adopting XYZ medical devices, 2020 to 2024," explicitly mention this time window and setting.

* Lead with recency over prestige. "Director of Procurement, US ASC chain, 2021 to 2024" matters more than a big-name employer from a decade ago.

* Include 1 to 2 concrete examples: "Responsible for selecting and negotiating contracts with major device vendors for 75+ ambulatory surgery centers" instead of vague "involved in purchasing."

* Confirm compliance: "I can speak directly to my past experience from 2020 to 2023 and will not share any current confidential information or MNPI."

**Speed matters.** Many projects fill within a few hours. Check email and LinkedIn several times a day. Aim to complete screeners within 30 to 60 minutes of receipt.

**Common mistakes:** Copying your CV verbatim, ignoring the specific company or product mentioned, exaggerating responsibilities, or leaving dates and volumes ambiguous.

## Optimize Your Expert Calls So You Get Rehired

Strong call performance leads to repeat invites. Here's what success looks like from the client's side.

Most expert calls last 45 to 60 minutes: brief intro and compliance reminder, client questions, clarifying follow-ups, and wrap-up. No slides unless pre-agreed.

**On the call:**

* Be exactly on time. Quiet environment. Good audio. Schedule it properly and don't rush.

* Start with a clear 2 to 3 minute overview of your relevant background, mapped to the project brief.

* Use specific, quantifiable statements: "We paid roughly $X to $Y per unit in 2023" or "We evaluated 3 vendors and selected one based on reliability and service."

* Stay inside compliance boundaries. Discuss only past roles. Avoid disclosing future guidance or MNPI. Decline anything too sensitive.

* Ask 1 to 2 clarifying questions early: "Are you more focused on US large IDNs or independent hospitals?"

Ending a few minutes early with a clear summary of 3 to 4 key takeaways often leaves a stronger impression than trying to fill every second. Clients can earn $200 to $500 per hour as experts, but repeat invites depend on quality, not just credentials. High feedback scores lead directly to more calls from the same network.

## Set Your Rates, Availability, and Boundaries

Treat expert network work as a real consulting gig with clear hourly rates, scheduling rules, and ethical limits.

**Typical rate bands:**

| Experience Level                     | Hourly Rate  |
|--------------------------------------|--------------|
| Mid-level manager, niche expertise   | $150 to $250 |
| Senior director / VP at known brand  | $300 to $500 |
| C level executives, rare specialties | $500+        |
[ExpertStackHub data](https://expertstackhub.ai/benchmarks/rates?utm_source=openai) shows the median across platforms at roughly $240/hour, with legal, cybersecurity, and finance verticals well above that.

Start slightly above your floor. Networks negotiate downward, rarely upward. Block 2 to 4 consistent time windows per week so schedulers can route calls without long back-and-forth.

Be strict about cancellations. Only cancel for real emergencies, give maximum notice. Patterns of rescheduling get you blacklisted by certain decision makers and clients.

Track hours, earnings, and networks each month. Invest a few minutes in a spreadsheet to see which expert networks bring the best opportunities for your niche.

Set firm boundaries on topics you won't discuss, including current confidential projects, ongoing negotiations, or any MNPI about public companies.
