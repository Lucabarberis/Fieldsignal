# Tax Due Diligence: A Buyer's Field Guide

Tax due diligence tells you what tax liabilities you're buying, what tax attributes you can use, and how to structure the deal so you don't overpay. This is your practical guide to running the process from LOI through close.

## Tax due diligence in one page: what you actually need to know

Tax due diligence quantifies tax risk, shapes deal structure, and prevents surprises after closing. It's not a compliance exercise. It's a pricing tool. Every material finding either changes the purchase price, changes the transaction's tax structure, or shows up as an indemnity in the SPA. Proactive tax due diligence prevents surprises that can stall or kill a deal.

In 2024 and 2025 deals, buyers focus on three things:

1. Undisclosed tax liabilities, especially state and local obligations, unfiled returns, and payroll or sales tax gaps.

2. Missed tax attributes like net operating losses, R&D tax credits, and foreign tax credits, plus whether limitations like IRC §382 restrict them.

3. How the deal structure (asset vs stock, elections like §338(h)(10)) interacts with federal income tax rules to affect depreciation, amortization, and step-ups.

Here's a concrete example. In a 2023 stock acquisition of a SaaS company with revenues across 15 states, diligence uncovered $5 to $10 million in unpaid state sales tax. The target company's economic nexus thresholds had been exceeded in multiple states post-Wayfair, but no registration or remittance had occurred. That finding led to a purchase price reduction, a larger escrow, and a requirement for a voluntary disclosure agreement before close.

Tax due diligence identifies potential tax liabilities and opportunities. It connects directly to valuation models, SPA terms (representations, warranties, indemnities), and post-close integration. If you treat it as paperwork, you'll leave holes in value.

## What is tax due diligence in M&A?

Tax due diligence is a targeted review of an acquisition target's past tax history, current tax compliance, tax attributes, and prospective tax liabilities within a transaction. It requires defining scope based on the business type and transaction structure. Tax due diligence has three main components: information gathering, interviews, and analysis.

Core objectives:

* Identify unpaid or underpaid taxes (federal, state, local, employment, indirect) and quantify potential tax exposure.

* Evaluate aggressive positions: transfer pricing, employee classification, nexus determinations, R&D credit claims. Tax due diligence evaluates the aggressiveness of a target company's tax strategies.

* Confirm usable tax attributes, including NOLs, tax credits, and carryforwards, and assess whether limitations apply.

* Model tax implications under different deal structures (asset vs stock, relevant elections).

* Effective tax due diligence involves a systematic review of tax compliance and liabilities, covering federal, state, local, and where applicable, international tax laws.

Tax due diligence can uncover tax-efficient opportunities like net operating losses and tax credits. It also helps identify contingent liabilities from ongoing audits or disputes with tax authorities.

One important distinction: tax diligence vs financial diligence. Financial due diligence gives you revenues, EBITDA, working capital (we cover the broader sequence in our [step-by-step due diligence process for buyers](/blog/the-due-diligence-process-step-by-step-for-buyers)). Diligence tax work refines what's in the financial statements. For example, a reported expense might be partially disallowed under tax law, or the financials might omit a liability that's material from a tax perspective. Tax findings often lead to adjustments of normalized earnings.

Both buyers and sellers run tax diligence, but the emphasis differs. Buyers focus on exposure and protection. Sellers focus on cleaning tax issues before buyers price them in. [Sell-side tax diligence](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2023/aug/m-a-transactions-the-value-of-sell-side-tax-diligence/) can reduce surprises and speed negotiations.

### How tax due diligence affects valuation and deal structure

Every material tax finding will either change price, change structure, or appear in the SPA as indemnities and covenants. Tax due diligence findings influence final deal structure. Tax due diligence can uncover risks affecting transaction pricing.

Common valuation impacts:

* Purchase price reductions for quantified exposures. In one report, [23% of deals in 2024 had price renegotiations](https://www.phoenixstrategy.group/blog/state-tax-due-diligence-in-manda-deals) due to state tax risks, with adjustments between 15 and 30% of the exposure amount.

* Escrow and holdback sizing tied to identified potential liabilities. Buyers may require indemnification clauses for tax liabilities.

* Specific indemnity baskets for known tax issues.

* Adjustments to net working capital or closing month income to reflect straddle period taxes.

Tax due diligence helps in structuring deals to maximize tax efficiency. It can influence whether a deal is structured as an asset purchase or a stock purchase. Stock purchases may need additional protections through warranties. Asset purchases require specific allocation strategies for tax treatment.

Tax due diligence evaluates a target's ability to use valuable tax assets post-acquisition. In a 2021 PE roll-up, bidders computed NOL limitations across tuck-in businesses, acquired companies with unused R&D and foreign credits, then consolidated to maximize usage and support a higher bid. In a 2022 asset deal for a manufacturing business, sales tax nexus findings in multiple states drove the buyer to restructure from stock to asset to limit successor liability.


## The tax due diligence checklist: what to actually review

A diligence checklist reduces unknown unknowns, but it must flex to deal size, industry, and jurisdictions. One size doesn't fit all. For a workstream-level view that sits above tax, see our [50-item M&A due diligence checklist](/blog/m-a-due-diligence-50-item-checklist-across-every-workstream). Here's what a practical due diligence checklist covers for a US-centric deal.

Core documents:

* Last 3 to 5 years of federal income tax returns. Information gathering includes analyzing tax returns from the past three years. Tax due diligence involves reviewing tax returns from the past three years.

* All state and local income, franchise, and sales/use tax filings. Tax due diligence checklists include reviewing federal and state tax returns.

* Checklists should cover payroll tax records and VAT records where relevant.

* Property tax bills and assessments.

* Sales and use tax returns by state and by product or service.

Organization and structure:

* Entity charts, legal entity lists, intercompany agreements.

* Transfer pricing documentation for related-party transactions.

* Tax sharing or allocation agreements, any internal or external tax opinions.

Tax attributes:

* Detailed NOL schedules and carryforwards.

* Tax credit carryforwards: R&D, foreign tax credits, energy credits under recent US legislation.

* IRC §382 analyses and other limitation assessments.

Controversies:

* A comprehensive checklist includes tax compliance certificates and audit history. IRS and state audit history since at least 2018.

* Correspondence with tax authorities, settlement agreements, pending assessments.

Employment and withholding:

* Employee vs independent contractors classification.

* Payroll tax deposits, W-2 and 1099 reconciliations.

* Benefits plans, deferred compensation issues.

Common checklist items include tax provisions in financial statements, accounting methods documentation, and indirect tax obligations. Tax due diligence includes creating an information request list and conducting management interviews. Ask for X, verify Y, demand documentation, run models.

### How to use a due diligence checklist without getting buried

Junior deal teams often treat the diligence checklist as a box-ticking exercise. That's backwards. It's a prioritization tool. Stage it.

* Phase 1 (at LOI or right after): federal and key state returns, major tax attributes, nexus exposure, unfiled returns, audit history. Enough to price exposure roughly and decide whether to proceed.

* Phase 2 (full data room access): transfer pricing, fringe benefits, detailed schedules, correspondence with tax authorities, local business taxes.

* Phase 3 (before signing): finalize indemnities, define escrow, deal structure elections, ensure discovered issues are addressed (e.g. via voluntary disclosure).

Align checklist items with your investment thesis. If you're buying a SaaS business with digital customers across many states, sales/use tax nexus is high priority. If manufacturing with international supply chain, transfer pricing and customs duties matter more.

Connect checklist outputs to a tracker that feeds directly into SPA schedules, valuation model tabs, and the internal investment memo. Use expert calls via FieldSignal to sanity-check unclear items, like whether a particular state is actively pursuing marketplace facilitator rules or digital services taxes.

## Inside the diligence process: how tax advisors actually work

The tax due diligence process follows three stages: information gathering, management Q&A, and quantitative analysis. About 60% of mergers involve third-party advisors for tax due diligence. External teams enhance tax due diligence efficiency and accuracy.

Most PE/VC buyers use CPA firms or law firms for formal diligence services. Smaller funds sometimes rely on boutique M&A tax shops. Professional guidance helps identify tax savings opportunities during transactions. Collaboration with tax advisors ensures a thorough risk assessment across the company's tax obligations.

Tax due diligence typically spans one to three months for most deals. Tax due diligence can take up to six months for complex, multi-jurisdictional structures. The full range is one to six months depending on the target's complexity, number of jurisdictions, and data quality.


### Stage 1: Information request list and data room review

The diligence requirements start with a tailored information request list, anchored in the generic checklist but customized for the deal structure and jurisdictions.

Priority requests:

* Federal income tax returns for the last 3 to 5 years.

* State returns in all states where the target has customers, employees, or operations.

* Sales and use tax returns by state.

* Payroll and employment tax filings.

* Transfer pricing studies and intercompany agreements.

* Any tax opinions issued since 2019.

* Export, import, or customs documents if applicable.

Tax due diligence requires auditing financial statements and assessing cash flow impacts from tax risks. The associate should track which documents arrive on time and flag gaps early so they don't stall later phases. Link each request to a risk category ("federal controversy," "indirect tax," "cross-border") to keep the data room organized.

Where external insight from former auditors or ex-tax authority staff, sourced via FieldSignal, can clarify common local practices versus strict statutory rules, bring those calls into this phase.

### Stage 2: Management interviews and follow-up

Once documents are reviewed, the tax department or outside advisors run focused interviews with the CFO, head of tax, or controller. Management interviews clarify questions from document reviews during due diligence.

Typical topics:

* What tax positions does the company consider aggressive?

* What internal controls exist for nexus reviews?

* How recently did the company conduct a sales tax nexus study?

* What incentives or credits were claimed and on what basis?

* How does the company classify workers?

* Any informal or voluntary disclosure agreements with states?

Probe around 2020 to 2022 relief measures: CARES Act payroll deferrals, Employee Retention Credit claims, loss carrybacks. These can create later-year exposures if misapplied.

Use expert calls with ex-employees or former Big 4 advisors via FieldSignal to cross-check management's narrative when something feels too clean, especially when the target claims zero state exposure but financials show sales activity in states with [economic nexus thresholds](https://conseroglobal.com/resources/due-diligence-readiness-navigating-state-and-local-tax-complexities-in-ma-deals/).

### Stage 3: Quantification, memos, and deal recommendations

The final diligence work product converts qualitative due diligence findings into numbers, ranges, and clear recommendations. Quantitative analysis assesses potential tax exposure and compliance issues.

Expected outputs:

* A quantified tax exposure schedule by jurisdiction with best case and worst case ranges.

* A summary of usable tax attributes (NOLs, credits) with limitation analysis.

* Recommended SPA protections: escrows, indemnities, specific covenants. Buyers may ask for indemnification if tax liabilities are found.

Ensure tax findings flow into the model: separate tabs for permanent vs temporary differences, and for one-off settlement costs. Tax due diligence helps buyers understand potential tax exposure and determine what adjustments are justified.

Prepare an internal memo section summarizing "red, amber, green" issues. Red means walk-away or big price knock. Amber means manageable via insurance, indemnity, or escrow. Green means low risk. This memo feeds directly into the investment committee deck with clear metrics, possible price adjustments, and structure trade-offs.

## Key tax risk areas you can't afford to miss

Most bad tax surprises fall into a few predictable buckets. Tax due diligence focuses on high-risk areas like sales tax and international reporting. Here's what to screen before senior advisors get involved.

* Federal income tax: aggressive R&D credits without proper backup, capitalization vs expensing disputes under Sec. 174 changes, transfer pricing with related parties, NOL limitations under §382 after ownership changes. Incorrectly deducted expenses can increase taxable income significantly, creating unexpected tax deductions reversals.

* Indirect and employment taxes: sales/use tax nexus in the post-Wayfair environment, worker misclassification (employees treated as independent contractors), payroll tax underpayments.

* International: withholding tax failures on dividends, interest, or royalties. Controlled foreign corporation rules, local [VAT/GST obligations](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-zone3/us/en/docs/services/tax/2026/us-tax-ma-tax-talk-indirect-tax-considerations.pdf), and emerging rules like Pillar Two global minimum tax.

* SALT sleeper risks: gross receipts taxes in Ohio or Washington, property tax reassessments after ownership changes, unclaimed property obligations, franchise taxes. Complex tax exposures, such as state and local tax compliance, need thorough investigation during diligence.

* Controversies: treat any unresolved audits since 2018 as a separate risk item with specific quantification. Tax due diligence uncovers potential back taxes, penalties, and interest that could become the buyer's burden. Buyers may inherit tax liabilities from the target company.

Tax due diligence can uncover potential tax liabilities that aren't visible in the financial statements or management presentations.

### Deal breakers vs "fixable" tax issues

Not every tax finding is a reason to walk. Some are routine adjustments. Others are true deal breakers. Over 60% of deals fail due to poor due diligence, so getting this categorization right matters.

Potential deal breakers:

* Intentional fraud or misstatement.

* Systemic non-filing in multiple major jurisdictions with substantial revenue.

* Abusive schemes already under tax authority scrutiny or criminal investigation.

Manageable issues:

* Late filings with quantifiable penalties.

* Documentation gaps on credits that can be remediated.

* Narrow nexus disputes that can be settled post-close via voluntary disclosure.

* Missing structuring elections that remain available.

Categorize findings as "price issue" (needs reduction), "structure issue" (may require change in deal architecture), or "protections issue" (needs SPA clauses, indemnification, holdback). Use 2019 to 2024 regulatory trends to calibrate what's manageable. States are increasingly aggressive on economic nexus enforcement, making what looked theoretical five years ago very real today.

## Technology, data rooms, and practical workflow tips

Tax due diligence lives in data rooms and spreadsheets. Workflow choices matter as much as technical analysis. Good structure saves days.

* Separate data room folders by jurisdiction (federal, each state) and tax type (income, indirect, employment). Clear naming: returns, audits, correspondence.

* Maintain shared trackers: a diligence checklist sheet updated as documents arrive, an issue list with priority and risk category, model spreadsheets with separate tabs for exposure by risk and attributes by jurisdiction.

* Integrate expert call outputs: upload transcripts, tag them to specific issues, summarize actionable points in the tax memo. Don't bury them.

* Document security: limit data room access for outside advisors, maintain NDAs, retain working papers for the period audit exposure may run.


### Common pitfalls in tax due diligence workflow

Watch out for these process failures:

* Late involvement of tax advisors, coming in after financial due diligence is nearly done. This leads to insufficient modeling.

* Incomplete or generic information request lists that don't adapt to industry or jurisdictional specifics.

* Failure to connect tax issues to valuation model assumptions, like leaving in NOLs assumed usable that are actually limited.

* Overlooking SALT and indirect taxes, especially for tech, SaaS, and e-commerce deals after the 2018 Wayfair decision.

* Over-relying on tax software outputs without human review. Software mapping errors and misclassification happen regularly.

* No escalation path if key documents aren't delivered. Missing returns or opinions should prompt senior escalation, price knock, or walk.

Diligence requires clear deadlines and accountability to keep successful transactions on track throughout the transaction process.
