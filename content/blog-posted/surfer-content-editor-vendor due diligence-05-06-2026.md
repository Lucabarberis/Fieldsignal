## What Is Vendor Due Diligence? (Buyer and Seller Perspective)

Vendor due diligence is the structured assessment of a third party’s financial, operational, cybersecurity, legal, and compliance risk before and during a vendor relationship. Vendor due diligence includes risk assessment and information gathering.

In TPRM, vendor due diligence means ongoing supplier risk work. In M&A, Vendor Due Diligence often means a sell-side report commissioned by the seller, common in Europe and the UK mid-market since around 2010. Bidders need to sign reliance letters to trust the vendor due diligence report.

Regulators raised expectations after 2008 and again after 2020. OCC Bulletin 2023-17 sets a lifecycle model for third party risk, including planning, due diligence, contracting, ongoing monitoring, and termination. Vendor risk is now legal risk, not just procurement preference.

VDD sits beside commercial due diligence, technical due diligence, and regulatory compliance reviews. The vendor due diligence process asks: what could this vendor break, expose, delay, misprice, or hide?

Not all vendors need the same review. A cloud host, payroll processor, freelance designer, and payments processor don't carry the same vendor's risk profile. Use a risk based approach aligned to the organization's risk appetite.

## Core Risk Areas to Cover in Vendor Due Diligence

Vendor risk assessments include financial, operational, and cybersecurity evaluations. A rigorous vendor assessment categorizes risk levels, financial stability, and compliance controls.

* Financial risk: review credit scores, tax returns, balance sheets, audited statements, cash flow, solvency, pricing history, and customer concentration. Financial stability assessments include reviewing credit scores tax returns and balance sheets. Regular updates of financial data are essential due to quick obsolescence in relevance.

* Operational risk: assess vendor's services, SLAs, delivery model, subcontractors, business continuity, and disaster recovery. Business Continuity Plans (BCP) ensure operational workflows continue during disruptions. Service Level Agreements (SLAs) are crucial for defining performance metrics and penalties for vendors.

* Cybersecurity and data protection: review SOC 2, ISO 27001, encryption, incident response, access controls, privilege misuse controls, vulnerability management, and security practices. Vendor due diligence helps identify potential cybersecurity threats, data breaches, cybersecurity breaches, and security breaches.

* Regulatory compliance: vendor assessments must align with the specific regulatory requirements of each industry. Data privacy compliance may require adherence to GDPR CCPA or HIPAA regulations, plus GLBA, FISMA, or other relevant regulations.

* Legal, political, and reputational risk: vendor background checks should include legal registration and structure reputation and operational history. Check sanctions, law enforcement lists, politically exposed persons, ownership, customer complaints, ongoing or past lawsuits, litigation history, compliance violations, regulatory violations, and regulatory risks.

* Employee practices and culture: review hiring, background checks, cybersecurity training, offboarding, whistleblower channels, unethical behavior reporting, and key personnel turnover.

## Building a Practical Vendor Due Diligence Checklist

Your due diligence checklist should match risk tiers: general vendors, sensitive data vendors, high risk vendors, and strategic or critical vendors. Critical vendors require the highest level of due diligence.

Request these document categories:

* Corporate information, legal entity, ownership, governance practices, and vendor's organization chart

* Financial statements, tax documents, debt, insurance, and updated financial health data

* Vendor's internal policies for security, privacy, access, HR, and incident response

* Certifications, audit reports, compliance evidence, and regulatory history

* Customer references from the last 24 months

* Subcontractor list and fourth-party controls

Ask precise questions. “Describe your incident notification timeline and escalation thresholds” beats “Describe your security.”

Standardize scoring so you can compare vendors side by side. Automate vendor due diligence processes to improve efficiency, but keep human review for judgment calls.

Over two-thirds of businesses rely on manual processes for vendor risk management, and many organizations struggle to fully understand vendor risks due to fragmented data. Vendor assessments often can't keep pace with timely onboarding needs, so keep the diligence checklist short enough to use.

## Risk-Based Tiers and Ongoing Monitoring

Risk evaluation must be continuous. Continuous monitoring helps detect significant changes in vendor risk, including layoffs, M&A, cyber breaches, price spikes, or regulatory enforcement.

Establish risk tolerance thresholds for vendor selection processes, then classify vendors as low, medium, high risk, or critical. Critical vendors should be reviewed at least annually for risks. Vendor due diligence reviews should occur at least annually for high-risk vendors, and you should review vendor financial health at least annually for high-risk vendors.

Suggested cadence:

| Tier     | Review cadence                           |
|----------|------------------------------------------|
| Critical | Annual review plus continuous monitoring |
| High     | Every 12 to 18 months                    |
| Medium   | Every 2 years                            |
| Low      | Every 3 years or renewal                 |
Continuous monitoring includes periodic reviews of vendor security protocols, security ratings, sanctions alerts, financial credit changes, SLA misses, and customer complaints. Regular monitoring ensures vendors meet company standards throughout the relationship and supports continuous compliance.

### Operationalizing Continuous Monitoring

To build a working program by Q4 2025:

1. Inventory all vendors and record data access, systems touched, and regulated activities.

2. Assign risk tiers and define metrics for each tier.

3. Track security ratings, SLA performance, credit score changes, complaint volume, and vendor's activity.

4. Map who owns each existing vendor relationship.

5. Use a workflow: detect issue, review internally within set business days, contact vendor, document remediation, escalate if unresolved.

6. Test the process annually so incident playbooks are real.

Ongoing monitoring is essential after vendor onboarding. Continuously monitor vendor relationships for changing risk profiles.

## Vendor Due Diligence in the Deal Room: Sell-Side and Buy-Side

In deals, vendor due diligence tests dependencies that can affect valuation. A PE associate might assess whether a SaaS target depends on one cloud provider, one outsourced dev shop, or one KYC vendor.

Sell-side VDD packs help potential buyers move faster during the sales process. Typical contents include vendor list with tiers, recent cyber incidents, responses, regulatory compliance summary, key contracts, change-of-control clauses, exit rights, and subcontractors.

Prepare files 3 to 6 months before launch, then refresh them during negotiations. Dynamic data refreshing during negotiations maintains report accuracy for buyers.

Clean files reduce hidden risks and hidden liabilities. Identifying hidden liabilities allows better negotiation terms or avoidance of hazardous partnerships. Establishing clear exit strategies in contracts protects interests and prevents excessive fees. Independent audits can uncover hidden costs during the vendor separation process.

## Step-by-Step Process: How to Conduct Vendor Due Diligence in 30–60 Days

1. Week 1 to 2, scope risk. Conduct vendor due diligence before onboarding new vendors. Map sensitive data, revenue exposure, regulated work, and critical functions.

2. Week 2 to 3, gather information. Use a standardized process, questionnaires, filings, sanctions checks, security ratings, and document requests.

3. Week 3 to 4, score risk. Rate financial, operational, cyber, legal, and regulatory categories as low, medium, or high. Tie the result to risk management protocols and the organization's risk appetite.

4. Week 4 to 5, decide and document. Write a recommendation, note accepted risks, and store all artifacts.

5. Week 5 to 8, contract controls. Add audit rights, SLAs, breach notice timelines, security obligations, termination rights, and remediation conditions.

6. Ongoing, monitor. Set review dates, alerts, and re-diligence triggers before onboarding closes.

### Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

* Treating all vendors the same. Fix it with risk tiers.

* Requesting documents no one reads. Fix it by linking each request to a decision.

* Ignoring fourth-party risk. Fix it by asking for subcontractor oversight.

* Skipping employee practices. Fix it by reviewing training, offboarding, and access control.

* Missing scope creep. Fix it by re-reviewing when vendor's services expand.

* Leaving legal late. Fix it by involving legal, security, and business owners early.

* Overbuilding policy. Fix it by trimming the template annually.

A common pattern: a small fintech depends on one KYC provider, skips contingency planning, then scrambles when the provider faces a 2023 enforcement action. Adequate due diligence would have flagged concentration risk and required a backup plan.

### How to Decide What’s “Enough” Diligence for Your Situation

Enough means defendable to your IC, board, customers, and regulators.

* Series A founder choosing a payments partner: basic checklist, security review, 1 to 2 expert calls.

* PE associate reviewing a software deal: full checklist, contract review, cyber review, several customer or former employee calls.

* Corporate M&A analyst reviewing a bolt-on: vendor inventory, material contract review, risk assessments, and selective on site visits.

* Low-risk supplier: basic corporate checks and renewal review.

Document why skipped steps weren't taken, such as no on site visits due to 2024 travel limits.
