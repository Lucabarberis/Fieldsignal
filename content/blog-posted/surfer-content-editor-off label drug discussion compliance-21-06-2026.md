## Understanding Approved Drug Labeling vs Off-Label Use

An approved drug is one where the FDA has granted licensure through a New Drug Application (NDA) or Biologics License Application (BLA). The approved labeling, codified in the United States Prescribing Information (USPI), defines the drug's indication, dose, route, population, and regimen.

* Label use means prescribing or administering a drug exactly as the approved labeling specifies: the right indication, population, dosage, and route.

* Off label use means a healthcare provider prescribes an fda approved drug for an indication, population, dose, route, or regimen not in the approved label. Off-label use occurs outside FDA-approved labeling.

* The FDA does not regulate the practice of medicine. Physicians may prescribe off label when no approved treatment exists for a patient's condition. That freedom belongs to the clinician, not the company that makes the drug.

* Pharmaceutical companies cannot promote off-label uses under U.S. law. The distinction is critical: a physician can prescribe what they determine is medically appropriate, but the manufacturer can't encourage or facilitate that prescribing for unapproved uses.

* FDA's regulations define intended uses based on [company statements](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/201.128), whether oral, written, or implied. If a company's actions suggest it intends the drug for an unapproved use, that triggers misbranding. Misbranding occurs if a drug's labeling describes unapproved uses.

* In 2001, 21% of patient-drug mentions were off-label. The practice is widespread and growing, which means your expert calls will surface it constantly.

* Expert calls often surface off label experiences from health care professionals. Your transcripts must clearly distinguish descriptive clinical practice from implied promotion or commercialization strategy. If they don't, you're creating a liability record.

## Regulatory Framework for Off-Label Communications

The U.S. regulatory framework for off label communications involves three agencies working in coordination. The FDA enforces misbranding rules. The DOJ brings False Claims Act cases when off label promotion leads to fraudulent government reimbursement. The OIG monitors compliance through Corporate Integrity Agreements.

* The core legal concept is "intended use" under [21 CFR § 201.128](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/201.128). Even if a drug is approved for one indication, statements by a company or its agents about unapproved uses can establish that the company intends the product for that unapproved use. That transforms otherwise lawful speech into unlawful off label promotion.

* [Misbranding under 21 U.S.C. § 352](https://legalclarity.org/what-is-21-usc-352-misbranded-drugs-and-devices/) is triggered when labeling or promotion is false, misleading, or omits required information. The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (the cosmetic act is referenced alongside drug regulation in this statute) prohibits introducing misbranded drugs into interstate commerce under 21 U.S.C. § 331.

* FDA defines clinical investigation as research involving human subjects, and any data from clinical trials used to support off label discussions should meet scientific standards including irb oversight where applicable.

* Unapproved use discussions become high risk when they show intended use for a new indication, especially when linked to sales tactics, targeting plans, or ROI projections.

Key dates that matter:

* **2014**: FDA issued guidance on distributing scientific publications on unapproved new uses.

* **2017**: FDA offered additional clarity on manufacturer communications with payors and formulary committees.

* **January 6, 2025**: FDA finalized the [SIUU guidance](https://www.raps.org/resource/fda-finalizes-guidance-on-off-label-communications.html) ("Scientific Information on Unapproved Uses"), allowing limited firm-initiated scientific exchange about off label uses. Scientific rationale for off-label use should rely on peer-reviewed literature. Communications must be truthful and non misleading, presenting strengths and limitations of the data. Pharmaceutical representatives can provide unedited articles on off-label uses upon request, but only under specific conditions.

SIUU does not protect commercial promotion or investment theses that pressure physicians toward unapproved use. It's enforcement policy, not a blanket safe harbor.

Regulators don't distinguish between a sales rep, an MSL, or an expert network call participant if the effect is off label marketing tied to commercial objectives. Enforcement typically couples FDA misbranding rules with False Claims Act theories brought by the government through whistleblower suits.

## Off-Label Drug Discussion Risks in Expert Network Research

Expert network calls are particularly sensitive because they're recorded, transcribed, and shared widely. Your due diligence team, board members, and investors all see these transcripts. If regulators subpoena them, they become evidence.

The setting emphasizes the importance of patient care and compliance with clinical guidelines in healthcare practices.](https://images.surferseo.art/4398173c-55b1-4609-9cb5-864aa40b89bb.png)

* Experts speculating on how a manufacturer "should market" an unapproved indication, or describing suggested sales messaging for unapproved uses, creates a record that looks like off label promotion support. That record can serve as evidence of intent.

* Asking experts to forecast revenue explicitly tied to an unapproved use, or to design sales playbooks around that unapproved use, crosses the line from research to promotion strategy.

* Investigators, investors, and strategists can ask about real-world off label use patterns. But questions must frame this as observational, not promotional or prescriptive. The difference between "how often do physicians use this off label" and "how should the company drive off label uptake" is the difference between research and a compliance violation.

* The numbers make the risk concrete: 80% of gabapentin prescriptions were for off-label uses. 46% of cardiac drugs prescribed off-label are not supported by strong evidence. Three-quarters of off-label prescribing lacks robust evidence. These statistics are fair game for your research. Using them to build a promotional case is not.

* In enforcement actions, 54% of off-label schemes targeted new disease subtypes, and 85% of off-label schemes target new diseases or dosages. That tells you where regulators focus their attention.

* In sensitive therapy areas like CNS, oncology, and pediatrics, off-label use and unapproved populations face heavy scrutiny from regulators and payers. If your call touches these areas, treat compliance as non-negotiable.

## Designing Compliant Question Guides for Off-Label Topics

Compliance starts with the interview guide. How you structure questions about unapproved uses of approved drugs determines your exposure. Standard medical ethics dictate full transparency in off-label discussions, and that principle extends to how you design your research.

Acceptable question framing:

* "In your clinical practice, what percentage of patients receive Drug X for Condition Y, which is not an fda approved use?"

* "What clinical guidelines do you follow when treating patients with Drug X off label?"

* "What clinical data or evidence-based rationale supports off-label prescriptions of Drug X in this population?"

* "What monitoring or safety signals have you observed with off label use of Drug X?"

* "Are patients informed of potential unknown risks with off-label use before receiving this treatment? How does informed consent work in practice?"

Unacceptable question framing:

* "How should Company X convince physicians to use Drug Y off label in Condition Z?"

* "Which messages would drive fastest off label uptake among oncologists?"

* "What promotional strategies would help Company X capture the off-label market for Condition Z?"

* "Can you draft positioning language for Drug Y's unapproved use?"

Additional controls for question guides:

* Include explicit instructions not to solicit confidential information, non-public trial data, or internal promotional strategies from experts who are current employees of manufacturers.

* Use neutral language when referencing unapproved uses. Repeatedly acknowledge that the indication is not fda approved to avoid implying endorsement.

* Thorough documentation of the off-label use discussed on each call is crucial. Records of what was asked, what was answered, and what disclaimers were given protect everyone involved. Documenting clinical rationale protects against malpractice claims for clinicians and protects your firm against promotion allegations.

> **Compliant vs Non-Compliant Off-Label Questions**
>
> ✅ "What real-world treatment patterns do you see for Drug X in unapproved Condition Y?"
>
> ❌ "How can we support off label marketing of Drug X for Condition Y?"

## Expert Screening and Conflict Controls

Not every healthcare provider, former employee, or consultant is appropriate for calls involving off label discussions. Vetting is necessary to protect both your firm and the expert.

* Screen for current employment at manufacturers of the drug under discussion. Experts who currently sit on promotional review committees, internal launch teams, or label expansion task forces should be excluded from off label calls where their knowledge could constitute MNPI.

* Screen for involvement in promotional planning. Anyone who participates in creating approved labeling, marketing collateral, or commercial strategy for the drug is high risk.

* Check for access to non-public clinical data from ongoing clinical trials. Sharing unpublished trial results on an expert call creates securities risk and regulatory exposure.

* Screen for obligations under existing Corporate Integrity Agreements. Some experts may be subject to monitoring requirements from prior enforcement actions.

* Consider the expert's professional role. MSLs (Medical Science Liaisons) are typically advanced-degree professionals like PharmDs or PhDs who communicate unbiased medical information to healthcare providers. MSLs engage with clinicians to discuss clinical trial results and must anchor discussions in FDA-approved labeling. MSLs must not promote off-label drug uses. MSLs can only respond to unsolicited off-label inquiries and must avoid proactive promotion of off-label uses. MSLs must document all unsolicited off-label inquiries, and all MSL interactions must be documented for compliance. MSLs should use only company-approved materials for responses. If your expert is a current or recent MSL, their compliance obligations are strict, and your questions must respect those boundaries.

## Real-World Enforcement: Off-Label Promotion Case Studies

These case studies show what regulators punish. Each one involves communication patterns that can easily occur on expert calls if compliance controls are missing.

![The image shows a judge's gavel resting on a wooden desk, accompanied by stacked legal documents, symbolizing the importance of compliance in legal and regulatory matters such as FDA-approved drugs and off-label use in patient care. This scene highlights the role of healthcare professionals in navigating clinical guidelines and the responsibilities involved in prescribing treatments.](https://images.surferseo.art/e177c148-f4d8-4d48-8c70-7460f6c18dac.png)

* **Neurontin (Gabapentin), 2004**: Warner-Lambert promoted Neurontin for off-label uses including bipolar disorder, migraines, and neuropathic pain. Pfizer's Neurontin case resulted in a [$430 million settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_off-label_promotion_pharmaceutical_settlements) covering civil and criminal claims. Regulators criticized the use of continuing medical education programs and research grants as cover for off label promotion. The lesson for expert calls: don't use "scientific exchange" as a wrapper for promotional strategy, and don't ask experts to present unapproved uses in ways that could feed commercial messaging.

* **Zyprexa (Olanzapine), 2009**: Eli Lilly settled for [$1.415 billion](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/eli-lilly-and-company-agrees-pay-1415-billion-resolve-allegations-label-promotion-zyprexa) over allegations it promoted Zyprexa for off-label uses including dementia, sleep disorders, and anxiety. The resolution included a Corporate Integrity Agreement requiring board-level compliance certification and disclosure of payments to physicians. The lesson: if your call transcript shows coordination between commercial and medical functions aimed at expanding use beyond the approved indication, you're replicating the exact pattern regulators target.

* **Risperdal (Risperidone), 2013**: Johnson & Johnson paid $2.2 billion for off-label marketing of Risperdal. The company promoted risperidone and other drugs for unapproved uses including dementia, anxiety, and anger management across multiple state and federal claims. The lesson: even widespread off label use by physicians doesn't make promotional activity legal. Your expert calls can document the practice, but they can't be used to design or support off label marketing campaigns.

* **Topamax, 2010**: J&J subsidiaries settled for approximately [$81 million](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/two-johnson-johnson-subsidiaries-pay-over-81-million-resolve-allegations-label-promotion). Topamax was approved for seizures, but the company promoted it for psychiatric uses through a "Doctor-for-a-Day" program where physicians were used to promote off-label uses during sales visits. The lesson: using physicians or experts as proxies for promotion is exactly what regulators look for, and it's exactly what can happen on poorly controlled expert calls.

Off-label promotion can lead to fines exceeding $7.9 billion when you aggregate major settlements across the industry. These aren't hypothetical risks.

## Best Practices for Clients Discussing Off-Label Use on Calls

**Do's:**

* Keep your focus on observational data. Ask what physicians actually do in practice, not what they should do or what a company should tell them to do.

* Use neutral wording. Say "unapproved use" or "use not included in approved labeling," not "opportunity" or "market expansion."

* Acknowledge when a use is not fda approved, every time it comes up. Don't assume the expert or listener knows the regulatory status.

* Frame your internal notes as market research or risk assessment. Patient care decisions belong to clinicians with the right knowledge and clinical guidelines, not to your investment memo.

* Ask about therapies and other drugs used for the same disease to understand the competitive treatment context without nudging toward any specific unapproved use.

* Reference only published, peer-reviewed research or data from completed clinical trials when discussing evidence for off label use. Three-quarters of off-label prescribing lacks robust evidence, so determine what evidence actually exists before drawing conclusions.

* Ask your legal or compliance team to review question guides when calls will clearly touch unapproved uses of higher-risk drugs, including those with black box warnings, REMS programs, or use in pediatric or CNS populations.

**Don'ts:**

* Don't ask experts to draft sales messaging, promotional copy, or positioning statements for unapproved uses.

* Don't request internal documents, launch plans, or marketing strategy from experts who are current or recent manufacturer employees.

* Don't connect call content to specific promotional campaigns for unapproved uses in your notes, memos, or investor decks.

* Don't ask experts to mention specific criteria for identifying prescribers who would be most receptive to off label adoption. That's targeting, not research.

* Don't ask for revenue projections explicitly tied to an unapproved indication without separating the research from any promotional intent.

* Don't present off label data in a way that's misleading or that makes false claims about efficacy or safety.

* Don't treat the call as a substitute for the company's own responsibility to conduct proper research before seeking a new indication from the FDA.
