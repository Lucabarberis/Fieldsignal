## Core Definitions: What Counts as a Conflict of Interest in Primary Research?

In the context of expert calls, surveys, and field interviews, a conflict of interest exists when an expert's financial interest or personal stake could reasonably affect, or appear to affect, the objectivity of the information they provide. This applies whether you're commissioning a single expert call or running a multi-week research project.

Financial interests include stocks, consulting fees, and patents related to research. A financial conflict of interest is specific: it means the expert has a monetary relationship, like a bonus tied to 2026 sales, an equity interest in a supplier, ongoing consulting fees, or remuneration received from a company you're studying, that creates an incentive to shade the truth.

In human subjects research, IRBs and public health service rules require disclosure of significant financial interest to protect research participants and research integrity. Under current regulations, the threshold for a significant financial interest is often $5,000 in combined remuneration and equity for publicly traded entities, or any ownership interest in non-publicly traded companies.

Undisclosed conflicts of interest can undermine scientific integrity and public trust. Even when no actual bias occurs, perceived conflicts still create reputational and deal risk for PE/VC, corporate development, and consulting buyers. If the expert looked clean on paper but later turns out to have held stock ownership in the target, your research results and conclusions get questioned retroactively.

## Common Conflict Scenarios in Expert and Custom Research

Most issues you'll face are repeat patterns. Recognizing them upfront lets you focus on managing conflicts instead of being surprised after the call is over.

Here are the scenarios that come up most often:

* Experts with equity interest or stock options in a target company who naturally overstate growth or downplay risks.

* Dependent children employed by a competitor, creating personal relationships that can influence how the expert frames competitive dynamics.

* Recent executives still on a retention package with accelerated vesting, whose personal gain depends on the company hitting near-term targets.

* A distributor whose commissions depend on a single OEM, or a hospital buyer who gets speaking fees from a pharmaceutical companies' sponsored advisory board.

* A principal investigator in human subjects research funded by a medical device company whose product they're studying.

* Current employees with access to confidential information or material nonpublic information, which is both a conflict of interest problem and a legal compliance red flag.

### Individual-Level Conflicts: Financial and Non-Financial

Individual conflicts center on the expert's own significant financial interest: consulting payments, royalties, stock ownership, or performance-based bonuses. Thresholds often used in research ethics include 5% or greater equity interest, or annual payments over $10,000, as concrete markers.

Non-financial conflicts include career advancement pressures. A senior product manager might overstate pipeline strength because their promotion depends on 2027 revenue. Conflicts of conscience arise from personal values conflicting with research, such as an individual researcher who personally champions a technology they're asked to evaluate objectively.

Personal relationships can create conflicts when evaluating products related to family members. If an expert's spouse works for a company in the research scope, that creates bias risk even without direct financial relationships.

Unintentional bias is a serious threat to research integrity. Even experts acting in good faith can produce bias resulting from these hidden incentives.

### Institutional and IRB-Level Conflicts

Institutional conflicts occur when financial interests affect research integrity at an organizational level, like universities or hospitals holding equity in companies sponsoring the research you're relying on.

IRBs and Conflict of Interest Review Boards are meant to identify, disclose, and manage financial conflicts for human subjects research. IRBs must manage conflicts of interest among their members, often requiring recusal of conflicted persons from oversight. Academic conflicts can bias peer review system processes when reviewers have ties to the work they're evaluating. Brown's Conflict of Interest Review Board reviews financial disclosures as one example of how institutions operationalize these checks.

The late 1990s and early 2000s gene therapy controversies forced US regulators and research ethics bodies to clarify institutional conflict policies. A university with a patent on a drug being evaluated by an academic in the trial had an institutional conflict that was not fully disclosed, leading to regulatory changes.

Why should buyers of primary research care? Institutional conflicts can affect how data is collected and reported long before it reaches your consultant, banker, or investment memo. If your deal thesis rests on published clinical research, check whether the research funding came from the company whose product was being studied.

## How Conflicts of Interest Skew Research Outcomes

Assume that any misaligned incentive will show up as bias in research design, data, or interpretation, even when experts believe they're objective.

Here's where conflicts interfere in the research process:

1. Research design and patient selection: choosing questions or populations that steer toward favorable answers. Industry-funded trials, for example, [used placebo comparators 78% of the time versus 49% in non-industry trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951352/), inflating positive findings.

2. Data collection: leading questions, selective prompting, or experts withholding relevant information.

3. Analysis and interpretation: cherry-picking favorable data, ignoring counterevidence.

4. Selective reporting: [industry-funded drug trials reported favorable outcomes 85.4% of the time versus 50% for government-funded trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3374868/). And 20% of researchers delay publication for personal advantage.

Financial conflicts can alter research outcomes significantly. Conflicts of interest can damage public trust in research when they're discovered after conclusions have already shaped decisions. Perceived impropriety can harm a researcher's reputation and, by extension, the credibility of all those who relied on their work.

### Impact on Investment, M&A, and Product Decisions

Biased primary research flows directly into DCF models, synergy estimates, churn assumptions, and product roadmap prioritization.

Examples:

* Mis-estimated 2028 market size because experts were still holding equity in a fast-growing vendor, inflating TAM projections by 15-30%.

* Understated customer churn risk because interviewees feared upsetting a supplier who paid them advisory fees.

* Leadership assessment calls where former executives with grievances overstated management dysfunction, creating conflicting interests in how you evaluate the target's team.

The cost of a missed conflict of interest check is measured in basis points of a deal, not in the research budget. An overestimate of growth driven by a few conflicted sources can inflate deal multiples enough to destroy returns.

## Regulatory and Ethics Frameworks You Should Know

These frameworks matter because regulators and universities wrote rules about conflicts for a reason, and those reasons apply to your own primary research process, even when your research activity isn't federally funded.

Federal regulations mandate disclosure of financial conflicts of interest. The two key US rules are:

| Regulation                                                                                                                          | Scope                                            | Key Requirement                                                                                                        |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| [42 CFR Part 50 Subpart F](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title42-vol1/pdf/CFR-2023-title42-vol1-part50-subpartF.pdf) | Public health service funded research            | Written interest policy, disclosure of significant financial interest including spouse and dependent children holdings |
| [45 CFR Part 94](https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/regulations-and-policy/guidance/financial-conflict-of-interest/index.html)                | Contractors and cooperative agreements under PHS | Comparable arrangements for managing and reporting financial conflicts                                                 |
PHS regulations promote objectivity in federally funded research and require bias-free research conduct. Investigators must report financial interests affecting research, and the federal government expects institutions to promote objectivity through management plans, recusal, or elimination of conflicting interests.

The national science foundation sets a $10,000 threshold for significant financial interests. NSF guidelines apply this threshold to federal grants and cooperative agreements. The national institutes of health use similar frameworks, and professional societies like the american medical colleges have issued their own guidance on research integrity.

Disclosure of conflicts is required for financial interests in research. This includes the informed consent process and informed consent form for clinical research, where research participants must be told about any financial support or conflicting interests that could affect clinical management or research protocols.

For buyers: a serious vendor for primary research should apply conflict of interest screening that aligns with these research ethics expectations, even when the work isn't covered by a grant application or federal grants.

## Practical Conflict Checks for Buyers of Primary Research

This is a process a junior associate or analyst can follow before commissioning any expert call, survey, or panel. Build it into your research plan for every research project.

1. Define high-risk roles: identify which expert profiles carry the most conflict risk (current employees, recent executives, board members, investors, paid advisors).

2. Collect detailed disclosures: require written disclosure of financial interest, equity interest, stock options, board seats, recent employment in the months preceding your project, and relationships involving spouse or dependent children. Ask about intellectual property rights, patents, and royalties.

3. Run internal name checks: search SEC filings, patent databases, LinkedIn, and news for undisclosed ties. Verify that the individual researcher's declarations match public records.

4. Review for financial conflicts: a project director or compliance lead should evaluate whether the disclosed interest is material to your research scope. Conflict of Interest Review Boards evaluate financial disclosures in academic settings; your team should apply a similar standard.

5. Decide: proceed, mitigate, or substitute: recusal involves stepping away from decisions that could be biased by conflicts. Apply that logic to expert selection. If the conflict is material, either limit the expert's scope or find a replacement.

Document all conflict checks in the research file. Deal teams, compliance, law firms advising on transactions, and outside counsel should be able to see you had a process.

### Red Flags That Should Trigger Extra Scrutiny

* Current employment at a public company you're evaluating.

* Insider access to unreleased research results or product data.

* Compensation tied directly to short-term pricing, volume, or revenue targets.

* Experts pushing a specific company or financial product with unusual enthusiasm, which can indicate undisclosed financial conflicts.

* Resistance to basic disclosure questions, or downplaying equity interest and financial relationships.

* An expert who's also serving as an expert witness in litigation related to the sector.

When multiple red flags appear together, either reject the expert or strictly limit the scope. No single call is worth the risk to your research integrity.

## Managing Conflicts: Accept, Mitigate, or Avoid?

Not all conflicts require rejection. Some are acceptable with disclosure, some need mitigation, and a minority require you to avoid using that expert or dataset entirely.

| Decision               | When to Use                                                                         | Example                                                                                                                                              |
|------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Accept with disclosure | Small residual interest, fully disclosed, doesn't touch core questions              | Former director with 0.5% vested equity commenting on market trends, not company-specific catalysts                                                  |
| Mitigate               | Moderate conflict that can be offset                                                | Balance conflicted expert with independent reviewers or unconflicted sources; limit topics on the call                                               |
| Avoid                  | Major stock ownership, current insider access, or financial support from the target | Active VP with unvested stock options commenting on 2027 revenue, or a researcher whose research funding comes entirely from the company under study |
Certain circumstances demand you eliminate conflicts entirely, particularly when the expert has access to confidential information or when such conflicts are tied to ongoing consulting arrangements worth significant amounts.

Managing conflicts of interest is part of good scientific practice and research integrity, the same way you control for sample bias or faulty survey instruments. The scientific community and research community treat conflict management as standard conduct, not optional.

### Documentation and Communication Inside Your Firm

Conflict decisions should be visible to investment committee members, partners, or clients who rely on your research. Don't bury them in personal notes.

For each expert or study, record:

* Declared financial conflicts and financial relationships

* How you assessed materiality (thresholds, interest requirements, project relevance)

* Steps taken to mitigate unintentional bias or bias resulting from the conflict

Clear documentation supports compliance reviews and future audits of your deal files. Train team members directly responsible for research to recognize and escalate conflict of interest questions. Professional ethics and scientific integrity require that such rights to review are maintained.

All those involved in the research process, from the project director to junior analysts, should understand that conflicts aren't just legal boilerplate. They affect whether your research results hold up under scrutiny.
