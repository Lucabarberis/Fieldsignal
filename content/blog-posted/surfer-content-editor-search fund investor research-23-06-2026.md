# Search Fund Investor Research: How LPs Vet Searchers

Search fund investor research is the single highest-leverage activity an LP performs before wiring capital. You're not betting on a sector or a fund strategy. You're betting on one person. Here's how serious LPs evaluate search fund entrepreneurs, what they look for at each stage, and where primary research fills the gaps that pitch decks can't cover.

## Why search fund investor research matters for LPs

LPs in search funds win or lose based on the searcher they back, not just the target company. The Stanford 2024 study reported a 35% IRR for search funds as an asset class. Search funds reported an internal rate of return of 35.1% in 2024. Those numbers are attractive, but they mask enormous variance between top and bottom performers. The difference almost always comes down to the operator.

Disciplined investor research focuses on the searcher's track record, sourcing method, and decision-making under uncertainty. This isn't a portfolio diversification play. It's a concentrated bet on one or two entrepreneurs who will acquire and run a single privately held company for years.

The diligence process looks a lot like early-stage private equity underwriting, but centered on a single operator. ## Overview of the search fund model and investor roles

A search fund is an investment vehicle where a search fund entrepreneur raises search capital to find and acquire a single small or medium-sized business, then runs it as CEO for years before exiting. Search funds allow access to entrepreneurship through acquisition as a unique asset class. Over 900 search funds have been launched worldwide since 1984, and over 700 of those originated in the US and Canada.

The traditional search fund model works like this:

* Raise approximately $300k to $600k in initial search capital from 10 to 20 investors

* Spend one to three years in the search phase identifying a suitable business

* Raise acquisition capital from the same investors (plus co-investors) to close the deal

* Search fund managers often serve as CEO post acquisition, running the acquired company for 4 to 7 years

* Exit via sale to a strategic buyer or recapitalization

Search funds sit between traditional private equity and independent sponsors, with far more concentrated operational risk. In 2023, nearly 100 search funds launched in the US and Canada. [Search capital raised increased from $5 million in 2010 to $75 million in 2023](https://www.searchfundmarket.com/en/search-fund-statistics), a record high. Total acquisition volume rose from $110 million to $880 million since 2010. Search funds are expanding in Europe, Latin America, and Asia.

Variations matter for diligence. A traditional search fund raises initial capital upfront. A self funded search means the searcher covers personal expenses during the search phase. Search fund accelerators provide mentorship, introductions, and sometimes committed capital. LPs vet each type differently.

Search fund investors include dedicated search funds, family offices, small PE firms, and experienced individual operators backing first-time CEOs. Search fund investors specialize in funding aspiring entrepreneurs. They provide capital to acquisition entrepreneurs and often provide mentorship alongside capital.

## Core investment questions LPs ask about a search fund entrepreneur

LPs evaluate the person first, then the search strategy, then the deal economics. Search funds are often led by entrepreneurs with MBAs or experienced mid-career professionals, but pedigree alone doesn't predict success.

Here are the key questions LPs use when doing search fund investor research:

1. Can this person source proprietary deals, or are they entirely broker-dependent?

2. Have they ever owned a P&L or led operations with real accountability?

3. Do they have sector or functional expertise in their target industry?

4. Will business owners actually sell to them? Do they have credibility as an acquirer?

5. Are they aligned with LPs on time horizon, equity split, and governance?

6. Do they show resilience and sound decision-making under uncertainty?

7. What does their sourcing and screening discipline look like in practice?

8. Can they walk away from a bad deal?

9. Are they comfortable with debt, bank covenants, and personal guarantees?

10. Is there evidence they can execute a transaction start to finish?

Search fund investors look for fragmented industries with growth potential through consolidation. They prioritize businesses with limited operational complexity and high barriers to entry. The ideal target company has a loyal customer base and a defensible market position. Investors prefer companies with high recurring revenue and strong EBITDA margins. Finding businesses with stable predictable cash flow is essential for search fund investors.

Investors typically sit on the board to provide strategic advice to the new CEO. Search fund investors act as mentors and board members to entrepreneurs. Investors typically contribute $25,000 to $2.5 million per deal.

Sophisticated LPs use external expert interviews to validate a searcher's self-described strengths, especially when they claim deep sector knowledge. A key factor for search funds is finding companies where the current owner is retiring, and LPs want to confirm the searcher understands how to approach those conversations.

## Assessing the search phase: strategy, sourcing, and focus

Most search funds fail or stall during the search phase. LPs scrutinize sourcing strategy intensely before wiring search capital. The search phase can last from one to three years, and searchers contact thousands of owners over 24 months to find targets.

LPs evaluate the search thesis on these criteria:

* Target EBITDA range: search funds typically target businesses with minimum $1 million EBITDA, often up to $5 million

* Business model: recurring revenue, stable cash flows, low customer concentration

* Owner profile: family owned businesses, founder-led companies, clear succession plan situations

* Geography: regions where the searcher has networks and understanding

Many experienced search funders now favor a focused thesis over an industry-agnostic approach. B2B services, mission-critical software, and technical facilities services are common focus areas. Search funds are interested in firms that do not require heavy capital expenditures.

LPs review the searcher's [pipeline-building plan](https://ctacquisitions.com/how-to-build-acquisition-pipeline-search-fund/) in concrete terms: weekly outreach volume, use of intermediaries versus proprietary sourcing, and CRM discipline. Search fund investors use search capital to find companies and acquisition capital to buy them.

## Evaluating acquisition phase discipline and deal judgment

LPs care less about the first deal a searcher shows them and more about the bad deals they walk away from. Search fund investors typically seek profitable established businesses.

Underwriting criteria LPs expect:

* Minimum EBITDA margins of 15% or higher

* Stable historical cash flow, not volatile or lumpy revenue

* Low customer concentration (no single customer above 20 to 30% of revenue)

* Low capex intensity

* Acquisition capital typically ranges from $500,000 to $5 million

* Investors usually provide 70 to 100% of the equity needed

LPs test a searcher's acquisition discipline with hypothetical deals. How do they respond to a SaaS business priced at 8x revenue? A cyclical industrial services company with thin margins? The ability to reject a deal is as important as the ability to find one.

LPs look closely at the searcher's comfort with debt structures, bank covenants, and personal guarantees in SBA-backed deals. Some LPs use external experts to sanity-check the searcher's planned value creation levers and identify blind spots in acquisition memos.

## Post acquisition operating plan and value creation expectations

LPs underwrite a relationship with a first-time CEO that typically lasts 4 to 7 years. They focus heavily on the 100-day and 3-year plans. Search fund entrepreneurs implement changes after 100 days of acquisition. Search funds focus on operational value creation after acquisition.

What LPs expect to see in a credible post acquisition plan:

* Customer retention initiatives and churn prevention

* Pricing discipline and benchmarking against comparable businesses

* Salesforce buildout with clear KPIs

* Basic financial controls: budgeting, monthly reporting, accounting infrastructure

There's a difference between a search fund backed by institutional investors and a self funded searcher relying on SBA debt and personal guarantees. Institutional LPs often demand formal governance, independent board seats, and regular reporting cadence. Self funded searchers may face more lender requirements but looser LP governance.

Search fund managers often work with a board of directors post acquisition. LPs evaluate the proposed board's mix: experienced search fund investors, industry operators, and functional experts in sales, finance, or HR.

## Comparing traditional search funds, self-funded search, and independent sponsors

LPs frequently choose between backing a traditional search fund, a self funded search entrepreneur, or an independent sponsor. Each model creates different risk and return profiles.

| Criteria           | Traditional Search Fund                                                    | Self Funded Search                                                    | Independent Sponsor                                           |
|--------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------|
| Capital structure  | Initial search capital raised upfront; acquisition capital committed early | Searcher covers own search costs; raises acquisition capital post-LOI | No committed search capital; equity raised deal by deal basis |
| Investor control   | Structured governance, board involvement, pro rata rights                  | Less LP influence pre-acquisition; governance builds post-close       | Variable; often transactional involvement                     |
| Searcher economics | Lower founder equity; less personal financial risk in search phase         | Higher founder equity; personal guarantees and SBA exposure           | Economics heavily tilted to searcher per deal                 |
| Typical deal size  | Enterprise value $10M to $30M                                              | Often smaller; limited capital resources constrain deal size          | Wide range depending on co-investor syndicate                 |
| Risk profile       | Concentrated on single operator; mitigated by structured governance        | High personal risk for searcher; less LP exposure in search phase     | High per-deal risk; portfolio diversification depends on LP   |
Search fund investors generally finance through initial search funding and later equity for acquisition. Traditional search funds usually raise search capital upfront with pro rata rights to provide acquisition capital. Many search funds in the independent sponsor category raise most equity portion post-LOI.

LPs weigh tradeoffs: higher concentration risk but stronger alignment with a single operator versus diversified exposure through private equity funds or fund-of-funds strategies. Investor research focuses on different questions for each model. For self funded searchers, it's personal balance sheet strength. For independent sponsors, it's co-investor quality and deal sourcing track record.

## How serious LPs run search fund investor research processes

Investor research is a repeatable process similar to early-stage private equity underwriting, compressed into a few weeks. Over 250 search fund partnerships have been backed by Endurance Search Partners, showing how institutional this has become.

Here's a 5-step process LPs follow:

1. **Initial triage of deck and CV.** Good decks include detailed track records, previous failures, concrete target parameters, and existing pipeline metrics. Aspiring MBA entrepreneurs from programs at Stanford University, Harvard Business School, or similar graduate school of business programs get attention, but LPs don't stop at pedigree.

2. **Reference checks.** Speak to prior employers, colleagues, and ideally former sellers or brokers. Ask for examples of decisions under pressure. Verify honesty and coachability.

3. **Thesis and sourcing review.** Request sample target lists, outreach plans, and pipeline metrics. Use expert calls to test sector assumptions about market fragmentation and acquisition availability.

4. **Simulated deal review.** Present hypothetical LOIs. Ask what the searcher would walk away from. Test their understanding of deal structuring, purchase price expectations, and financing terms.

5. **IC memo and terms negotiation.** The internal memo contains all major risks, mitigation steps, governance structure, reporting cadence, equity vesting, and exit mechanics.

Institutional investors often run backchannel checks with prior managers, professors, and co-founders. Common red flags: lack of focus, unrealistic multiples, overreliance on brokers, or no clear approach to building a management team post acquisition.

## Key metrics and outcomes: what LPs should track across their search fund portfolio

Disciplined LPs treat search funds as a repeatable asset class. They track funnel metrics at each stage, benchmarking against published academic sources.

Key metrics to monitor:

* Acquisition success rate: approximately 63 to 67% of funded searchers acquire within 36 months

* Aggregate returns: the [Stanford Graduate School of Business 2024 study](https://www.hormescapital.com/archivos/enlaces/2024-Search-Fund-Study-Selected-Observations_Stanford-6.pdf) reported 35.1% gross IRR and 4.5x return on invested capital

* Median enterprise value of acquisitions: approximately $14.4M in the US

* Deal multiples: 5.6x EV/EBITDA in US traditional search fund deals

* The average search fund has an investment horizon of four to seven years

* Search funds raised $75 million in capital in 2023

The International Search Fund Center at IESE and research from the Stanford Graduate School of Business provide vintage-year data on survival and default rates. Successful search funds deliver returns comparable to or exceeding traditional private equity. Many LPs use structured post-mortems on failed searches to refine searcher selection. Common failure causes include customer concentration risk (approximately 28% of deal failures), execution issues (24%), and overpayment or structure problems (18%).

LPs should benchmark their own results against these studies, then adjust underwriting criteria, sector focus, and board composition accordingly. Search funds tend to perform best when LPs are active, engaged board members rather than passive capital providers.
