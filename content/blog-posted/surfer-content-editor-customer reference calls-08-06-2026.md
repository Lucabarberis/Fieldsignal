# Customer Reference Calls for Due Diligence: A Buyer's Playbook

Customer reference calls are the fastest way to verify revenue quality, product stickiness, and competitive position before you wire funds. This playbook covers when to run them, who to talk to, what to ask, and how to turn raw call notes into deal decisions.

## Why customer reference calls matter in commercial due diligence

Customer reference calls involve conversations between an existing client and a prospective buyer of the company that serves them. They provide objective third-party validation to help prospects build confidence in a deal thesis.

* VCs, PE firms, growth equity, and corp dev teams use structured customer reference checks to pressure-test assumptions about retention, pricing power, and switching cost. These calls help inform purchasing decisions and aren't about collecting testimonials.

* Hearing real-world results establishes immediate credibility among prospects. Buyers often trust candid feedback from industry peers over sales teams. Customer advocacy can influence B2B purchasing decisions significantly.

* Reference calls accelerate deal velocity and reduce the sales cycle length. They help prospects overcome final objections regarding purchases, whether that's a $20M acquisition or a Series B investment.

* Customer reference calls sit between desk research and full market studies in cost and speed. They complement but don't replace a background check on founders or executives. A strong reference check can help mitigate hiring risks, and the same logic applies to "hiring" a company into your portfolio.

## What a customer reference call is (and how it differs from HR-style reference checks)

A customer reference call is a 30 to 60 minute structured interview with a current or former customer of the target company. The focus is product usage, renewal behavior, ROI, and switching intent.

* In the hiring process, reference checks verify a candidate's previous employment and skills. Employers often ask about a candidate's job performance, and many employers use them to assess a job candidate's fit within a team environment. Reference checks provide insights into a candidate's work ethic, can reveal discrepancies in a candidate's resume, identify inaccuracies in job applications, and reveal a candidate's commitment to professional development. A prospective employer or hiring manager might ask candidates to provide former managers' contact info, then check past performance, candidate's strengths, and candidate's dedication. These checks help a prospective employee get a job offer and help employers avoid negligent hiring. They help verify candidates' job performance and skills across their career.

* In deal work, none of that matters. You care about contract value, renewal history, decision criteria, and competitor comparisons, not whether a person showed up on time as a new employee at their last position.

* You'll combine "on-sheet" references provided by the company and "off-sheet" references sourced independently. This mirrors how rigorous founder reference checking works in VC, where investors call backchannel sources, not just the list the candidates supply. The reference checking process in commercial diligence is more structured than general questions about a potential hire's skills at a current employer in a different industry.

## When to run customer reference calls in your deal process

* A typical funnel: initial screen, early memo, IOI or term sheet, confirmatory due diligence, investment committee. Reference checks are typically conducted after interviews with management, just as you'd conduct reference checks after interviews for better insights on a job candidate.

* Do the first wave of customer reference calls once you're 50 to 70% confident in the deal. That's after LOI in M&A or after the partner meeting in VC. You have a thesis but can still walk away before a job offer equivalent gets made.

* Earlier calls make sense if the thesis hinges on a few lighthouse customers or a single enterprise cohort from 2021 to 2023. If those logos are the reason you're checking references, test them in week one.

* If the first round exposes concerns like churn pockets, pricing pushback, or weak onboarding, run a second targeted round. Focus on accounts matching your ICP, prior churners, or segments underrepresented in the first wave.

## Planning your customer reference call program

Effective customer reference calls require strategic planning. Before you dial anyone, build a plan.

* Create a one-page "learning agenda" with 5 to 7 core questions: retention drivers, churn triggers, competitive alternatives, pricing power, and user-level satisfaction. Asking the right questions upfront saves you from vague answers later.

* Decide scope. For a Series B SaaS check, plan 6 to 12 calls. For a PE platform buyout, 10 to 20. Mix segments (SMB, mid-market, enterprise), regions, and use cases to gain insights across the customer base.

* Distinguish company-supplied contacts (fast access, higher bias) from independently sourced contacts (slower, higher candor). You need both for conducting reference checks that produce balanced reference information.

* Prepare a concise intro script. Set expectations on confidentiality, timing, and that the call isn't a sales pitch or support ticket. Collect contact info for each reference in advance.

## Who to speak with: on-sheet vs off-sheet customer references

* On-sheet references are accounts the company suggests: happy customers, recent wins, marquee logos. Select satisfied customers for reference calls who match the target prospect's profile. The information provided is useful but biased toward positive feedback.

* Off-sheet references are customers and ex-customers you or a partner like FieldSignal source independently using expert networks, social data, or procurement contacts. A former employee of the target or a churned account often gives you the most honest signal.

* For a $10 to 50M ARR SaaS target, aim for 4 to 6 on-sheet and 6 to 10 off-sheet calls spread across cohorts signed between 2019 and 2025.

* Include churned customers, down-sells, and "silent users" who hold budgets but rarely log in. Speak directly with existing customers for valuable insights, but don't ignore the ones who left.

* For sensitive deals, avoid pinging every top-10 logo directly. Prioritize ex-admins, project champions, or former decision makers. You can still identify risk without alerting the account's managers.

## Core question themes to cover on every customer reference call

Structured calls enhance the effectiveness of customer reference communications. Follow a consistent structure so you can compare responses across references like a standardized template, while leaving room to dig deeper.

* Cover these themes: buyer journey, implementation process, product fit and gaps, support quality, pricing and ROI, competitive set, renewal and expansion intent. Customer reference calls provide insights into vendor capabilities across all of these areas.

* Each theme should have 3 to 5 core questions plus room for follow-ups. Skip the 40-question script. It kills candor. Common questions should prompt stories, not yes/no. Use open ended questions to maximize value from calls by focusing on key areas.

* You're not chasing NPS. You're trying to determine future revenue durability, expansion potential, and risk that spreadsheets miss. Customer reference calls validate ROI and usability through specific questioning.

### Adoption, usage, and job performance impact

This subsection covers how the product affects actual user workflows and job performance at the customer organization.

* Ask what teams use the product daily, weekly, or rarely. Probe whether usage is wide (across departments) or shallow (one power user), since renewal risk rises with concentration.

* Push for specific metrics. Strong answers sound like "we cut month-end close from 10 days to 4 in 2023." Vague statements about productivity don't help you model anything. Concrete examples are important to validate customer satisfaction and feedback.

* Ask how the product changed responsibilities for individual contributors and managers. This tells you whether it's embedded in workflows or sitting on the shelf.

### Implementation, support, and ongoing work ethic of the vendor

Focus here is on the vendor's reliability, responsiveness, and work ethic as customers experience it.

* Ask whether the initial rollout hit milestones on schedule. Probe how the vendor behaved when issues surfaced. Ask about the candidate's commitment to professional development, translated here as the vendor's investment in improving their own product and support over time.

* Request one example where the vendor went above and beyond, and one where they under-delivered or missed an SLA. These stories predict how the company will handle scale.

* Assess ongoing support quality: ticket response times, feature request handling, and how the vendor manages responsibilities post-go-live.

### Pricing power, renewals, and switching risk

This is the core of commercial diligence. You need to determine whether customers will tolerate price increases and stick through 2026 to 2028 budgeting cycles.

* Cover typical deal size, discounting versus list price, and recent renewal negotiations. Budget pressure in 2023 to 2025 made these conversations revealing.

* Ask: "If they raised prices by 20% next term, what would you do?" This gauges switching intent. Differentiate "annoyed but staying" from "actively piloting a competitor." That distinction changes how you model churn.

* Understand whether satisfaction translates to growth or just inertia. A locked-in customer isn't the same as an expanding one. Future performance depends on this distinction.

### Competitive set and differentiation

* Map which competitors were evaluated in 2021 to 2024 RFPs and why the target won or lost. Ask which vendors the customer would shortlist today if they re-ran the process.

* Capture the language customers naturally use to describe differentiation. Feed those phrases into your investment memo. This is valuable information that no desk research produces.

## Best practices for conducting high-quality customer reference calls

Good calls are structured, consistent, and candid. These best practices apply whether you're conducting reference checks for a hire or for a deal.

* Contact references by phone for more effective communication. Phone calls let you hear tone, hesitation, and enthusiasm that email surveys miss. Effective reference calls require careful preparation and active listening to gather insights.

* Open with a confidentiality promise and timebox (30 minutes). Limiting attendees on reference calls helps maintain an intimate conversation. Calls without the vendor present encourage open discussion about challenges.

* Push past "they're great" and answer with specifics. Ask for concrete examples. Best practices for reference calls include targeted questioning and understanding context. Avoid leading questions or sharing your thesis up front, which biases the answer.

* Document all reference checks as part of the process. Post-call documentation is essential for capturing key takeaways and themes. Use a consistent format so you can compare across calls. In HR, you might use a 1-to-10 scale to rate candidate skills; similarly, consider scoring each reference on key dimensions like implementation difficulty, ROI confidence, and renewal risk.

## Legal, ethical, and relationship considerations

Customer reference calls aren't background checks in the HR sense, but you still need to respect NDAs, confidentiality obligations, and competition rules. Be aware of written consent requirements and ensure customers don't share material non-public information.

* Coordinate with the target company on timing and volume. Calling too many customers can spook key accounts or overload a small CS team. Treat it like you'd handle contact with a current employer during a sensitive hire.

* Some buyers route all inquiries through a central contact. Others prefer direct outreach. Both have trade-offs. Avoid aggressive outreach to customers in the middle of live renewals or pricing disputes, since those accounts can't provide information freely.

* In commercial diligence, just as in HR, the goal is to avoid negligent hiring, or in this case, negligent investing. The risk of skipping this step is real.

## Synthesizing insights into an investment or M&A decision

Convert your call notes into 1 to 2 clear pages for your IC memo.

* Group findings by theme: retention, pricing power, competitive risk, product gaps. Tally how many references mentioned each issue. This creates a "Customer Voice" section with real weight.

* Map findings back to your original thesis. Where did reference information validate assumptions? Where do you need to adjust churn, NRR, or sales efficiency? Conduct this step with discipline.

* Include 4 to 6 anonymized verbatim quotes that capture both enthusiasm and reservations. Hearing real-world results in a person's own words carries more weight than your summary.
