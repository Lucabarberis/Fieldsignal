# Selective Disclosure and Expert Calls: How to Stay Compliant

If you run expert calls, surveys, or panel interviews involving public companies, you need to understand selective disclosure rules before your next project. Here's how to stay on the right side of Regulation FD and insider trading law without giving up the primary research that drives your investment thesis or strategy work.

## Executive Summary: What You Can and Cannot Say on Expert Calls

Selective disclosure in research requires ethical planning to ensure participant safety and compliance with regulations. If you deal with public company information in any capacity, you must not elicit or receive material nonpublic information during expert interviews, surveys, or panel calls. Violating that boundary exposes you, your firm, and potentially the expert to enforcement action under federal securities laws.

Regulation FD governs what issuers and their representatives can selectively disclose to certain enumerated persons. Insider trading rules under the Securities Exchange Act, specifically Section 10(b), Rule 10b-5, Rule 10b5-1, and Rule 10b5-2, govern trading and tipping on material nonpublic information. These are separate bodies of law, but they overlap on expert calls whenever a speaker has access to information that hasn't been made public.

Expert network research is allowed when two conditions are met:

1. Experts are not current "covered" issuer personnel speaking on behalf of the company.

2. Calls and surveys are structured to avoid eliciting material nonpublic information and breaches of duty.

Selective disclosure research explores how companies report information to influence market perceptions. Your job is to make sure your research process doesn't become a channel for that kind of reporting.

Here's what you should do immediately:

* Use a compliance script at the start of every call. Remind the expert not to share material nonpublic information, trade secrets, or information covered by NDAs.

* Vet every expert before the call. Exclude current senior officers, investor relations staff, and public relations officer roles at public companies.

* Refuse to ask for and refuse to accept material nonpublic information. If an expert volunteers it, stop the call.

* Document everything. Keep transcripts, screening forms, expert attestations, and call summaries for audit.

* Maintain a clearly defined internal policy that spells out what counts as material nonpublic information, what topics are off limits, and what happens when a call goes sideways.

## Regulation FD and Selective Disclosure: Core Rules You Need to Know

The Securities and Exchange Commission adopted [Regulation Fair Disclosure](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/33-7881.htm) (Regulation FD) in 2000 as a direct response to issuers giving material nonpublic earnings data and guidance to favored securities analysts and selected institutional investors without making that information broadly public. The SEC enacted Regulation FD in 2000 to regulate disclosures and stop the practice of tipping off a few market participants before everyone else heard the news. The SEC enacted Regulation FD in 2000 to enhance market transparency across capital markets.

Selective disclosure occurs when companies release non-public information to a select group before public access. In practice, that means an issuer or a person acting on its behalf shares material nonpublic information with a limited audience, like a handful of research analysts or big funds, without simultaneous initial broad distribution to the public. Regulation FD prohibits selective disclosure of material information to prevent this exact scenario.

Selective disclosure creates an unlevel playing field benefiting informed professionals over average investors. The rule was designed to eliminate that advantage.

Key requirements under Regulation FD:

* Intentional disclosures of material nonpublic information to covered persons must be made public simultaneously. The issuer must use methods of fair disclosure like a press release, a Form 8-K filing, or a widely attended conference call.

* Non intentional selective disclosure must be made public "promptly." That typically means within 24 hours or before the next day's trading on the New York Stock Exchange after a member of senior management learns of the disclosure. Selective disclosures must be publicly disclosed simultaneously or within 24 hours.

* Full and fair disclosure means using broad, non-exclusionary channels. A private discussion with two analysts doesn't count. A widely distributed press release followed by a subsequent conference call does. Regulation FD prohibits selective disclosure to analysts without corresponding public release.

* Companies must disclose material information to all investors, not just the ones with superior access or the biggest positions.

The main categories of covered persons under Regulation FD include:

* Broker-dealers and their associated persons

* Investment advisers

* Certain institutional investment managers (those filing Form 13F reports)

* Investment companies and their affiliated persons

* Hedge funds and other market professionals

* Any security holders where it's reasonably foreseeable they'll trade on the information

Regulation FD focuses on disclosures by issuers and people acting on their behalf. It doesn't directly regulate buy-side investors or third-party research buyers. But violations can taint expert calls if a covered person is speaking in an issuer capacity and discloses material nonpublic information to you. If that happens, your downstream trading or recommendation activity triggers separate insider trading liability.

## Material Nonpublic Information: Where Expert Calls Cross the Line

Information is "material" if a reasonable investor would view it as important in making an investment or voting decision. The Supreme Court has described materiality as whether there's a substantial likelihood that a reasonable shareholder would consider it significant. A reasonable person evaluating the same determination would weigh both the time and the magnitude of the information against the company's overall profile.

Information is "nonpublic" if it hasn't been broadly disseminated through recognized channels. That means no major press release, no Form 8-K, no widely circulated news coverage, no public webcast. If the market hasn't had a chance to absorb it, it's still nonpublic.

Here are concrete examples that frequently show up in expert calls and cross the line:

* Unannounced earnings trends. "We're going to miss consensus by 10%" is textbook material nonpublic information.

* Non-public churn data. Customer loss rates that haven't appeared in filings or investor presentations.

* Price increases or contract changes not yet communicated publicly.

* Pending M&A discussions. Any mention of acquisition talks, LOIs, or deal structure before public disclosure.

* Undisclosed product failures. Safety issues, recalls, or defects that haven't been reported.

* Major customer wins or losses not yet in a filing or press release.

Seemingly small quantitative details can be material when they change the expectations reflected in price targets or valuation models. If an expert says "December renewal rate dropped from 93% to 80%," and that data point isn't public, it's almost certainly material. It provides additional material details that would significantly alter an analyst's model.

Once information has been disclosed via Form 8 K, press release, or a widely attended webcast, and enough time has passed for the market to absorb it, it's generally treated as public. But the absorption threshold can be tricky. A few hours after a press release might not be enough. A full trading day usually is.

Three short scenarios to illustrate:

* **Scenario 1**: A current VP of Sales at a public SaaS company reveals next quarter's pipeline conversion rate during an expert call. The market doesn't know this number. That is material nonpublic information. The call should be stopped.

* **Scenario 2**: A former engineer describes the architecture of a product that already launched and was publicly documented in a 10-K filing. This is safe. The information is historical, public, and experiential.

* **Scenario 3**: A customer shares satisfaction scores and contract terms for a mid-size deal. If the contract isn't large enough to move the issuer's securities price and the data isn't company-wide material, it's likely safe. But if the customer is a top-5 account and the renewal terms significantly altered expectations, it could be material.

Frame your research objectives around public information interpretation, operational practices, and customer experience. Don't ask for future guidance, unreleased metrics, or anything the issuer hasn't put into the investment community through recognized disclosure channels.

## Insider Trading, Tipping, and Selective Disclosure: Legal Framework

Insider trading risk in selective disclosure scenarios comes from two main legal theories: classical insider trading (corporate insiders trading on information owed to shareholders) and misappropriation (outsiders breaching duties of trust or confidence to use confidential information for trading). Both are enforced through Rule 10b-5 under the Exchange Act.

Here's what you need to know about the key rules and case law, without the legalese:

* **Dirks v. SEC (1983)**: The personal benefit test for insider trading was established in 1983. This case set the standard for tipping liability. A tipper is liable if they received a personal benefit (money, reputation, reciprocal favors) from passing on the information. A tippee is liable if they knew or should have known the tipper breached a duty.

* **United States v. O'Hagan (1997)**: Defined the misappropriation theory. Someone outside the company who misuses confidential information obtained in breach of a duty of trust can face insider trading liability. This is how expert network scandals get prosecuted. The [Galleon Group and SAC Capital cases](https://www.sec.gov/enforcement-litigation/reports-investigations/34-46898) showed that expert networks used to transmit clinical trial results and other material nonpublic information to hedge funds resulted in prison time and massive penalties.

* **Rule 10b-5**: Prohibits fraud, misrepresentation, and material omissions in connection with the purchase or sale of securities. Covers both classical and misappropriation theories.

* **Rule 10b5-1**: Addresses trading "on the basis of" material nonpublic information. Provides a narrow affirmative defense if a trading plan was established before the trader became aware of the information. After [December 2022 amendments](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2022-222), the SEC added cooling-off periods, good-faith certifications, and limits on overlapping plans.

* **Rule 10b5-2**: Defines duties of trust or confidence in certain circumstances, including familial relationships and express confidentiality agreements. This rule catches situations where someone receives material information under an implicit or explicit promise of confidentiality and then trades.

Insider trading liability involves determining when a selective disclosure crosses into illegal activity. You face risk if you:

1. Trade or recommend trades while aware of material nonpublic information obtained from an expert call.

2. Cause someone else to trade (tip) while aware of the tainted information.

Regulation FD and insider trading law are related but distinct. Regulation FD governs issuer communications and disclosure practices. Insider trading rules under the Securities Act and the Exchange Act govern market conduct when material nonpublic information is present. You can violate one without violating the other, but often they overlap.

Private company selective disclosure to a small investor group typically doesn't trigger Regulation FD. But it can still involve misappropriation or fiduciary-breach theories if confidentiality duties are violated. If an expert breaches an NDA to share private company financials and you trade on that, you're exposed.

Formal Rule 10b5-1 trading plans don't excuse knowingly eliciting material nonpublic information in research. Those plans address later trading once a plan was validly established before awareness. They're not a shield for the research process itself.

## Regulation FD in Practice: Who Is Covered and How Disclosure Works

Regulation FD applies to issuers with securities registered under Exchange Act Section 12 or that are required to file under Section 15(d). That includes most U.S. public operating companies, many closed-end funds, and issuers engaged in ongoing reporting obligations. Public companies of all sizes fall under this rule if they have registered securities.

Here's how coverage, timing, and methods work in practice:

* A "person acting on behalf of an issuer" includes directors, executive officers, investor relations and public relations officers, and employees or agents who regularly communicate with securities market professionals or security holders. If someone in these roles discloses material nonpublic information to you during an expert call, the issuer has a Regulation FD problem, and you potentially have an insider trading problem.

* Occasional offhand comments by lower-level issuer personnel typically aren't attributed to the issuer for Regulation FD purposes. But those employees can still create insider trading risk if they owe duties of confidentiality to the company. The distinction matters for how you vet experts.

* The SEC enforces Regulation FD against selective disclosures to ensure all investors receive fair information access, permitting investors to make decisions on equal footing.

Common exclusions to Regulation FD:

* Communications to media outlets (journalists covering the company).

* Communications to credit rating agencies operating in the proposing release or rating process.

* Communications to persons who owe a duty of trust or have signed NDAs. If your expert signs a confidentiality agreement with the issuer, the issuer's disclosure to that expert might be excluded. But if the expert then tells you, that's a different problem.

* Most communications in registered offerings governed by the Securities Act, including public or private sales of securities under a securities offering.

The timing standard for non intentional disclosure is critical. "Prompt" public disclosure means as soon as reasonably practicable, no later than 24 hours or before the start of the next New York Stock Exchange (York Stock Exchange) trading day after a senior official is aware that an unintentional selective disclosure of material nonpublic information occurred. Selective disclosures must be made public within 24 hours under this framework. Auditor notification may also be triggered depending on the nature of the disclosure.

Compliant public disclosure methods include:

* **Form 8-K filed with the SEC**: The issuer furnishes a current report under Regulation FD, ensuring the information reaches the investment community through a website posting and the SEC's EDGAR system. This method provides adequate notice to all market participants.

* **Widely distributed press releases followed by a scheduled conference call or subsequent conference call**: The issuer distributes the information broadly, then hosts a call open to all investors and analysts. They must provide adequate notice of both the time and the format.

* **Archived webcasts and issuer websites**: After a live event, the issuer posts the recording on its website. Combined with prior notice, this counts as public disclosure for Regulation FD purposes, giving the investment community access.

Regulation FD violations don't create private Rule 10b-5 actions. You can't be sued privately for a Regulation FD violation alone. Enforcement is through SEC actions seeking cease-and-desist orders, injunctions, and civil penalties against issuers and responsible individuals. The [AT&T case in 2021](https://www.mwe.com/insights/sec-brings-rare-regulation-fd-enforcement-case-implications-for/) is a clear example: the SEC charged AT&T when its investor relations executives made private calls to securities analysts disclosing disappointing smartphone sales ahead of public release. The company made the same determination that many issuers do, that selective outreach was harmless, and it wasn't.

Regulation FD mandates public disclosure when material non-public information is shared privately. The rule is enforced consistently, and ignorance of its scope isn't a defense.

## Designing Compliant Expert Calls and Surveys

Well-structured expert calls avoid selective disclosure and insider trading risk by excluding current covered issuer personnel, screening for conflicts, and policing material nonpublic information in real time. Researchers must balance participant autonomy with the need to withhold information to reduce bias in studies. The same principle applies to expert calls: you want honest, experience-based answers, not leaked financials.

Here's a five-step process for compliant research:

**1. Define the research question in non-MNPI terms.** Frame your questions around historical data, observable practices, customer experiences, and publicly available information. "How did vendor X's implementation compare to vendor Y's in 2023?" is safe. "What's vendor X's Q3 revenue going to look like?" is not. Data minimization in cryptographic research addresses the issue of revealing excessive personal information, and the same principle of minimizing unnecessary data exposure applies to expert call design.

**2. Vet and recruit experts.**

* Exclude current senior officers, IR staff, and public relations officers of public issuers from speaking in their issuer capacity.

* Confirm employment start and end dates. A "former" employee who left two weeks ago may still have material nonpublic information covered by NDAs.

* Obtain written acknowledgment that the expert understands their confidentiality obligations to their current or former employer.

* Check whether the expert is subject to active NDAs, non-competes, or other restrictions that would prevent them from speaking freely.

**3. Use compliance scripting.** At the start of every call, read a scripted compliance warning. Tell the expert:

* Do not share unreleased financial results, product development plans, pending M&A activity, or regulatory decisions.

* Do not share information covered by NDAs or trade secrets.

* Confirm they understand what counts as material nonpublic information.

* If they're unsure whether something is public, they should skip it.

**4. Moderate for MNPI in real time.** The interviewer or moderator listens for red flags and redirects questions immediately. If the expert starts sharing what sounds like material information, the moderator stops the line of questioning.

Red-flag topics to redirect away from:

* Unannounced earnings or quarterly results

* Upcoming price or contract changes not yet public

* Pending M&A discussions or deal terms

* Regulatory decisions before public release (e.g., FDA feedback not yet disclosed)

* Pipeline data or sell securities activity not publicly quantified

* Any forward-looking guidance the issuer hasn't released

**5. Document and store call records.** Keep transcripts, call summaries, expert screening forms, and attestations. Time-stamp everything. If your compliance team or outside counsel needs to review a project from 18 months ago, they should be able to pull the full record and see exactly what was asked and answered.

Example compliant question types:

* "How did the vendor's implementation process compare with others you evaluated in 2023?"

* "Which features caused your team to switch providers in 2022?"

* "How did their 2021 pricing change, which was publicly announced, affect your renewal calculus?"

These questions focus on the expert's own experience and publicly known events. They don't ask the expert to disclose what the issuer hasn't made public.

### Scenario A: Pre-Investment Diligence on a Public SaaS Company (2026)

You're evaluating a mid-cap public SaaS company for a potential position. You want to understand customer retention patterns and competitive positioning.

**Safe to ask:**

* Historical renewal rates that appear in public filings or investor presentations

* Customer comparisons based on publicly available pricing tiers

* Implementation experience and onboarding quality from former customers

* How publicly disclosed product changes (documented in press releases or 10-K filings) affected the customer's workflow

**Risky / MNPI territory:**

* Next quarter's churn rate if the company hasn't disclosed it

* Pipeline growth metrics not in any public filing

* Unreleased guidance or changes to contract terms that haven't been announced

* Internal NPS scores or customer satisfaction data that hasn't been shared with the investment community

**How FieldSignal structures this:** Short surveys to existing or former customers asking about their public renewal experiences. Experts who worked at the company recently or in senior roles are filtered out. Call scripts are used. Any answers that touch unreleased data are flagged and excluded from deliverables.

### Scenario B: Commercial Diligence on a Private B2B Payments Startup (2025 Series B)

You're a VC associate doing commercial diligence on a private company raising a Series B. Private companies are not subject to Regulation FD. But experts may still owe contractual confidentiality duties, and investors can still face insider trading risk if they trade on stolen or misappropriated information.

**Safe to ask:**

* How customers adopt and use features

* Onboarding experience and support quality

* Competitive comparison based on known product capabilities

* General satisfaction with pricing relative to alternatives

**Risky territory:**

* Asking for internal financial results not shared by the company publicly

* Requesting investor presentations or board materials

* Asking former employees to disclose internal metrics still covered by active NDAs

* Details about the funding round terms before the company has announced them

**How FieldSignal structures this:** Experts must attest they left before a specific date and aren't bound by active non-disclosure or non-solicitation obligations that would prevent them from speaking. ### Scenario C: Competitor Intelligence on a Medtech Product Launch (2024)

You're a corporate strategy analyst tracking a competitor's device launch. The competitor is publicly traded.

**Safe to ask:**

* Comparing feature sets of competing devices already on the market

* Regulatory approvals already granted and publicly documented

* Pricing list data that's publicly available

* Clinical study results published in peer-reviewed journals

**Risky / MNPI territory:**

* Unannounced regulatory failures or FDA warning letters not yet public

* Upcoming launch dates not disclosed in filings or press releases

* Internal test data from trials that haven't been published

* Manufacturing issues or quality problems not yet reported

**How FieldSignal structures this:** Moderators ensure that experts don't share trial performance data not yet published. Only experts with no current role in R&D or regulatory affairs at the target company are used. Each call starts with a compliance warning. If an expert tries to reveal pre-launch or unpublished data, the moderator redirects or terminates. Transcripts are stored for audit.

In all three scenarios, the pattern is the same: define what's public, stay within those boundaries, document your process, and terminate when someone tries to cross the line.

## Working With Your Legal and Compliance Teams

You should involve your legal or compliance function before scaling expert calls. They need to sign off on frameworks that address Regulation FD, insider trading, and confidentiality issues. Getting that sign-off upfront is cheaper and faster than cleaning up a problem after the fact.

Here's a simple four-step internal alignment process:

**1. Share a draft research policy internally.** Write down what your firm considers material nonpublic information, what topics are acceptable and unacceptable for expert calls, and what the escalation path looks like if a call goes wrong. This doesn't need to be 50 pages. Two to three pages with clear definitions and examples works.

**2. Review and refine MNPI definitions relevant to your strategy.** What's material differs by sector. In SaaS, churn and net revenue retention are often material. In medtech, clinical trial results and FDA decisions are material. In fintech, transaction volume and regulatory approvals matter. Tailor your definitions so your team knows what to watch for in the sectors you cover.

**3. Agree on recordkeeping standards.** Decide what transcripts, notes, expert screening forms, and attestations are stored, where they live, and who reviews them. Your compliance team or outside counsel should be able to pull any project file and see the controls you applied.

**4. Schedule periodic reviews of sample transcripts.** Pick a handful of completed projects each quarter and review the transcripts or summaries. Look for questions that got too close to MNPI, expert answers that should have been flagged, or gaps in documentation. This is how you catch problems before they become enforcement actions.

Memorialize a written "no-MNPI" policy for primary research. Include definitions, examples, red-flag topics, roles and responsibilities, and penalties for violations. Even a one-page document is better than nothing.

Small funds and growth-stage companies that don't have full-time compliance staff can still adopt lighter-weight versions of these controls. Pre-clearance of high-risk projects reduces personal liability for associates and partners.
