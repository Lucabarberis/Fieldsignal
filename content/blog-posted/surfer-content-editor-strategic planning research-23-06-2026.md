## Core Questions Strategic Planning Research Should Answer

Every research program must start from specific decision questions, not generic "market scans." If your brief says "tell us about the market," you've already lost focus.

Strategic planning facilitates effective decision-making by offering a decision framework. That framework starts with concrete questions. Here are the ones corp strategy teams actually ask:

1. **What's the addressable market size and growth rate for our core product segments in our top three geographies (2026-2030)?**

2. **What realistic market share gains can we capture vs incumbents under different competitive response scenarios?**

3. **Should we enter a new geography or vertical by building, acquiring, or partnering? Which entry mode gives the best risk-adjusted return?**

4. **Which product adjacencies deserve investment over the next 3 years vs incremental maintenance of existing offerings?**

5. **For a specific M&A target: what are the margin drivers, price elasticity, customer win/loss patterns, and integration effort?**

6. **How should capital be allocated across business units for maximum return? Which units to reinvest in, which to exit?**

7. **Under a defensive posture: what threats exist (substitutive technology, regulation, cost inflation), and when should we exit segments vs double down?**

These questions differ by strategic play. Growth strategies focus on new markets, products, and share gains. Turnaround or defensive strategies test whether internal changes or divesting units is more viable. Portfolio reshaping strategies determine which businesses to keep, sell, or spin out.

Situational analysis assesses internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats. Tools like SWOT analysis (evaluating strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats) and [PESTLE analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PEST_analysis) (examining political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental factors) help structure these questions before research begins.

How strategy teams are organized matters here. About 30% of companies use a CEO office mandate for strategy. Another 21% of strategy teams are strategy orchestrators facilitating decision-making. And 10% of strategy teams provide strategy service and intelligence. The questions you prioritize depend on which model your organization runs.

## Designing a Strategic Planning Research Program

Strategic planning research should follow a repeatable cycle tied to your corporate strategy calendar. One-off projects help, but a recurring program compounds value over time.

Here's a simple 6-step process:

1. **Clarify mandate and decisions.** Identify what decisions the upcoming board meeting, IC, or C-suite offsite requires. Example: "Do we enter Southeast Asia in 2027?" or "Which two BUs get incremental capital for 2027?"

2. **Define hypotheses.** Write 3-5 testable hypotheses for each decision. These might cover growth assumptions, competitive response scenarios, or pricing sensitivity. Use SMART goals: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound objectives keep hypotheses actionable.

3. **Choose research methods.** For each hypothesis, pick the method suited to the data gap: desk research, survey, expert interview, internal data pull, or scenario model. More on methods below.

4. **Collect and synthesize data.** Execute the research. Run desk scans, field surveys, conduct calls, gather internal metrics. Synthesize into structured findings organized by hypothesis.

5. **Stress-test strategy.** Use scenarios and sensitivity analysis. Get feedback from experts or internal leaders. Test assumptions under alternative futures.

6. **Monitor and refresh.** Set tracking indicators. Plan refresh periods (annual refresh, 3-year plan review) to update when new data arises or the environment shifts.

**Time horizons matter.** Tactical research covers 12-18 months (annual operating plan, near-term product decisions). Corporate strategy research covers 3-5 years (portfolio planning, M&A). Long-horizon research spans 7-10 years and typically only applies in capital-intensive sectors like energy, pharma, or infrastructure.

Resource allocation involves distributing financial, human, and technological resources to support strategy. Your research program needs dedicated budget, staff time, and tool access.

A strategic plan aligns stakeholders by ensuring clarity of mission and vision. Alignment and buy-in ensure that every stakeholder works toward shared objectives. Start research scopes 90 days before board offsites so outputs are ready for decision-making.

About 40% of companies push strategy resources onto the business units rather than centralizing them. Another 30% of companies have strategy teams as CEO offices. ## Key Methods: From Market Research To Strategic Research Insights

Strategic planning research blends classic market research with executive-ready strategic research insights. Here's an overview of the five major method categories and what each one does.

* **Secondary / desk research.** Existing market reports, government filings, industry publications, trade journals. Role: framing, sizing, identifying trends, regulatory shifts, competitor public information. This is where secondary data analytics starts.

* **Quantitative market research.** Custom surveys, customer usage data, segment sizing, adoption curves. Role: quantify hypotheses around demand, pricing sensitivity, willingness to pay.

* **Qualitative expert interviews.** Conversations with former executives, domain experts, customers, lost customers, suppliers. Role: understand motivations, constraints, competitive behavior, and things you didn't know you didn't know.

* **Internal data analysis.** Sales metrics, customer behavior, usage data, CRM insights, cost structure, margin trends. Role: ground external hypotheses in your own data. Sanity-check assumptions.

* **Advanced analytics / modeling.** Scenario modeling, forecasting, Monte Carlo simulations, price elasticity models, cohort analysis. Role: test decisions under uncertainty, explore trade-offs and risk ranges.

AI transforms complex data into confident strategic decisions when applied to these methods. Advanced analytics optimizes strategies and drives measurable impact across all five categories.

Each method answers different question types. Desk research frames the problem. Quant sizes it. Experts explain behavior and constraints. Internal data grounds your assumptions. Analytics test scenarios and quantify trade-offs.

## Practical Research Design For Corporate Strategy Teams

Good research design is about speed, relevance, and signal-to-noise. Not academic rigor for its own sake.

**Core design decisions:**

* **Sample definition.** Who exactly are you talking to? Job titles, company sizes, geographies. Don't mix enterprise buyers with SMB purchasers in the same sample.

* **Geography coverage.** Are you comparing US vs EU norms? Regulatory differences? Pricing expectations? Decide upfront.

* **Method mix.** What combination of quant surveys, qual interviews, and expert calls fits the question and timeline?

* **Timing.** When does the decision need to be made? Work backward from that date.

**MVP research vs full program:**

* **MVP (2-3 weeks):** 6-8 expert calls plus light desk research. Enough to test a quick 2026 market thesis or validate a single deal hypothesis before an IC meeting.

* **Full program (4-8 weeks):** Multi-country survey (200+ responses) plus 15-20 expert calls plus desk research plus scenario modeling. For a flagship corporate strategy refresh or major market entry decision.

**Common mistakes:**

* Over-broad questionnaires that try to cover everything and end up covering nothing well

* Under-specified B2B target profiles (talking to "IT buyers" when you need "mid-market CISO at 500-2000 employee companies in financial services")

* Treating one big survey as a substitute for ongoing insight

* Running research without hypotheses, which produces interesting but undirected data

## From Research To Strategic Narratives & Board Materials

Research only matters when it shapes the strategy story that goes to the CEO and board. Raw transcripts and survey data don't move decisions. Strategic thinking, translated into clear narratives, does.

**How to translate raw data into board-ready materials:**

* Distill surveys, interview transcripts, and models into a small set of strategic research insights. Each insight should link directly to a decision or tradeoff.

* Use a simple storyline structure: context (what's happening in the market), key insights (what we learned), implications for growth options (what this means for our choices), recommended moves (what we should do), and quantified impact ranges (what the financial upside/downside looks like).

**Practical tips:**

* Include anonymized verbatim quotes from expert calls and customer interviews to give color in board decks. Keep sources fully protected.

* Build 1-page insight summaries for each major finding. Use 2x2 matrices for positioning, scenario tables for risk ranges.

* Show the difference between base case and downside explicitly. Boards want to know the risk, not just the opportunity.

Strategic planning is linked to better agency performance in public sectors, and the same principle holds in private companies. When research feeds clearly framed narratives, leadership makes better calls.
