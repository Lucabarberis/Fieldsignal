# Pricing Research Methods: Conjoint, Van Westendorp, and More

Pricing research is the systematic use of market research to find the optimal price point for a product or service, measure price sensitivity, and replace guesswork with data. It reveals customers' willingness to pay accurately. Effective pricing research can increase profitability by 11%. This article covers the pricing research methods that matter most for PE/VC, strategy, and founder teams making real pricing decisions.

## Pricing research: what it is and when you actually need it

Pricing research links three things together: willingness to pay, perceived value, and competitor pricing analysis. The output is concrete. You get revenue forecasts, margin scenarios, and win-rate assumptions that feed directly into investment memos and board decks. Pricing research helps identify optimal price points for products and tells you where your pricing structure sits relative to alternatives.

You need pricing research at specific moments:

* You're overhauling SaaS plans or pricing tiers for 2026.

* You're gating new product features behind a paywall.

* You're expanding into a new geography with different purchasing power.

* You're shifting pricing models from fixed to usage-based or subscription.

* You're fundraising and need defensible monetization assumptions.

Most teams don't need every pricing analysis method available. You need one or two methods matched to the decision you're making, the budget you have, and the timeline you're working with. Understanding price sensitivity helps optimize pricing strategies for profitability, but only if the method fits the question.

## Core survey-based pricing research methods

Survey based pricing research is the fastest way to quantify price sensitivity and map acceptable price ranges before you commit to live experiments. These pricing research tools are typically run via online panels with 200 to 1,000 respondents, depending on whether you're pricing a consumer product or a niche B2B offering.

This section covers the Van Westendorp price sensitivity meter, the Gabor Granger pricing method, and monadic price testing. Each produces different outputs. All feed into the demand curves and revenue simulations that PE/VC and corporate finance teams use to model pricing scenarios.

### Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter (PSM)

The [Van Westendorp price sensitivity meter](https://www.intotheminds.com/blog/en/van-westendorp-pricing-study/) asks four open-ended questions. The Van Westendorp method uses four questions to gauge price sensitivity: at what price is this product too cheap (you'd question quality), a bargain (a good deal), getting expensive (but you'd still consider it), and too expensive (you'd walk away). The method also uses these four questions to gauge price acceptability, giving you psychological floors and ceilings.

![The image depicts a researcher pointing at the intersection point of intersecting curved lines on a graph, which likely represents the optimal price point in pricing research. This visual highlights the importance of analyzing various pricing scenarios and consumer preferences to determine effective pricing strategies.](https://images.surferseo.art/bbbfb1eb-41b5-413f-a215-a55680e3b481.png)

Responses are plotted as cumulative distributions. Where the curves intersect, you find the indifference price point (where equal numbers say "cheap" and "expensive"), the optimal price point (where "too cheap" meets "too expensive"), and the full acceptable price ranges. Price sensitivity measures how price changes affect consumer purchasing behavior, and the westendorp price sensitivity meter maps that sensitivity visually.

When Van Westendorp works well:

* Early-stage products with limited pricing history or no market benchmarks.

* MVP pricing where you need a quick read on acceptable prices for investor decks.

* Tight timelines or small budgets where you need directional pricing insights fast.

Two common pitfalls. First, B2B respondents routinely understate budgets in pricing surveys. The price they say they'd pay is lower than what they actually sign contracts for. Second, teams confuse "acceptable price" with "revenue-maximizing price." One analysis found Van Westendorp results biased low by roughly [35% compared to discrete choice methods](https://www.pricingsolutions.com/van-westendorp-analysis/). Price sensitivity can vary significantly across different consumer segments, so segment your sample carefully.

### Gabor Granger pricing method

The Gabor Granger pricing method is a sequential approach that tests purchase intent at specific price points to build a demand curve directly. The [Gabor-Granger method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabor%E2%80%93Granger_method) assesses willingness to pay at various price points, making it more behavioral than Van Westendorp. Gabor-Granger determines willingness to pay at various price points by walking each respondent through escalating or descending prices.

The survey flow is straightforward. Show a product description, then ask "Would you buy at $69?" If yes, raise to $89. If no, drop to $49. Continue until you've mapped each respondent's maximum willingness to pay across your pre-set price levels.

What you get:

* A demand curve showing the percentage willing to buy at each price level.

* The revenue-maximizing price point (price times demand).

* Visibility into price elasticity between successive different price points.

Example uses include deciding between $49, $69, and $99 for a SaaS subscription, setting a mobile app premium tier, or validating B2B service packages at various price points. Compared to Van Westendorp, Gabor Granger is more purchase-oriented but still hypothetical. It gives you a demand curve, not real conversion data.

### Monadic price testing and monadic price experiments

Monadic price testing shows different prices to separate groups. Each respondent sees only one price, removing direct comparison bias and mimicking how buyers encounter pricing in the real world. Monadic price experiments isolate the effect of price alone.

The design is simple. Randomly assign respondents to cells, each seeing a single price point (for example, $49, $69, or $89). Measure purchase intent, likelihood to recommend, or perceived value at that one price. Because there's no comparison set, you get a cleaner signal on how each initial price point performs in isolation.

When monadic price experiments are strongest:

* You're testing a small number of discrete price levels.

* You're validating a short pricing ladder or tier boundaries.

* You're comparing your offer against a specific competitor benchmark.

Two practical tips. Make sure each cell has at least 100 completes. Keep product descriptions identical across cells so only price differs. Effective pricing studies require clear survey design to avoid bias, and monadic tests live or die on consistent execution across cells.

## Conjoint analysis: pricing in the context of real trade-offs

Conjoint analysis is the reference method when you need to understand how price, product features, and brand interact in real purchase decisions. It doesn't just ask "what's the optimal price?" It asks "which combination of features, brand, and price will win market share?" [Conjoint analysis measures the importance of product features](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0377221701001473) and pricing simultaneously, which makes it far more powerful for complex offers.

Use cases include SaaS bundle design, FMCG portfolio pricing, automotive trim levels, and good-better-best B2B service tiers. Conjoint analysis measures the importance of product features and pricing together, showing the relative importance of each attribute in driving purchase decisions. It usually requires 300 to 500+ respondents and solid statistical design, so it's a higher-investment method with a higher strategic payoff.

### Choice-based conjoint and brand price trade-offs

In choice-based conjoint, respondents see sets of competing offers. Each set bundles different product configurations, brand names, and price levels. Respondents pick one option or choose "none." Discrete choice modeling then estimates part-worth utilities for every attribute level, including price.

The result is a simulator. You can model what market share and revenue you'd get under different pricing scenarios, feature mixes, and competitive conditions. This is where consumer preferences become quantifiable inputs for forecasting.

Brand price trade-off (BPTO) is a related approach. It focuses explicitly on how target customers trade off price versus brand strength. You include competitor brands as attributes and measure how much extra people will pay for your brand versus a cheaper challenger, or how a new entrant could price 10 to 15% below incumbents without eroding perceived quality. Brand price trade analysis tells you the dollar value of your brand in a competitive context.

When to choose conjoint or BPTO over simpler methods:

* Your offer is complex, with multiple feature trade-offs that affect pricing.

* You're making portfolio or bundle decisions, not just setting a single price.

* The stakes are high enough (forecasting revenue for 12 to 24 months) to justify a deeper study.

## Experimental pricing research: testing prices with real behavior

Experiments differ from pricing surveys in one critical way: they observe what people actually do at different prices, not what they say they'll do. Price experimentation gathers real behavioral data through varying price tests on different customer segments. Companies should conduct iterative market testing to measure price changes on sales before a full launch.

Experiments are strongest once you already have a pricing hypothesis from survey methods and financial modeling. Common approaches include online A/B tests, monadic price testing in live funnels, and proposal price variation in B2B deals. Treat experiments as final validation for high-stakes pricing changes affecting 2026 and 2027 forecasts.

### From survey insights to real-world price experiments

The process has four steps. First, run Van Westendorp, Gabor Granger, or conjoint to define the viable price range. Second, model the revenue impact across different pricing scenarios. Third, design online or sales-led experiments within that range. Fourth, roll out the winning price, typically in one region or segment before going broad.

Two concrete setups work well. For subscription pricing, run a website A/B test showing different prices to randomized visitors for at least four weeks. Track conversion, revenue, and average order value. One [SaaS company testing a 15% across-the-board price increase saw roughly 8% revenue gain](https://www.agenticaipricing.com/continuous-pricing-experiments-a-b-testing-your-monetization-model/) despite a slight drop in conversion. For B2B, present slightly different pricing in proposals to similar prospects under controlled conditions, then measure win rate, average deal size, and sales cycle length.

Key metrics: conversion rate, average contract value, churn or refund rate, and total revenue over a fixed period. Common pitfalls include changing multiple variables at once (price and messaging), running tests too short to reach statistical significance, and ignoring seasonality. Recent research also shows [interference bias in dynamic pricing experiments can mislead profit estimates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14538).

## Market-based pricing research and competitor pricing analysis

Market-based pricing research places your price in the context of competitors, alternatives, and buyer expectations. For PE/VC and corporate strategy readers, this research is essential for understanding how pricing aligns with positioning and how it will affect share shifts and long-term business success.

Competitive benchmarking involves surveying competitor prices for similar products or services. Competitor pricing analysis reveals market trends and pricing norms. Brand price trade and price perception mapping show where your brand sits: premium, parity, or value-focused. Understanding customer value helps inform pricing strategy decisions that hold up in competitive markets.

### Competitor pricing analysis and expert insight

A rigorous competitor pricing analysis goes far beyond scraping public pricing pages. You need list price data, discount norms, typical contract structures, and non-price sweeteners like support tiers, SLAs, and implementation costs. Teams that map actual contract data often find discounting of [10 to 30% off list for annual contracts, and 40 to 50% for enterprise deals in competitive situations](https://getfairview.com/blog/competitor-pricing-analysis).

Three concrete data sources: public pricing pages and plan tiers, RFPs and sales collateral shared by buyers during win/loss interviews, and confidential expert interviews with former sales leaders, buyers, or channel partners.

## Designing a pricing research program that investors and leadership trust

The goal isn't to run every method. It's to build a pricing research plan that executives and investment committees will actually trust. Pricing research helps your team determine demand, set the right price, and maximize profit with defensible data.

The structure has five steps: clarify pricing objectives, choose methods, collect data, model scenarios, and align on decisions. Typical timelines run three to four weeks for a focused survey-based study, six to eight weeks for conjoint plus expert interviews. Businesses can enhance customer acquisition through informed pricing strategies, but only if each method's output feeds a specific forecast.

### Step 1: Define the pricing decision and constraints

Start by writing a clear pricing question. Not "optimize pricing" but something like "Can we move our entry plan from $39 to $49 without losing more than 5% conversion?" Price laddering identifies emotional benefits linked to pricing, which can inform how you frame the question.

Document constraints upfront: required gross margin, fixed costs and variable costs that set your floor, competitor reference points, sales objections, and contract limits. Align with finance and revenue leadership early so your research outputs tie to approved targets.

### Step 2: Select the right pricing research methods

Pricing methodologies should align with specific product lifecycle and market dynamics. Here's a rule of thumb:

* If you have a new, simple product, use Van Westendorp to find the acceptable range.

* If you need the revenue-maximizing price among specific price points, use Gabor Granger.

* If your offer has complex feature trade-offs, use conjoint analysis.

* If you're validating a short list of various price points, use monadic price testing.

Penetration pricing captures market share with lower initial prices. Skimming pricing targets customers willing to pay premium prices. Price skimming recoups development costs by setting a high initial price on unique products. The pricing analysis method depends on whether you're entering or defending a market. When stakes are high, combine methods: Van Westendorp to bound the range, then conjoint or monadic experiments to refine within it.

### Step 3: Collect data with quality in mind

Poor sampling ruins pricing research. Respondents must match your real target market by job title, company size, region, and purchasing authority. For B2B, 50 well-chosen respondents often beat 500 generic ones.

Generic survey panels work for consumer pricing. For B2B pricing studies, you'll likely need targeted expert or customer samples. FieldSignal can recruit targeted insiders when generic panels don't match your target customers. Practical checks: use attention filters, write realistic product descriptions, and set price ranges that reflect real budgets. Accurate data starts with the right sample.

### Step 4: Turn pricing data into financial scenarios

Convert survey outputs, your demand curves, optimal price zones, and elasticity estimates, into revenue and margin scenarios. Effective pricing strategies benefit from blending quantitative modeling and behavioral psychology. These scenarios should slot directly into the spreadsheets your PE/VC or corporate finance team already uses.

Model at least three cases. Conservative: a price reduction from current levels with modest sales volume gains. Base: the optimal price from your research with expected conversion. Aggressive: prices rise to the upper end of the acceptable range with projected churn. Tie each case to explicit price levels and assumed conversion or churn changes so decision-makers can see the trade-offs. These scenarios are only useful if they connect clearly to board or investment memos.

### Step 5: Align leadership and act on the findings

Pricing research only pays off if sales, product, finance, and leadership agree on a decision and a timeline. Present findings in a focused format: one page on methods and sample, one page of price sensitivity charts, one page of financial impact by scenario. That's enough for a market launch decision.

Set a clear go-live date for new prices. Define the metrics that trigger a revisit: churn over 8%, win rate dropping below 30%, or sales volume falling outside the modeled range. Don't let pricing research turn into a months-long debate. Speed matters. Act on the data.
