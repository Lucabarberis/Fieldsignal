## How the global fashion industry really looks in 2024–2026

The global fashion industry, including apparel and footwear, is close to $2.25 trillion in size. If it were a country, its economic output would rank among the top ten by GDP. Consumer spending on clothing reached $2 trillion in 2021, and growth has continued at low single-digit rates since. That scale matters for any investor trying to find where returns actually concentrate.

The volatility from 2020 to 2023 reshaped the market landscape. An 18% contraction brought sales to about $1.45 trillion, followed by a sharp rebound toward $1.9 to $2.0 trillion by 2023. Forecasts for 2024 through 2026 project low single-digit CAGR of 3 to 5% in most markets, reflecting macro headwinds and shifting consumer behavior. The US apparel market alone is estimated to reach $373 billion.

Regionally, the US and China remain the largest apparel retail markets, followed by the UK, Germany, and Japan. These five countries still make up a large share of global apparel demand, but gdp growth in India and Southeast Asia is pulling investment attention eastward.

Womenswear historically accounts for more than half of apparel spending. Specifically, 53% of global fashion retail spending is on womenswear. Menswear and childrenswear take smaller but faster-shifting shares. Footwear and accessories behave differently from core apparel in downturns. Footwear tends to rebound stronger, while accessories often polarize. Jewelry sales are growing at over four times the rate of clothing, signaling a shift in discretionary spending patterns.

Fashion market research helps identify consumer preferences and trends across these categories. The structural forces investors must factor in include: sustainability expectations, social-media-driven micro-trends, the rise of resale, athleisure dominance, luxury polarization, and persistent pressure from fast fashion players. E-commerce accounted for nearly 20% of global retail sales in 2021 and has only grown since.


## Key apparel and fashion segments investors need to understand

Rigorous apparel market research starts by defining which exact segment you care about. Growth, margin, and risk profiles differ sharply across womenswear, menswear, childrenswear, athleisure, luxury fashion, premium contemporary, fast fashion, off-price, workwear and uniforms, and performance sportswear. Picking the wrong category gives misleading valuation multiples.

**Fast fashion.** Market research here must address product turn speed, sourcing concentration in China, Bangladesh, and Vietnam, exposure to TikTok-driven demand cycles, and regulatory scrutiny on textile waste. Brands like H&M, Zara, and Shein define this space. Shein has reportedly surpassed H&M and Zara combined in some consumer spend metrics by 2024, compressing incumbents' margins and raising questions about long term success for traditional fast fashion companies.

**Athleisure and sportswear.** This segment has outperformed overall apparel in both China and the US since around 2020, driven by wellness trends, work-from-home behavior, and sneaker culture. [Lululemon grew roughly 220% from 2020 to FY2025 to reach about $10.6 billion in revenue](https://businesstats.com/sports-brands/). Nike remains the largest player at roughly $51 billion, but challengers like On Running (up 285% over three years) are gaining share fast. Sustainable apparel options are a growing trend in consumer preferences within this category.

**Luxury and bridge luxury.** Polarization is sharp. LVMH, Kering, and Richemont-type portfolios capture high-income demand. Aspirational luxury buyers, especially Gen Z and younger millennials, are increasingly pulled into resale and outlet channels. Luxury brands face a bifurcation: top-tier houses hold pricing power, while "affordable luxury" gets squeezed from above and below. Consumers increasingly demand sustainable materials and ethical production from these fashion brands.

**Resale and second-hand.** The resale market is expected to grow two to three times faster than the primary market. Global resale apparel grew 17.6% in 2024 to reach $204.7 billion. Circular fashion is driven by environmental regulations and conscious consumerism. Investors must understand unit economics, authentication costs, and the cross-over between resale and primary sales. Key players include The RealReal and Vinted.

Market research helps identify new customer segments and new opportunities across all of these segments.

## Core questions apparel investors should answer with market research

Before you conduct fashion market research, define 6 to 8 decision-critical questions that will materially change your investment thesis or valuation. If answering the question differently wouldn't change your model, it's not worth researching.

Example questions to build your brief around:

* Is this brand's e commerce growth actually incremental, or just channel shift from wholesale?

* Are core customers trading down to off-price, fast fashion, or resale?

* What's the gross margin durability given sourcing concentration and tariff exposure?

* How fast do styles need to be replaced to stay relevant in trend-sensitive segments?

* What's the return rate in online channels vs in store, and how does that affect unit economics?

* How much brand switching is happening in the target demographic?

Conducting market research requires understanding consumer behavior and competitive intelligence. Structure your questions across four pillars.

**Customer behavior.** Quantify frequency of purchase, price sensitivity, and cohort retention for your target market. Build detailed buyer personas based on demographics and psychographics. Those buyer personas should include insights into purchasing habits and pain points. Focus on segments like Gen Z, Gen Alpha parents, or high-income millennials in the US, UK, and Germany. Consumer feedback is crucial for refining pricing strategies and understanding shopping habits.

**Channel mix and e commerce.** Research should probe the brand's exposure to DTC vs wholesale, marketplace vs owned site, and the role of physical retail in conversion and returns. Pricing strategies should reflect perceived product value to consumers across each channel.

**Product and assortment.** Ask about the share of revenue from top categories like sneakers, denim, or dresses. Understand historical bestsellers and how quickly old SKUs are replaced by new drops. If product offerings fall short of emerging trends, that's a red flag.

**Supply chain and margin durability.** Competitive analysis identifies market opportunities and evaluates pricing strategies at the sourcing level. Ask about sourcing countries, lead times, fabric choices, and vulnerability to tariffs or wage increases in key apparel hubs. Market research helps brands optimize pricing strategies effectively when supply chains are under pressure.

## How to conduct fashion market research: practical methods for investors

Effective apparel market research blends primary and secondary research in a clear sequence. You don't start with expert calls. You start with a thesis, then validate it.

**Step 1: Clarify your deal thesis.** Define who you're evaluating, what segment they're in, and what margin expectations drive your model.

**Step 2: Scan secondary data.** Use industry reports, databases, and public filings to size the addressable market and growth rates. Secondary research uses existing data collected by others and is often more affordable than primary research. Sources like Euromonitor, McKinsey State of Fashion reports, WTO trade data, and national statistics offices give you the baseline.

**Step 3: Run expert interviews.** [Primary research](/blog/primary-research-methods-examples-and-b2b-use-cases) collects unique data directly from sources. It's more labor-intensive but provides tailored insights that syndicated reports can't deliver. Fashion market research includes competitor benchmarking and trend analysis that only insiders can validate.

**Step 4: Test with customers and channel partners.** Surveys, store checks, and customer interviews validate what experts told you.

**Step 5: Synthesize findings into your model and memo.** Data analysis helps identify patterns, recurring themes, and emerging trends. Combining both research types enhances market understanding and gives your IC materials real teeth.

Market research requires blending quantitative data with qualitative insights. Identifying macro-trends and micro-trends is crucial in market research. Data cleaning is essential for ensuring high-quality research results before anything goes into your model.

### Primary research tactics specific to apparel and footwear

Apparel and footwear benefit from field techniques rarely available in other sectors. Surveys and interviews are common primary research methods, but fashion adds physical observation to the toolkit.

Store visits and virtual store walks matter. Visit flagship locations, outlet stores, and multi-brand retailers in cities like New York, London, or Shanghai. Observe merchandising, pricing ladders, promotion frequency, and shopper mix. Photo-log shelf space, promotional signage, and price tiers during store checks. Pair those observations with interviews of former store managers or regional directors to deeply understand what's working vs what's staged.


Run customer IDIs with specific segments. Talk to Gen Z sneaker enthusiasts in Germany or US parents buying childrenswear. Explore how they choose fashion brands, channels, and price points. Test reactions to product images, sustainability claims, or new direct-to-consumer apparel concepts through quick online communities.

### Using secondary fashion market research without overpaying

Paid industry data is useful, but many funds and early-stage fashion businesses are priced out of large subscriptions from providers that bundle apparel into massive packages. Secondary research uses existing data from published sources, and you don't always need to pay top dollar to get it.

Key types of secondary sources for apparel market research: global fashion market sizing reports, category-specific reports (e.g., "China luxury activewear 2025"), consumer lifestyle surveys, and apparel trade statistics. You can extract practical numbers from the World Bank, ILO, WTO, and national statistics offices for apparel production, labor costs, and trade balances. These data sources are free.

A concrete workflow: start with one or two foundational industry reports for context, then fill gaps with free or low-cost databases, then spend remaining budget on targeted expert calls. This is how you gain insights without buying multiple overlapping subscriptions.

## Special topics apparel investors should research in 2024–2026

Beyond generic market research, investors should probe several cross-cutting issues that will shape apparel returns in the next few years. These topics affect every segment.

**Sustainability and regulation.** The EU and UK are tightening Extended Producer Responsibility rules for textile waste. Research should test whether a target brand's claims about sustainable fashion align with actual practices. Sustainable practices influence pricing strategies in fashion, and consumers continue to push for transparency across the value chain. Ask about: percentage of traceable raw materials, water and chemical usage, and carbon emissions per SKU.

**Inflation and consumer trade-down.** Mid-income shoppers in the US and UK are trading down to discounters, second-hand, or fast fashion. High-income consumers keep spending on luxury items. Jewelry sales are growing four times faster than clothing sales in many markets, reflecting how discretionary budgets are shifting. Understanding these industry conditions is critical for modeling demand.

**E commerce vs physical retail.** Online apparel's share of retail sales has climbed toward 20 to 25% globally. That affects store profitability, returns, and marketing campaigns. KPIs to probe: online conversion rate, return rate, fulfillment cost, and store sales per square foot. Augmented Reality try-ons help decrease return rates in apparel and are worth asking about.

**AI and personalization.** AI is used by over 35% of fashion executives for various tasks, including demand forecasting, product image generation, and virtual stylists. Hyper-personalization uses advanced analytics to tailor shopping experiences. Over 35% of fashion executives use AI specifically for competitive analysis. Ask experts whether those tools are actually improving sell-through and reducing markdowns, or if it's mostly technology hype.

**Global supply chain risk.** Tariffs and higher duties are reshaping global trade in the apparel industry. [US apparel imports from China dropped from roughly 30% to 21% between 2019 and 2023](https://www.mckinsey.com/featured-insights/week-in-charts/is-nearshoring-in-fashion), with Bangladesh, Vietnam, and India picking up share. Nearshoring involves moving manufacturing closer to primary consumer bases, but actual near-shore sourcing has remained flat at about 17% in that timeframe. Brands can adjust prices based on competitor analysis findings, but supply chains are the bigger margin lever.


## Comparing ways to get apparel market insight as an investor

Investors usually mix three options to conduct fashion market research: syndicated reports, ad-hoc desk research, and expert networks or custom primary research. Each has a role. None works alone.

**Syndicated reports** give you high-level apparel industry data across countries and segments. They're useful for sizing and growth forecasts but often lack deal-specific insight about a given brand's positioning, existing customers, or unit economics. Competitive analysis helps brands understand market strengths and weaknesses at a category level, but reports won't tell you why a particular brand's margins are compressing.

**Internal desk research** means associates reading annual reports, earnings calls, and fashion industry news. It's low cost but time-intensive. When targets are private companies or early-stage brands, gaps multiply. You won't find markdown rates, wholesale terms, or brand perception data in a 10-K.

**Expert networks and primary research providers** connect you with former fashion executives, buyers, and suppliers for 1:1 calls, panel interviews, or custom surveys focused on your exact thesis. Primary research involves collecting data directly from sources who lived the P&L you're trying to model.

## Building an apparel market research plan for your next deal

Here's a practical template: a 2 to 4 week research sprint you can bolt onto pre-IC work for an apparel acquisition or growth equity deal. It works for any segment.

**Step 1: Define scope and markets.** Specify the segment. For example: US womenswear DTC brand with roughly $50M revenue, targeting millennial and Gen Z women, selling through owned e commerce and wholesale partners. Name your target audience clearly. For brand-level questions, an explicit [brand market research plan](/blog/brand-market-research-methods-and-vendor-selection) keeps the work focused.

**Step 2: Map data sources.** Collect a limited set of secondary sources: one global apparel or fashion industry overview, one or two category reports (e.g., "US Gen Z fashion 2025," "UK womenswear 2025"), and trade or labor data for main sourcing hubs. Don't overbuy.

**Step 3: Schedule expert calls.** Target 8 to 12 interviews with a mix of former brand executives, ex-buyers from key retailers like Nordstrom or Zalando, and supply chain specialists who've operated in the same price tier and region as the target. These calls reveal valuable insights about fashion trends, brand value, and competitive dynamics that reports miss.

**Step 4: Run customer or channel checks.** 10 to 20 short customer IDIs or a quick survey testing NPS, repeat intent, and how the target stacks up on style, price, and sustainability against competitors. Focus groups work if you need deeper qualitative texture on consumer preferences.

**Step 5: Synthesize into your model.** Translate findings into concrete numbers: realistic organic growth, sustainable gross margin, marketing efficiency in e commerce, and capex needed for store expansion or logistics. This is where strategies become numbers. You achieve confidence not from more data, but from better data.
