# Fund of Funds Research: Methods and Workflow

Fund of funds research is how PE and VC teams evaluate, select, and monitor the underlying funds that make up a fund of funds portfolio. It's a structured process that moves from pipeline building through due diligence to portfolio construction and ongoing monitoring. This article walks through the full workflow, the metrics that matter, and where primary research fits in.

## Quick overview: how fund of funds research actually works

A typical private equity fund of funds team starts by building a pipeline of candidate GPs using databases like Preqin and PitchBook, referrals, and direct outreach. A fund of funds typically raises money from investors to purchase shares in other funds, so the research job is identifying which funds deserve that capital. The team screens candidates by vintage year, strategy, geography, and track record metrics before moving into full due diligence.

This article focuses on private equity fund of funds investing in private markets: buyout, growth, venture capital, secondaries, and co investments. Not mutual fund-of-funds or ETFs.

FoF research has three core stages:

1. Pipeline building: sourcing, screening, and mapping the universe of candidate GPs.

2. Due diligence: quantitative performance analysis, qualitative evaluation, legal and operational checks, and reference-based expert research, all captured in a diligence checklist.

3. Portfolio construction and ongoing monitoring: setting allocation sizes, managing diversification, and tracking fund performance over 10+ year fund lives.

External research partners like FieldSignal fit into stages two and three. Expert calls with former LPs, ex-employees of GPs, and portfolio company customers help test GP claims on value creation, sourcing strength, and team dynamics. These feed directly into IC memos and monitoring reports.

## What is a private equity fund of funds?

A fund of funds pools capital from investors to invest in other investment funds rather than directly owning individual companies. In private equity, a PE FoF is a pooled investment vehicle, typically structured as a limited partnership, that allocates investment capital across multiple funds managed by different GPs. The general partner manages the fund of funds investments, while limited partners provide the capital.

![A group of business professionals is seated around a conference table, actively reviewing financial documents that include financial statements and due diligence checklists related to various private equity funds and hedge funds. They are discussing investment objectives and portfolio management strategies, focusing on the performance of different asset classes and the potential risks involved in fund investing.](https://images.surferseo.art/04cc5bd9-70cf-43b3-b1d2-8c0f3311dddc.png)

FoFs invest in multiple funds rather than individual securities. They sit between end-investors, such as pensions, endowments, family offices, and high net worth individuals, and the underlying private equity funds in the capital stack. A professional portfolio manager handles the fund of funds and selects underlying funds based on strategy, team quality, and expected returns.

Consider two concrete examples. A European pension plan uses a PE FoF to gain exposure to U.S. mid-market buyout pe funds where direct access would require €20-50 million minimum commitments per fund. Or a multifamily office uses a venture capital fund of funds to access top-quartile early-stage VC funds that aren't accepting smaller investors directly. Venture capital fund of funds invest in various VC funds, while private equity fund of funds invest in multiple PE firms.

Funds of funds are common in hedge funds, private equity, and target-date retirement funds. A fund of funds can invest in hedge funds, and funds of funds can include mutual funds and index funds. A target-date fund is a type of mutual fund that can be a FoF. FoFs can mix primary fund commitments, secondary purchases of existing LP interests, and co investments into direct deals. Each of these strategies changes the research workflow, which later sections cover in detail.

## Why investors still use fund of funds in private markets

Investors face double fees in fund of funds structures. That's the loudest criticism, and it's valid. But FoFs persist in private markets because they solve real problems that many institutional investors and certain investors can't solve on their own.

The primary aim of a fund of funds is to achieve broad diversification. Funds of funds offer enhanced diversification across multiple asset classes and geographies, spreading risk across vintages, strategies, sectors, and regions. Funds of funds provide diversification across multiple funds. A fund of funds can provide instant multi-level diversification and expert oversight. Building that kind of exposure from scratch by committing to 20+ individual PE funds requires significant capital and years of relationship-building.

Fund of funds can offer access to exclusive investment opportunities. Investors in funds of funds gain access to top-tier funds that require high minimum investments, often €10-50 million per fund. FoFs can provide access to high-minimum funds for smaller investors who don't have the ticket size to invest directly. Investors in fund of funds may have lower minimum investment requirements because the FoF aggregates capital from many LPs.

Many LPs don't have in-house teams for GP due diligence, ongoing monitoring, or detailed portfolio construction in private assets. Funds of funds simplify operational management for investors by providing broad exposure across different asset classes. Retail funds of funds spread capital across multiple index funds to simplify asset allocation for individual investors.

FoF managers also bring manager selection expertise, relationship networks, and the ability to underwrite emerging managers that aren't visible in standard databases. Particularly those LPs entering private markets for the first time, FoFs provide a way to gain access without building an internal team from scratch.

## How private equity funds of funds work in practice

A fund of funds typically operates as a limited partnership. The FoF GP manages the investment process, while FoF LPs commit capital that gets called over time as underlying fund commitments are made. The FoF itself is an LP to several private equity funds.

The lifecycle runs in two phases. First, a 3-5 year investment period where the FoF commits to underlying PE funds, secondaries, and co investments. Then a harvesting phase of 10-15 years where distributions flow back to FoF LPs. Commitment periods for fund of funds can exceed 15 years, and the liquidity of a fund of funds is limited by the least liquid underlying fund.

FoFs allocate across three buckets, each affecting research depth:

* Primary commitments: forward-looking research based on GP track record and team.

* Secondary investments: backward-looking analysis of existing portfolios and discount to NAV.

* Co investments: direct investments alongside GPs requiring deal-level diligence.

Extremely large fund of funds may struggle to deploy capital effectively, which is why most target a specific size range. Internal teams on a FoF platform typically include investment (sourcing, GP selection), research (quantitative and qualitative analysis), operations and finance (managing capital calls and distributions), legal and compliance (LPA review, terms), and risk or portfolio management.

### Fee structures and balance sheet implications

Private equity funds usually charge 2% management and 20% performance fees, commonly called "2 and 20." FoFs add their own fee layer on top. FoFs typically charge a management fee of 0.5% to 1.0%, with a performance fee around 5%. Some charge a 1% management fee and 5% performance fee. The fees charged at each level compound.

FoFs have two layers of fees: one for the FoF and one for underlying funds. Investors in FoFs may pay a total of 3% management fees when you combine both layers, plus carried interest from underlying funds and possibly a smaller FoF-level carry. Cumulative costs of funds of funds can erode long-term returns due to layered fees.

Funds of funds aim for superior risk-adjusted returns but may have additional fees that eat into net performance. FoFs face pressure to reduce their fee structures due to performance issues. Some [institutional FoFs have negotiated fees down](https://www.hpt.group/blog-posts/fund-of-funds-structure-guide) to 0.25-0.50% management with no carry.

| Management fees      | 1.5-2.0%             | \~1.0% FoF + 1.5-2.0% underlying           | \~1.0% FoF + underlying (co-invest often lower)   |
| Carried interest     | \~20%                | \~5% FoF + \~20% underlying                | FoF carry + underlying carry (co-invest often 0%) |
| Capital calls        | Direct from GP       | Passed through FoF                         | Mixed timing                                      |
| Minimum commitment   | €10-50M+             | Lower (aggregated)                         | Varies by deal                                    |

Capital calls and distributions from underlying funds flow through to FoF investors. On an LP's balance sheet, committed but uncalled capital shows as unfunded commitments. Distributions contribute to DPI and appear on the income statement as realized gains.

### Types of PE fund of funds and their research focus

Key FoF types in private markets, each with a different research emphasis:

* Buyout-focused FoFs: research emphasizes financial leverage, exit track record, and portfolio operations. Performance benchmarking against vintage peers is central.

* Venture capital FoFs: more uncertainty, earlier-stage fund managers. The diligence checklist shifts toward team evaluation, sourcing quality, and sector trends over financial statements.

* Secondary-only vehicles: research leans heavily on existing portfolio quality, valuation discounts, remaining total value, and cash flow modeling in the secondary market.

* Co-investment platforms: direct investments alongside GPs. Research looks more like direct PE deal work, requiring fast financial modeling and market sizing.

* Multi-strategy FoFs: mix strategies across different asset classes. Research must weigh correlations, overlap risk, and strategy trade-offs.

Structural nuances matter too. Evergreen FoFs backed by insurer balance sheets have no defined end date and allow rolling deployment. Closed-end vintage-year FoFs backed by traditional LP capital follow a more predictable lifecycle. The funds model you're evaluating changes how you structure diligence timelines and what data is available.

## Core research questions in fund of funds investing

This is the "what you're actually trying to answer" list for fund of funds research. Each dimension below drives specific questions your team should address before making an informed decision on any GP commitment.

* GP quality: Who are the partners? What's the turnover? Are incentives aligned? Is there key-man risk?

* Strategy fit: Where in the capital structure does this GP play? How does this fund complement the existing portfolio?

* Performance: What are past performance metrics relative to industry benchmarks and peer groups?

* Risk: What are potential risks around vintage, concentration, liquidity, and valuation?

* Operations and legal: What are the LPA terms, reporting quality, and governance structure?

* ESG and policy: Do practices align with LP investment objectives and policy constraints?

The performance of funds of funds should be evaluated against specific strategy-aligned metrics, not generic benchmarks. Fund of funds managers focus on choosing skilled fund managers for risk management, and investors analyze underlying fund holdings to identify concentration risk in funds of funds.

### Strategy fit, portfolio construction, and concentration risk

FoF research teams set top-down allocation targets by vintage year, region, sector, and fund type to manage concentration and correlation risk. Portfolio construction is about making sure each new GP commitment actually improves the overall portfolio rather than overlapping existing exposure.

Common constraints include maximum exposure to a single investment in any GP (often 3-7% of committed capital), sector caps, geographic limits, and restrictions on non-control growth strategies within a buyout-focused vehicle. A €500 million FoF might target 20-25 underlying funds with 3-7% position sizes each.

Research teams evaluate whether a new commitment fills a gap or creates redundancy. If you already have three U.S. mid-market buyout funds, adding a fourth doesn't improve diversification. The goal is to reduce risk through deliberate allocation, not simply accumulate fund investing positions across other funds.

### Quantitative analysis: performance, risk, and benchmarking

The main metrics used to assess private equity funds and FoFs:

* IRR (internal rate of return): gross vs. net, measuring time-weighted returns.

* TVPI (Total Value to Paid-in): total value created per dollar invested.

* DPI (Distributions to Paid-in): actual cash returned.

* RVPI (Residual Value to Paid-in): remaining unrealized value.

* PME (Public Market Equivalent): comparing PE returns to public markets.

Compare a candidate PE fund's performance to relevant quartiles and peer groups using vintage-year data from providers like [Preqin](https://www.preqin.com/) or Cambridge Associates. Key differences between top-quartile and median performers can be substantial. Absolute numbers without context are meaningless.

Analyze drawdown patterns, loss ratios, write-off rates, and consistency across economic cycles. How did the GP perform pre-2008, post-2008, and during COVID? Financial statements and financial records from past funds tell this story. For secondaries and co investments, deal-level modeling feeds back into fund-level risk assessments. Active managers who've survived multiple cycles with consistent returns deserve more weight than those with a single strong vintage.

### Qualitative research: team, sourcing, and edge

The qualitative side of fund selection covers partner stability, succession plans, decision-making processes, and internal incentives at the underlying PE funds. Due diligence became common with the [Securities Act of 1933](https://www.sec.gov/about/about-securities-laws), and it involves examining financial records before transactions. Today, investors perform due diligence to assess potential risks across both quantitative and qualitative dimensions.

Due diligence can be categorized as hard or soft. Hard diligence covers financials, legal terms, and market conditions. Soft diligence covers culture, team dynamics, and reputation. Due diligence helps reduce risk exposure in investments, and it's essential for broker-dealers to avoid liability.

FoF teams evaluate a GP's sourcing engine, sector expertise, and value-creation playbook. Does the GP have proprietary deal activity, or are they seeing the same auctions as everyone else? What's the track record of individual companies in their portfolio?

Reference checks are a primary tool. Calls with former employees, portfolio company executives, co-investors, and prior limited partners surface information that no data room can provide. ## Building a fund of funds due diligence workflow

A standardized diligence workflow ensures consistency across every GP evaluation. It also helps smaller FoF teams operate with the same discipline as much larger institutions. The goal is comparability: when you evaluate GP number fifteen, you want the same rigor you applied to GP number one.

![A team of researchers is gathered in a modern office, reviewing financial documents and taking notes, likely focusing on due diligence related to private equity funds and hedge funds. They appear engaged in discussions about investment capital and portfolio management strategies, essential for institutional investors and fund managers.](https://images.surferseo.art/feb8dc71-446e-4392-95a4-5f6fe69f8efe.png)

Here's a practical numbered process:

1. Screening: filter by vintage, strategy, minimum track record, fee structure, and preliminary references.

2. Desk research: pull data from databases, benchmark past funds against peers, review financial records.

3. Management meetings: strategy deep dives with GP partners, on-site visits when possible.

4. Expert calls: former LPs, ex-employees, portfolio company leaders, and co-investors.

5. Legal and financial term review: LPA, side-letters, preferred return terms, clawback provisions.

6. IC memo: synthesize all findings into a recommendation for the investment committee.

Tighter workflows improve comparability across GPs and make it possible for mid-market FoF teams to cover more ground with fewer people.

### Designing a private equity fund diligence checklist

A practical diligence checklist covers these major sections:

* Firm history and background

* Team composition, incentives, and turnover

* Track record and performance data

* Investment strategy and fund selection criteria

* Portfolio construction and deployment approach

* Operations, controls, and fund administration

* Legal terms, fee structure, and governance

* ESG and policy alignment

* Reference and expert interview findings

Each section should include both hard data (performance metrics, balance sheet strength at GP level, management fees details) and soft insights (culture, decision process, reputation from reference checks).

Document every source: CIMs, DDQs, data rooms, and primary interview transcripts. Conclusions need to be defensible in IC and to potential investors.

Here's an illustrative example: during diligence on a growth equity GP, a FoF team's checklist flagged that the LPA contained no key-man clause and the fee step-down after the investment period was unusually small. Expert calls with two former LPs confirmed that deals fail to materialize when senior partners leave, which had happened once before. The FoF passed on the commitment.

### Primary, secondary, and co-investment diligence nuances

Diligence on primary funds focuses on future deployment, team, and strategy. You don't have visibility into specific deals at commitment time. You're underwriting assumptions about the GP's ability to source, execute, and exit based on past performance.

Secondary fund and LP-interest diligence leans heavily on existing fund financials, cash flow models, and discount dynamics in the secondary market. You can see the underlying fund's portfolio, analyze individual assets, model remaining value, and estimate exit timing.

Co-investment diligence looks more like direct PE deal work. You need fast financial modeling, market sizing, and customer or supplier interviews. Timelines are compressed, sometimes weeks rather than months.

FoF teams adjust research intensity and timelines across these three categories while keeping a consistent approval framework. The diligence checklist adapts, but the IC process stays the same.

## Ongoing monitoring and reporting for fund of funds

Ongoing monitoring is the second half of fund of funds research. It kicks in after initial commitments and runs for 10+ years in a typical private equity fund lifecycle. Invest once, monitor for a decade.

Standard monitoring activities:

* Quarterly report reviews: tracking capital calls, distributions, fund NAV changes.

* Annual meeting attendance: hearing GP updates and peer LP perspectives.

* Portfolio company sampling: speaking to a sample of underlying individual companies to verify operational health.

* Periodic reference refreshes: especially when performance deteriorates or a GP changes key personnel.

FoFs track capital calls and distributions, update internal valuations, and reconcile these with LP reporting and internal balance sheet metrics. Market conditions change, and the assumptions made at commitment time need continuous testing.

### Look-through reporting, data aggregation, and tools

LPs expect look-through reporting from FoFs. That means sector exposures, geographic breakdowns, and stage breakdowns at the portfolio company level, not just fund level.

The challenge: dozens of underlying funds report on different schedules, use different valuation policies, and define terms differently. You need internal templates to standardize NAV reporting, normalize DPI/TVPI metrics, and reconcile data for benchmarking.

Fund administration platforms and portfolio monitoring tools help automate this aggregation. The specifics are vendor-dependent, but the principle is consistent: standardize inputs so you can produce meaningful outputs for your LPs.

Primary research, such as periodic expert calls into key holdings, can sanity-check GP valuations and commentary between formal reporting dates. When a GP says a portfolio company is performing well, a quick call with a customer or supplier either confirms or challenges that claim.
