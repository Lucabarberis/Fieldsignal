# Family Office Investment Research: Frameworks and Process

Family offices don't invest like pension funds or endowments. Their research is faster, more personal, and filtered through a set of values that most institutional LPs never consider. If you're raising capital, managing a deal, or advising wealthy families, you need to understand how this research actually works.

## Executive summary: how family offices actually run investment research

Most serious family offices follow a defined due diligence process that combines qualitative interviews, document review, reference checks, and background investigations. The typical cycle runs 3 to 5 months and involves 3 to 5 substantive meetings. Over 90% of inbound opportunities get eliminated at the first screening stage.

Decision making is principal-led and values-driven. A chief investment officer or the family principal controls the final call. ## What is a modern family office and how does it invest?

A family office is a legal entity established by ultra-high-net-worth families to manage their wealth. It handles both financial and personal affairs, from investment management to tax and estate planning to philanthropic oversight and legacy planning. A family office typically manages $30 million or more in assets, though many control hundreds of millions or more.

Family offices manage financial affairs for ultra-high-net-worth families with three primary objectives: preserving family wealth across multiple generations, compounding capital tax-efficiently, and supporting family values such as philanthropy, operating businesses, and impact themes. Investment management is a core service of family offices, but so is wealth planning, insurance planning, estate planning, and coordination across multiple jurisdictions.

Here's what the investment side looks like in practice:

* Family offices maintain a balance between public markets and private markets, often with 40 to 50% or more allocated to [alternatives like private equity, venture, and real estate](https://altss.com/knowledge-center/frameworks/family-office-investment-criteria-framework?utm_source=openai).

* Family offices may invest directly in startups or real estate, sometimes behaving like small PE firms.

* Family legacy and multi-generation planning shape risk tolerance, liquidity needs, and time horizon.

* Services and investment strategies of family offices are tailored to long-term family goals, not quarterly benchmarks.

* Some offices outsource almost all investment research to external investment advisors, wealth managers, and research vendors.


### Single-family office vs multi-family office vs virtual models

Family offices come in three common structures. Each one changes how you'll interact with them, how fast decisions happen, and what kind of research infrastructure they have in-house.

**Single family office (SFO).** Serves one ultra-high-net-worth family. Single-family offices typically require at least $100 million in net worth to justify the cost. Operating a single family office can cost around $1 million annually for talent and infrastructure. They hire in-house chief investment officer roles, analysts, and operations staff. Single-family offices offer highly personalized services, provide maximum control over family wealth management, and ensure confidentiality in financial affairs. They oversee both liquid and illiquid family office investments across asset classes.

**Multi family office (MFO).** A multi family office manages the financial affairs of multiple families, serving multiple unrelated families under one roof. MFOs charge fees on assets under management and standardize family office services like reporting, financial education, governance support, and administrative support. Their decision making process feels more institutional, often involving committees and consensus.

**Virtual family office (VFO).** A virtual family office coordinates external advisors, including wealth managers, accountants, lawyers, and research partners, instead of permanent in-house staff. This model works well for many families in the $30M to $150M investable assets range who want to avoid the fixed overhead of a full office. VFOs often rely on outside experienced professionals for research and diligence.

How structure affects your interaction:

| Factor          | SFO                 | MFO                      | VFO                         |
|-----------------|---------------------|--------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Decision speed  | Fast, principal-led | Slower, committee-driven | Variable, advisor-dependent |
| Research depth  | Deep, in-house      | Standardized             | Outsourced                  |
| Flexibility     | High                | Moderate                 | High                        |
| Cost to operate | \~$1M+/year         | Shared across families   | Lower fixed costs           |
Regardless of structure, serious offices still expect a clear diligence process, transparent information, and professional communications. Establishing a family office requires significant capital for talent and infrastructure either way.

## Core research priorities

The research agenda at most family offices is driven by three filters: capital preservation, alignment with family values, and clarity around downside risk. Everything else is secondary.

* **Strategy.** Family offices scrutinize investment strategies first. They want to understand return drivers, risk management controls, concentration, liquidity profile, and fit with existing exposures. They don't just want high IRRs. They want to know what drove past returns and whether the approach is repeatable.

* **Operational and reputational risk.** Controls, compliance, data security, and the quality of back-office providers matter. Data security is a growing challenge for family offices, with roughly 24% having experienced a breach or fraud event. Gaps here kill deals.

* **People.** Key employees and decision makers get disproportionate attention. A single bad actor can create permanent reputational damage for the family. Family offices face unique operational challenges for each family, and integrity of the people involved is a primary concern.

* **Long-term fit.** Families care about partnership potential, not just financial returns. Transparency, willingness to communicate bad news, and respect for the family's legacy and privacy all factor in. The family's goals extend well beyond a single fund cycle.

Family offices must clarify their mission to avoid operational confusion. A clear mission statement keeps research focused and prevents drift. Many family offices formalize this with an investment philosophy document.

### How family values and legacy shape research questions

Mission and values show up directly in diligence questions, especially for direct investments and first-time managers. This isn't optional. It's structural.

* Family values translate into concrete screening criteria: exclusion lists (tobacco, gambling, weapons), ESG or impact themes, geographic preferences, and sector priorities. A family that built its wealth in industrials often favors similar sectors. Family offices may focus on values-based investing including ESG and Impact Investing.

* Many offices assess whether an investment strengthens or threatens the family legacy, including media risk, political exposure, and alignment with philanthropic goals.

* Next generation family members sometimes push offices toward tech, climate, and social impact themes. This changes the research brief and the types of experts they seek out.

* Family offices specialize in structured succession planning for wealth transfer. Family governance documents, including investment policy statements and family constitutions, formalize these research constraints for future generations.


## The family office due diligence process: stages and timelines

Here's how the due diligence process typically unfolds, from first contact to commitment. Total timeline: 2 to 6 months, depending on the office and the deal.

1. **Pre-screening.** A principal, CIO, or MFO investment team filters inbound deals using minimum check size, sector, geography, and historical performance cutoffs. Over 90% of opportunities get eliminated here. Relevant information must be immediately accessible in your materials.

2. **Initial call.** A 30 to 45 minute conversation probing the high-level investment strategy, team history, and basic terms. Chemistry matters. For a single family office, the principal may attend directly.

3. **Detailed diligence.** Review of data rooms, financial models, draft limited partnerships or LPAs, and third-party research. Follow-up calls focus on track record, risk management, and pipeline. This phase runs 4 to 8 weeks.

4. **Reference and background work.** Calls to former colleagues, portfolio company executives, or customers to validate claims. This is where people risk gets tested.

5. **Decision and documentation.** A small internal committee or principal signs off. Legal counsel finalizes documents. Operations teams handle subscription and onboarding. Tax planning and estate taxes considerations get addressed at this stage.

### What family offices look for in emerging managers

Family offices are often the first institutional-style check for seed-to-Series-A funds and new direct deal platforms. They accept looser track records but ask sharper questions.

* They want deal-by-deal case studies showing how the team sourced, added value, and handled failures. Team cohesion and sourcing advantage matter more than a long performance history.

* Families often prioritize alignment on values, transparency, and access. Co-investment rights, reporting detail, and a clear vision for how capital will be deployed are table stakes.

* Emerging managers should expect 3 to 4 calls over several months. Come ready with concise memos, sample quarterly reports, and clear use-of-proceeds narratives.

* High-quality third-party research, such as expert interview transcripts or customer surveys, can materially strengthen an emerging manager's credibility with an affluent client base.

## Background investigations on owners, directors, and key employees

People risk is existential for family offices. Operating a family office involves attracting top financial and legal professionals, and the same standard applies to anyone they invest alongside. A single reputational misstep by a manager or counterparty can damage a family's name permanently.

* **Identity verification.** Family offices verify names, dates of birth, corporate affiliations, and board seats for principals at GPs, operating companies, and major counterparties.

* **Legal and regulatory history.** Criminal, civil litigation, regulatory, and bankruptcy checks going back 7 to 10+ years. They look for patterns, not isolated incidents.

* **Professional credentials.** Verification of licenses, education, past employment, cross-checked against public records, press, and industry databases.

* **Reputational screening.** Review of media archives, sanctions and watchlists, and public social media profiles to surface political exposure or values conflicts. This protects both the family business and the family name.

* **Practical outcomes.** Red flags can adjust position size, trigger deal protections like enhanced reporting or protective terms, or cause the office to walk away entirely.

* **Market research.** Family offices validate themes like healthcare software, logistics infrastructure, or energy transition before committing capital. They test these through primary research: customer interviews, competitive analysis, and unit economics review.

* **Manager research.** For private funds, offices assess sourcing edge, portfolio construction, team stability, and whether the GP adds value relative to existing managers. ## Designing a repeatable due diligence process for family offices

A repeatable process protects against both missed red flags and wasted bandwidth. Here's a 5-step framework that works for offices managing significant assets across multiple asset classes:

1. **Intake and quick triage.** Receive pitch, check against mandate filters (strategy, geography, check size). Fast reject or advance.

2. **Strategic fit and values alignment screen.** Overlay the family's mission statement, exclusion lists, and long term objectives. Does this coordinate investments with existing portfolio?

3. **Deep research and expert input.** Run commercial and financial diligence. Schedule expert calls. Review data rooms, risk registers, and prior quarterly letters.

4. **References and background checks.** Take up references. Run people-integrity investigations. Surface any legal or reputational risk.

5. **Investment memo and decision.** Prepare memo covering financials, values fit, operational risks, and people assessment. Decision by principal or committee. Document reasons and key assumptions for reference purposes. Our [investment memo template guide](/blog/investment-memo-template-how-pe-and-vc-investors-structure-them) shows how PE and VC investors structure these.

Track post-mortems on both successful and failed deals. Use that feedback to refine research checklists. This discipline helps sustain wealth across multiple generations and gives future generations a clear record of how decisions were made.

### Balancing speed and depth for smaller teams

Most family offices have 1 to 4 investment professionals. Research frameworks must respect limited bandwidth.

* Set materiality thresholds. Do full-scale diligence only above certain check sizes or when exposure would exceed a set percentage of family wealth.

* Standardize a short-form memo for fast "no" decisions. Use a long-form memo only for serious contenders that clear initial filters.
