# Jobs to Be Done Research: A Practitioner's Field Guide

## Why JTBD research matters for deals, strategy, and product bets

Jobs to be done research is a structured method for understanding what customers actually hire a product to do, based on qualitative evidence from customer interviews and observation techniques, not internal opinions or feature wishlists. The JTBD framework helps identify customer needs in days, not months. It feeds directly into investment memos, market-entry decisions, and product roadmaps.

## The core JTBD concepts you actually need in research

Skip the academic genealogy. Here are the concepts that matter when you're doing real work.

**Job to be done.** The progress a customer is trying to accomplish in a specific situation, separate from any given product or feature. Customers hire products to make progress or solve problems in their lives. The purpose of JTBD is to uncover the functional, emotional, and social goals driving behavior.

Clayton Christensen created the [JTBD framework in 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jobs_to_be_Done) through his "Marketing Malpractice" article in HBR, later expanded in "Competing Against Luck." * Social: "Signal to my CFO that we use enterprise-grade tools."

Jobs to Be Done helps identify customer motivations across all three dimensions. JTBD encourages defining customer jobs rather than focusing solely on product features.

## A 7-step JTBD research process you can actually run

Here's a concrete, repeatable process. Each step notes when to use your own team versus an expert network like FieldSignal for sourcing.

**The seven steps at a glance:**

1. Scope

2. Recruit

3. Interview

4. Map jobs

5. Define outcomes

6. Quantify

7. Translate

This process works for early-stage validation (seed to Series A) and late-stage diligence (pre-IPO, PE roll-up). The differences are mainly in sample size and rigor. Document each step in a research log so insights are defensible to partners, ICs, or exec teams.

### Step 1: Define the decision you're informing and scope the jobs

JTBD research fails when the scope is fuzzy or tied to a vague "understand users" goal. Start with precision.

Write a 1-paragraph research brief that states the business decision and time horizon. Example: "Invest or pass on a US-based vertical AI HR tool in Q3 2026, with a 3-to-5-year hold period." This is your anchor.

List initial hypotheses about the main customer jobs. For instance: "Avoid non-compliance with new EU AI regulations" or "Reduce manual candidate screening time by 50%." These hypotheses get tested, not confirmed.

Include explicit out-of-scope areas. If you're studying HR buyers, exclude jobs for finance or legal personas unless they're directly in the purchase decision. Decide up front whether you care more about new adoption jobs (switching into the product) or ongoing usage jobs (renewal, expansion).

### Step 2: Recruit the right mix of customers and experts

You need actual customers and near-customers, not just internal SMEs. Real decision making and customer needs only surface from people who've lived the switching moment.

Companies can identify customer needs through interviews and surveys. For a typical mid-market SaaS JTBD study, target 15 to 25 interviews across this mix:

* Current users (both decision makers and day-to-day operators)

* Churned customers who left in the past 90 days

* Buyers who evaluated but passed

* Channel partners or integrators with cross-client visibility

Including both a CIO-level decision maker and the analysts who execute the work matters. Their jobs and desired outcomes differ. The entire user base isn't monolithic.

### Step 3: Structure JTBD interviews around moments of change

JTBD interviews focus on stories of switching or first adoption, not generic satisfaction questions. JTBD research aims to understand the timeline of decision making and customer struggles.

Center the discussion on specific events. "Tell me about when you realized your old fraud tool wasn't working" is better than "What do you like about your current tool?" Walk forward chronologically through the decision.

Core areas to cover in every interview:

1. **Trigger events.** What pushed them to start looking? (the "push" force)

2. **Existing solutions and workarounds.** What were they using before, including manual processes?

3. **Desired outcomes.** What did "better" look like in measurable terms?

4. **Anxieties and constraints.** What almost stopped them from switching?

5. **Decision tip.** What finally moved them to act?

Avoid pitching or leading. Keep interviews solution-agnostic so the customer jobs emerge from their words. This is how you create empathy with real user goal and dig deeper into underlying motivations, not confirm your own biases.

### Step 4: Map the jobs and break them into job steps

This step converts raw interview transcripts into a structured job map, not a feature wish list.

Write one plain-language main job per segment. Use customer language. Example: "Keep payroll accurate and compliant across 4 countries every month." That's the user goal. Think of it as building a jobs atlas for the market you're studying.

Break each main job into smaller job steps across the timeline:

| Job Step          | Example                                      | Key Dimension               |
|-------------------|----------------------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Detect issue      | "Notice a payroll discrepancy"               | Functional                  |
| Understand impact | "Figure out which entity is affected"        | Functional + Emotional      |
| Select response   | "Choose whether to fix manually or escalate" | Functional + Social context |
| Monitor results   | "Confirm next pay run is clean"              | Functional                  |
Tag each step with functional, emotional, and social context based on real quotes. "I was afraid of missing our board deadline" is emotional. "I didn't want to look like I picked an immature vendor" is social. These aren't extras. They often explain why customers stay with bad existing products or resist a new solution.

Create a simple table or one-page diagram for each segment that captures job steps, pain points, and current tools.

### Step 5: Turn job steps into measurable desired outcomes

This is where qualitative stories become operational metrics. JTBD can help develop need statements in days, not months.

Use a simple sentence pattern to standardize outcomes:

* "Minimize the time it takes to \[step\]"

* "Reduce the likelihood that \[undesired event\] occurs"

Convert ambiguous pain points into quantified desired outcomes. "Onboarding is slow" becomes "Reduce onboarding errors from 3% to below 0.5%." "Implementation takes forever" becomes "Cut median implementation from 90 to 30 days."

Group outcomes by theme: speed, accuracy, risk reduction, cost, emotional satisfaction. This reveals which dimensions matter most to each segment. Companies can identify 50 to 150 measurable desired outcomes per market. Desired outcomes can number over 100 in a single market when you include all job steps across segments. A tight list of 30 to 60 outcomes per major job is typical for serious task analysis work.

Avoid stuffing product-specific language into outcomes. "Improve our Salesforce integration" isn't an outcome. "Reduce time to reconcile CRM data with billing records" is.

### Step 6: Quantify importance and satisfaction where it matters

Not everything needs a survey. But when you're making a capital allocation or roadmap bet, quantified priorities beat gut feel.

Create a short survey asking target users to rate each desired outcome on importance and current satisfaction with existing solutions, using a consistent Likert scale (1 to 10 works fine). High-importance, low-satisfaction outcomes signal strong opportunities. This is the core of outcome driven innovation scoring.

[Survey research shows](https://www.koji.so/docs/survey-vs-interview) that 67% of respondents quit unfinished surveys due to fatigue, and average email survey response rates sit at 15 to 25%. Keep your survey tight: 20 to 30 outcomes maximum per instrument.

Example: in a 2026 B2B AI tool evaluation, users rate "trust in data lineage" as very important (9/10) and poorly satisfied (3/10). That gap points to a product or thesis angle worth pursuing. For time-constrained deals, approximate this step using relative ranking in interviews instead of full surveys.

### Step 7: Translate JTBD insights into product, GTM, and deal decisions

Research without decisions is slideware. Here's where jobs theory meets the real product development process.

Write 3 to 5 concise JTBD statements per segment. Format: "When \[situation/trigger\], the \[role\] wants to \[core progress\] so they can \[outcome\]." Example: "When closing Q4 books under tighter audit scrutiny, the controller wants to reconcile multi-entity accounts without overnight delays to avoid rework and audit flags."

A well-defined job statement keeps product teams focused on user needs during development. JTBD helps prioritize product development based on customer needs, not competitor mimicry.

**For product managers and product teams:** pressure-test every major roadmap initiative. Does it directly improve a high-importance, low-satisfaction outcome? If not, question why it's there. This is how you develop products that gain clarity on what matters.

**For PE/VC teams:** align investment theses around the specific jobs a target serves best. Make explicit in IC memos where competitors are weak on critical job steps.

**For GTM and marketing strategy:** marketers using JTBD write campaigns that address customers' desired outcomes and emotional states. Use job language in site copy, outbound scripts, and pitch decks. Frame the product around the customer experience of making progress, not a feature bucket. This gives potential customers a fresh perspective on why your product matters.

## Using JTBD to sharpen growth strategies and competitive intelligence

JTBD isn't just a UX or product tool. It's a lens on competition and growth that gives you a new perspective on your market.

Competition in JTBD includes any alternative that could fulfill the same job. That means manual workflows, spreadsheets, hiring an extra analyst, or simply doing nothing. These "non-consumption" alternatives often represent the largest share of the market.

The classic quarter inch drill and quarter inch hole example applies here: nobody wants a drill. They want a quarter inch hole. And some of them are using a hammer and nail instead. Map competing solutions and other solutions to the same customer jobs and desired outcomes, highlighting where alternatives overperform or underperform across job steps.

This mapping reveals white-space growth strategies. Can you bundle adjacent jobs into one workflow? Serve an underserved segment with unique constraints? JTBD provides clarity for strategic growth initiatives and product innovation. It's a great example of how reframing the market by jobs, not products, opens opportunities that feature-level analysis misses.

## JTBD research in practice: common mistakes and how to avoid them

Many teams try JTBD once, get abstract results, and give up. Here are the pitfalls that kill projects.

**Vague job statements.** Writing "delight users" or "drive growth" as a job is useless. Jobs need situational context. "When onboarding a new enterprise client in the first 14 days" is specific enough to act on. An innovative approach means grounding every statement in real stories from interviews.

**Jumping to solutions.** If your interviewer asks "Would you use this feature?" you're doing task analysis on your own product, not JTBD research. Keep the conversation solution-agnostic. Let the customer motivations and unmet needs emerge from their own words. As Bob Moesta puts it: ["bitching ain't switching."](https://usegavel.com/bob-moesta/switch-interview) Complaints aren't decisions. Focus on actual switching behavior.

**Only talking to happy customers.** If you ignore non-buyers and churned users, you only see your strengths. You miss the jobs you're failing and the customer insights that matter most. You need to promote empathy by hearing from people the product didn't work for, and dig deeper into why.

**No connection to decisions.** JTBD findings that don't tie back to metrics, deal theses, or roadmap priorities produce nice decks that gather dust. Every JTBD project should end with explicit recommendations: invest here, cut this, message that.

**Guardrails that help:**

* A 1-page discussion guide shared before interviews start

* A shared coding schema (job, outcome, force, emotion) for transcripts

* One owner responsible for writing final JTBD statements and connecting them to the decision at hand

* "Reduce the likelihood that \[undesired event\] occurs"

Convert ambiguous pain points into quantified desired outcomes. "Onboarding is slow" becomes "Reduce onboarding errors from 3% to below 0.5%." "Implementation takes forever" becomes "Cut median implementation from 90 to 30 days."

Group outcomes by theme: speed, accuracy, risk reduction, cost, emotional satisfaction. This reveals which dimensions matter most to each segment. Companies can identify 50 to 150 measurable desired outcomes per market. Desired outcomes can number over 100 in a single market when you include all job steps across segments. A tight list of 30 to 60 outcomes per major job is typical for serious task analysis work.

Avoid stuffing product-specific language into outcomes. "Improve our Salesforce integration" isn't an outcome. "Reduce time to reconcile CRM data with billing records" is.

### Step 6: Quantify importance and satisfaction where it matters

Not everything needs a survey. But when you're making a capital allocation or roadmap bet, quantified priorities beat gut feel.

Create a short survey asking target users to rate each desired outcome on importance and current satisfaction with existing solutions, using a consistent Likert scale (1 to 10 works fine). High-importance, low-satisfaction outcomes signal strong opportunities. This is the core of outcome driven innovation scoring.

[Survey research shows](https://www.koji.so/docs/survey-vs-interview) that 67% of respondents quit unfinished surveys due to fatigue, and average email survey response rates sit at 15 to 25%. Keep your survey tight: 20 to 30 outcomes maximum per instrument.

Example: in a 2026 B2B AI tool evaluation, users rate "trust in data lineage" as very important (9/10) and poorly satisfied (3/10). That gap points to a product or thesis angle worth pursuing. For time-constrained deals, approximate this step using relative ranking in interviews instead of full surveys.

### Step 7: Translate JTBD insights into product, GTM, and deal decisions

Research without decisions is slideware. Here's where jobs theory meets the real product development process.

Write 3 to 5 concise JTBD statements per segment. Format: "When \[situation/trigger\], the \[role\] wants to \[core progress\] so they can \[outcome\]." Example: "When closing Q4 books under tighter audit scrutiny, the controller wants to reconcile multi-entity accounts without overnight delays to avoid rework and audit flags."

A well-defined job statement keeps product teams focused on user needs during development. JTBD helps prioritize product development based on customer needs, not competitor mimicry.

**For product managers and product teams:** pressure-test every major roadmap initiative. Does it directly improve a high-importance, low-satisfaction outcome? If not, question why it's there. This is how you develop products that gain clarity on what matters.

**For PE/VC teams:** align investment theses around the specific jobs a target serves best. Make explicit in IC memos where competitors are weak on critical job steps.

**For GTM and marketing strategy:** marketers using JTBD write campaigns that address customers' desired outcomes and emotional states. Use job language in site copy, outbound scripts, and pitch decks. Frame the product around the customer experience of making progress, not a feature bucket. This gives potential customers a fresh perspective on why your product matters.

## Using JTBD to sharpen growth strategies and competitive intelligence

JTBD isn't just a UX or product tool. It's a lens on competition and growth that gives you a new perspective on your market.

Competition in JTBD includes any alternative that could fulfill the same job. That means manual workflows, spreadsheets, hiring an extra analyst, or simply doing nothing. These "non-consumption" alternatives often represent the largest share of the market.

The classic quarter inch drill and quarter inch hole example applies here: nobody wants a drill. They want a quarter inch hole. And some of them are using a hammer and nail instead. Map competing solutions and other solutions to the same customer jobs and desired outcomes, highlighting where alternatives overperform or underperform across job steps.

This mapping reveals white-space growth strategies. Can you bundle adjacent jobs into one workflow? Serve an underserved segment with unique constraints? JTBD provides clarity for strategic growth initiatives and product innovation. It's a great example of how reframing the market by jobs, not products, opens opportunities that feature-level analysis misses.

## JTBD research in practice: common mistakes and how to avoid them

Many teams try JTBD once, get abstract results, and give up. Here are the pitfalls that kill projects.

**Vague job statements.** Writing "delight users" or "drive growth" as a job is useless. Jobs need situational context. "When onboarding a new enterprise client in the first 14 days" is specific enough to act on. An innovative approach means grounding every statement in real stories from interviews.

**Jumping to solutions.** If your interviewer asks "Would you use this feature?" you're doing task analysis on your own product, not JTBD research. Keep the conversation solution-agnostic. Let the customer motivations and unmet needs emerge from their own words. As Bob Moesta puts it: ["bitching ain't switching."](https://usegavel.com/bob-moesta/switch-interview) Complaints aren't decisions. Focus on actual switching behavior.

**Only talking to happy customers.** If you ignore non-buyers and churned users, you only see your strengths. You miss the jobs you're failing and the customer insights that matter most. You need to promote empathy by hearing from people the product didn't work for, and dig deeper into why.

**No connection to decisions.** JTBD findings that don't tie back to metrics, deal theses, or roadmap priorities produce nice decks that gather dust. Every JTBD project should end with explicit recommendations: invest here, cut this, message that.

**Guardrails that help:**

* A 1-page discussion guide shared before interviews start

* A shared coding schema (job, outcome, force, emotion) for transcripts

* One owner responsible for writing final JTBD statements and connecting them to the decision at hand
