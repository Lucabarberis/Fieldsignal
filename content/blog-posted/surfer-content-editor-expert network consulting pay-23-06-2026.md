## How expert network consulting pay works

Expert networks connect clients with subject matter experts for paid calls, paid surveys, and sometimes workshops or longer projects. Engagements can range from quick calls to long-term projects and workshops. The most common model is a one hour call billed per hour with a clear minimum.

Here's how the pay flow works:

1. Expert registers, gets vetted, and sets their own rate.

2. Client requests a match. The network suggests profiles.

3. Expert is selected, schedules the call, and completes compliance screening (no MNPI, no confidential data, conflict disclosures).

4. Expert completes the call or survey.

5. After compliance clearance, the network pays the expert, typically by bank transfer or prepaid card within 7 to 30 days.

The client pays the network a per call fee. The network passes a fixed rate honorarium to the expert. The margin sits between the client price and expert fee. Most networks don't disclose that spread to the expert. Project-based fees are often used for longer engagements covering the entire scope of work.

Concrete role examples:

* Product manager at a Series B SaaS company

Three ways to increase your rates at this stage:

* Tighten your one-paragraph bio to focus on specialized knowledge you use daily.

* Focus on narrow process or tooling questions where you're the daily user, not a generalist.

## What drives expert network hourly rate differences

Four factors drive most variation in expert network pay per hour, regardless of which platform you work with: seniority, niche or technical depth, urgency of the project, and geography or regulatory complexity.

Consulting firms, private equity firms, and corporate strategy teams will pay top dollar when an expert can shorten time-to-answer on high-stakes questions. Note that the same expert may see offers from different networks that vary by 20 to 40 percent based on how each network prices its own margin.

### Technical and niche expertise

More technical and regulated domains command higher rates. Specialized knowledge in technical fields commands higher fees because mistakes cost more and timelines are tighter. The scarcity of niche expertise directly impacts hourly rates.

* Interventional cardiology devices

* IVD diagnostics reimbursement

* EU MDR compliance for Class III medical devices

* Oncology drug trial operations

* Core banking modernization

Sectors with high complexity such as healthcare, finance, technology, and energy pay higher expert compensation. Experts who connect technical details to commercial outcomes (pricing, adoption curves, churn risk) sit at the top of each band. Physicians and specialists in regulated industries consistently command higher rates. Frame your bio around specific tools, standards, and decision types you own.

### Urgency, project size, and influence pay

Urgent projects can incur rush fees for experts. "Rush" requests for calls within 24 to 48 hours or over weekends often carry a premium of 10 to 30 percent over your standard hourly rate. Engagements requiring significant preparation yield higher payouts.

For example, a PE fund racing to validate a medical devices roll-up thesis in under a week may pay $800+ per hour for multiple senior operators across procurement, R&D, and sales. Very large deals, such as pre-IPO work or 9-figure acquisitions, push buyers to accept higher pay per call because the influence pay on their decision is material.

You can ask the network contact whether a project is flagged as urgent before quoting your fee.

### Region and market familiarity

Location affects pay. North American consultants earn more, typically 10 to 30 percent above equivalent roles in other regions, because many buyers and businesses sit in these markets. Consultants in major hubs can command higher rates due to localized market norms. Rates are often higher for experts in major financial hubs like New York, London, and Hong Kong.

For some projects, clients pay premiums for on-the-ground knowledge in emerging markets where data is thin. Hospital procurement guidance in Southeast Asia or distributor dynamics in Latin America are good examples. Bilingual experts, especially in regulated categories, often command higher rates due to the small number of qualified people.

Don't undersell yourself because you're outside the US or EU. What matters more is access to scarce, current information about specific companies and markets.

## Setting your expert network hourly rate: a step-by-step approach

Here's a concrete process any professional can use to set a 2026 hourly rate.

1. Map your seniority and function. Where do you fall in the three bands above?

2. Benchmark against the ranges in this article. A director-level role in a common SaaS category starts around $400.

3. Add a premium for scarce niches or urgent availability. If you're one of a small number of people who can speak to a specific topic, price accordingly.

4. Set a floor rate you won't go below. This is your fixed rate minimum.

5. Revisit your rate after 5 to 10 successful calls. Strong feedback gives you room to raise it.

For example, a senior product leader in B2B SaaS might land on $500 per hour as their standard consultation rate. A clinical operations lead in medical devices with understanding of EU MDR might land on $650 per hour. Keep one consistent "standard" rate across networks and only flex upward for rush or complex assignments.

### Common mistakes when pricing your time

* Underpricing relative to your seniority. This signals inexperience to sophisticated buyers, especially hedge funds and PE funds that compare experts side by side.

* Accepting the first rate suggestion from a network without question. Always ask, "What do similar profiles typically charge for this type of call?"

* Having different rates across networks with overlapping clients. Professionals who quote $300 on one platform and $500 on another create confusion.

* Ignoring preparation time in your mental math. If a call requires two hours of prep, factor that into your floor.

* Treating lower rates as a way to get more volume. Most networks reward depth and quality over low cost.
