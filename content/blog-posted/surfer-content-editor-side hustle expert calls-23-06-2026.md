# Side Hustle Expert Calls: How to Get Started

Few side hustles pay $300 to $500 per hour for knowledge you already have. Side hustle expert calls are exactly that: short, paid consulting calls where you answer questions about your industry for investors, strategy teams, and consultants willing to pay for your expertise.

This guide covers how the process works, what you can realistically earn, how to get started this week, and how to avoid the compliance mistakes that get people kicked off platforms.

## What side hustle expert calls are (and how much you can actually earn)

Side hustle expert calls are one-hour consulting calls arranged through expert networks. You get on the phone or Zoom with a private equity associate, a hedge fund analyst, or a corporate strategy team. They ask you specific questions about your industry. You answer. You get paid.

Expert network calls typically last about one hour. Hourly rates for expert consultations range from $100 to $5,000, depending on how rare your knowledge is and who's asking. For most mid-career professionals, the realistic range is $150 to $500 per hour. C-level executives can charge over $1,000 per consulting call.

## How expert networks work behind the scenes

An expert network is a company that recruits experts, vets them for compliance, and arranges paid consulting calls between those experts and paying clients. Expert networks arrange over 1 million calls annually across the expert network industry. Over 140 expert network companies exist globally, and the [industry generated roughly $3 billion in annual revenue by 2025](https://legalclarity.org/what-are-expert-networks-definition-and-legal-risks/).

Platforms connect individuals with professionals for personalized paid consultations. Here's how the process works from your side:

1. **Profile creation.** You register with one or more expert network firms. You provide your work history, specialization, and relevant details. Some networks pull from your LinkedIn profile.

2. **Being shortlisted.** When a client needs someone with your background, a project manager contacts you with a brief description of the topic and a few screening questions.

3. **Screening.** You answer questions like "Do you have recent experience with X?" or "Which competitors did you evaluate?" Sometimes there's a short phone vetting call.

4. **The consulting call.** You join a 30 to 60 minute phone or Zoom session. It's narrow Q&A, not a presentation. Some projects involve paid surveys, diary studies, or short written deliverables instead.

5. **Getting paid.** After the call, you get paid your agreed rate. The network bills the client a higher amount and keeps the difference.

Expert network companies make money by charging clients a [markup over the expert's hourly rate](https://insights.woozleresearch.com/expert-network-pricing-explained-what-investment-professionals-actually-pay-for-primary-research/). This covers matching, compliance infrastructure, recruiting, and scheduling. The expert network business model depends on volume and quality of matches.

Expert networks require compliance training before participation. Every network runs conflict checks and requires you to confirm you won't share material non-public information. Expert networks impose strict compliance requirements post-scandals involving insider trading cases in the late 2000s and early 2010s.

## Are you a good fit for expert network consulting calls?

Companies buy proven solutions, not impressive credentials. What matters for expert network consulting is narrow, recent, commercial experience. A title like "VP of Sales" is less useful than "managed B2B SaaS sales pipeline tools, US mid-market, 2020 to 2024."

Here are the kinds of people who regularly get consulting calls:

* Product managers at B2B SaaS firms with recent feature launches or pricing decisions

* Mid-level pharma marketing managers or hospital procurement leads

* Former Big 4 consultants who've moved into industry with hands-on domain knowledge

* Ex-FAANG engineers who worked in AI/ML, cybersecurity, or semiconductors

* Regional sales leaders with specific sector and geography experience over several years

* Healthcare professionals are in high demand within expert networks, especially those with deep knowledge of reimbursement, payor mix, or regulatory changes

Clients want concrete data. They're asking about real pricing anecdotes, customer adoption patterns, competitive comparisons, vendor selection criteria, and deal-cycle details. They don't want theory. Consultation sessions should be targeted brainstorming and validation sprints, not abstract conversations.

**Self-diagnostic checklist:**

* Have you influenced or owned a decision with a six- or seven-figure dollar amount (sale, vendor selection, procurement)?

* Do you have recent (past 3 to 4 years) experience in a specialized niche that's commercially relevant?

* Can you clearly name competitors or alternatives you evaluated or worked with?

* Do you have exposure to buyer criteria, budgeting cycles, or cost structure in that domain?

* Are you conflict-free and able to speak about your domain without violating NDAs?

If you said yes to most of those, you're a strong candidate. Define concrete metrics before starting a paid consulting package so you can track whether this side income is worth your time.

## Getting started: profiles, outreach, and your first expert network

You can get your first screening invite within a week. Here's the plan:

1. **Clean up your LinkedIn profile.** Make your role, industry expertise, achievements, and specialization obvious. A strong LinkedIn profile increases visibility to expert networks. Many experts get their first call because a project manager found them via a Google search or LinkedIn search, not because they applied.

2. **Articulate 2 to 3 tight expertise themes.** Pick focus areas where you can credibly say you've done recent work: a product domain, a function, a geography. Identify your niche focusing on industry experts for consultations.

3. **Register with 3 to 5 expert networks.** Start with a mix of big names and boutique firms. Professional networks help find specialists in consulting or coaching, so make yourself findable.

4. **Respond quickly to the first screening invite.** When an invite hits your inbox, answer within a few hours. Speed matters more than perfection.

### Polishing your LinkedIn profile for expert network visibility

Your LinkedIn profile is your storefront for this side hustle. Here's what to fix:

* **Headline.** Make it keyword-rich and specific. Example: "Director of Revenue Operations | B2B SaaS | Salesforce / HubSpot | US Mid-Market." Don't write "Seeking new opportunities."

* **About section.** State what you've done: domain, outcomes, scale, geography. Use numbers. "Scaled ARR from $2M to $12M" or "managed procurement budgets worth $15M annually."

* **Experience section.** Flesh out recent roles with specific vendor names, product names, customer segments, deal sizes, and regional focus. Expert network project coordinators search for this level of detail.

* **Skills.** Include tools, sectors, functions: "enterprise SaaS pricing," "medical device regulatory," "AI feature strategy."

* **Featured.** Add articles, presentations, or anything public that validates your claims. Check for professionals who publish case studies and testimonials, and consider doing the same.

## How to get picked for well-paid consulting calls

Once you're in an expert network database, getting selected depends on two things: how you answer screening questions and how clearly your niche shows up in your profile. Here's a mini-playbook:

* **Respond fast.** If you get an invite, reply within a few hours. Networks and clients favor availability. Being interested and responsive puts you ahead of 80% of experts.

* **Write in full sentences.** Don't just check boxes. If the form asks "which competitors," list real names. If it asks "what was your role," describe specifics: metrics, timeline, budget, market.

* **Echo domain keywords.** If the call is about "industrial automation in North America," use that exact phrasing in your answer. Make it obvious you're the right match.

* **Be honest about conflicts.** If you worked for or with a company involved in the research, declare that upfront. It's easier than getting rejected after you've committed.

* **Expect a modest hit rate.** Many experts get staffed on roughly 1 out of 4 or 1 out of 5 invites. You'll need volume and persistence. Don't get discouraged when the first few don't convert.

* **Decline irrelevant projects gracefully.** Saying "this isn't my area, but here's what I do know well" builds trust with project managers over time. It also reduces compliance risk.

Most calls go to experts who respond quickly with specific, relevant answers. That's the whole game.

### Positioning your niche so project managers notice you

Generic labels don't get you picked. Sharp, monetizable descriptions do.

* **Before:** "Operations Manager in logistics." **After:** "US East Coast regional operations lead for 3PL cold-chain, 2019 to 2024."

* **Before:** "Healthcare strategy consultant." **After:** "Payer mix shifts and vendor selection for outpatient imaging, 2021 to 2024."

Pick 2 to 3 themes to emphasize across every profile and screening form. Expert network consultants who get the most calls tend to have precise, recent, and slightly obscure specialties. They match what private equity firms and corporate strategy teams are trying to verify.

People with similar experience in a niche that's actively seeing M&A activity or regulatory change will get more invites than generalists with impressive titles.

### Handling compliance, NDAs, and insider trading risk

Almost every expert network call starts with a compliance script. You'll confirm you won't disclose material non-public information, trade secrets, or anything under NDA. Calls are monitored to prevent inappropriate disclosures. This isn't optional.

Here's what you must avoid:

* Undisclosed M&A deals or preliminary deal terms

* Unannounced pricing changes or future product launches

* Internal win-loss reports, board presentations, or strategic plans not publicly available

* Any information covered by an NDA with a current or former employer

* Experts must avoid discussing confidential company information, period

Sharing non-public information can lead to permanent removal from networks. Getting flagged kills your side hustle across multiple platforms because networks do communicate.

Expert network companies and their clients rely on [mosaic theory](https://legalclarity.org/what-are-expert-networks-definition-and-legal-risks/), using only public sources and experiential insights to avoid insider trading issues. If a client pushes into restricted areas, explicitly say you can't share that information and redirect to what you can discuss. Nothing you share should move a stock price.

## Setting your expert network hourly rate (without underpricing yourself)

Most expert networks recommend starting rates of $150 to $300 per hour. Expert network consulting rates start at $100 per hour for very junior or generalist profiles. Hourly rates can reach up to $5,000 for top experts with extremely rare knowledge.

Here's how to think about pricing:

* **Starting out.** If you're doing your first call, $150 to $300 per hour is reasonable. Don't undercut yourself just to get volume.

* **Mid-career specialist.** A regional sales manager at a mid-market SaaS company with 2020 to 2024 experience might command $400 to $700 per hour. Experts can earn $300 to $500 per hour on average across [most verticals](https://expertstackhub.ai/benchmarks).

* **Rare or senior profiles.** A hospital pharmacy director, a former product lead at a fintech unicorn, or someone with deep knowledge of a regulated domain can charge $800 to $1,500+ per hour. Experts can charge $1,000 per hour for specialized knowledge.

* **How billing works.** Expert network firms bill clients by the minute but quote everything as an hourly rate. Your pay is calculated on the actual minutes you spend on consulting calls.

* **Raising your rate.** After 3 to 5 successful calls with positive feedback, bump your hourly rate by $50 to $100 for new projects. Inform existing networks of the change. Many coaches offer a free or low-cost discovery consultation, but you shouldn't. Your time has value.

* **Don't compete on price.** Slashing rates rarely drives more demand. Specificity of expertise and response speed matter more than being the cheapest expert per hour. Being cheapest rarely helps in this market.

### Factoring in unpaid time and no-shows

Your posted rate isn't your effective rate. Here's what eats into it:

* Pre-call questionnaires, compliance training, and reading briefs can take 15 to 30 minutes per call. Most of this time is unpaid.

* Client cancellations happen. Some networks compensate for no-shows. Many don't.

* Screening invites that don't convert still cost you time. If you respond to 5 invites and get staffed on 1, you've spent 30+ minutes on responses that didn't pay.

Mentally halve the advertised hourly rate when deciding if this side hustle is worth your time at your current career stage. If the halved number still beats your alternatives, it's decent money.

Set a minimum rate threshold and decline lowball offers, especially if you already have a demanding full-time job. A side hustle expert can bypass years of trial and error by pricing correctly from the start.

## How to run a strong expert call (and get invited back)

A good conversation gets you invited back. A great one turns into a recurring consulting relationship. Here's the playbook for the 60 minutes before, during, and after:

* **Before the call.** Read the brief carefully. Scan recent earnings transcripts, press releases, and trade news so you can reference specific events from the past year. Experts should prepare organized information for calls to succeed.

* **First 3 to 5 minutes.** Compliance script and introductions. Ask for or confirm the agenda. "Do you want pricing or competitive dynamics first?" This sets the tone.

* **During (45 minutes).** Give concrete, opinionated answers. Name competitors, pricing bands, customer segments, conversion rates. Use examples from your own work. Experts provide actionable strategies and illuminate hidden blind spots that public reports can't. If you don't know something, say "I don't know." Clients respect honesty.

* **Wrap-up (5 to 10 minutes).** Summarize the key point or two you think matters most. Suggest 2 to 3 implications or risks. This is the part clients remember.

* **After.** Follow any required process. Sign off on transcripts if asked. If allowed, share relevant public references you cited.

Use AI tools to validate product ideas and optimize business setup for your own research prep before calls. Scanning recent news and analyst reports takes minutes and makes you sound current.

### What clients actually ask about in 2024 to 2026

Private equity investors, corporate M&A teams, and consulting firms are asking about specific themes right now:

* Pricing power under inflation: can companies pass cost increases to customers, or are margins compressing?

* Supply chain shocks from 2022 to 2024: which disruptions persisted and which resolved?

* AI feature launches post-ChatGPT: how are product teams embedding generative AI, and what's the competitive pace?

* Labor shortages and talent retention, especially in skilled trades and healthcare

* Payor and reimbursement shifts in healthcare, plus regulatory uncertainty

* Vendor consolidation: how do mergers affect switching costs and innovation?

A private equity associate or corporate strategy team uses your answers to build a commercial "mosaic" alongside public filings, industry reports, and customer surveys. They combine your perspective with other topics and data points. Occasionally summarize what you've said ("If I had to rank the top 3 risks for this product in 2025, they'd be...") to make their notes easier.

### Red lines: what not to say on an expert network call

Here are specific examples of forbidden disclosures:

* Unreleased pricing sheets for future launches

* Internal win-loss reports or competitive battlecards

* Confidential SLAs or contract terms with specific customers

* Preliminary board presentations or draft strategic plans

* M&A deal terms that haven't been publicly announced

* Product features still in development and not yet disclosed

If a client pushes into restricted areas, say explicitly: "I can't share that. I can speak to what's publicly available or what I personally experienced." No ambiguity. No hinting.

Getting flagged for a compliance issue can get you removed from a network. Most networks share compliance flags. One mistake can kill this side income across multiple platforms.

## Where expert calls fit in your broader career and income mix

Expert calls are usually irregular and deal-driven. Demand spikes during M&A waves, major regulatory events, or when a sector suddenly gets "hot" with private equity firms or venture capital firms. They're better as supplemental side income than a primary job.

Realistic volume expectations:

* 1 to 3 calls per month per network is solid if your niche is in demand

* Some months you'll get several invites per week. Others, nothing.

* Being registered on more networks smooths out the inconsistency

Non-financial benefits are real:

* You hear how investors and acquirers think about your market

* You sharpen how you articulate your own industry expertise

* You pressure-test your own career assumptions and roadmap

* Some experts get job offers or advisory opportunities from these conversations

Risks to manage: don't let your current job suffer. Block calls early morning, late afternoon, or during clear PTO windows. If you're a consultant working for consulting firms, check your employment agreement for moonlighting restrictions. A thousand dollars per call isn't worth a conflict with your employer.
