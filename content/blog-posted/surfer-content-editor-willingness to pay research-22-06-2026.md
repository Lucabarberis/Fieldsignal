# Willingness-to-Pay Research: Methods for B2B Pricing

Willingness to pay is the maximum price a customer will pay for a specific product or service under defined conditions. In B2B, the goal of willingness to pay research is to turn that number into concrete pricing decisions: tier structure, list price, discount floors, and upsell pricing. It's not abstract market theory. It's the input that tells you whether your price is leaving money on the table or killing your win rate.

## What is willingness-to-pay research in B2B?

Willingness to pay (WTP) research differs from asking customers what they'd pay in an open-ended survey. Structured research methods tie responses to actual purchase behavior, defined product offers, and statistical designs that isolate trade offs. Willingness to pay reflects market demand for products, and measuring it properly means you can price with confidence rather than intuition.

For B2B SaaS, data tools, and services, WTP is often represented as a price range, not a fixed amount. It varies based on customer demographics and market conditions. A mid-market fintech buyer and an enterprise healthcare system don't share the same ceiling. Treating WTP as a single number averages out the variation you need to capture.

Willingness to pay influences pricing strategies and product development. Market demand is reflected in customers' willingness to pay. This article covers the core concepts (including marginal willingness to pay), the main research methods (Gabor-Granger, Van Westendorp, conjoint analysis, and pricing experiments), how to measure WTP in practice, and how FieldSignal plugs into this work with expert interviews and targeted surveys.

Here's how the three levels of pricing intelligence stack up:

* **No research**: You set prices based on gut feel, cost-plus, or copying a competitor. High risk of underpricing or losing deals you should win.

* **Light surveys**: You ask unstructured questions and get directional clues, but responses are prone to bias and lack the rigor to justify a pricing change.

* **Structured WTP research**: You use defined methods, precise targeting of decision-makers, feature trade offs, quantitative modeling, and behavioral validation. This is the standard for serious business decisions.

## WTP vs marginal willingness to pay: core concepts for pricing

Customers willingness to pay for an entire offer is different from what they'll pay for a specific upgrade, feature, or service level. The first is willingness to pay WTP. The second is marginal willingness to pay (MWTP), and it's what you need to design tiers and upsells.

* WTP in B2B context: the maximum amount a specific customer segment (e.g., US mid-market SaaS companies with 100 to 500 employees) would spend for a defined package under 2024 to 2026 market conditions. This sets your baseline list price or standard pricing tier.

* Marginal willingness to pay: the extra price a segment will pay for one specific enhancement. For example, if a data platform's base product costs $5,000 per month, the MWTP for SSO integration might be $1,200 extra per month for the enterprise segment, while a dedicated CSM might add $2,500. These values drive add-on and tier pricing.

* WTP can indicate which product features are worth pursuing. Understanding WTP guides product development towards market demand. WTP influences product feature prioritization in development. WTP data helps determine which products to develop or discontinue.

* MWTP is usually derived from conjoint analysis or discrete choice experiments, not simple surveys. These methods force respondents to make trade offs between feature and price combinations, revealing what they actually value.

* A demand curve is the aggregate of individual WTP values across a market. WTP research feeds directly into understanding market demand, but it's not the same as tracking units sold at a given price point. You still need revealed preference data to validate.

* Example: WTP for a "core analytics platform" might be $4,000 per month among mid-market buyers, while MWTP for "priority support with 4-hour SLA" might be $800 per month on top of that. The core price and the add-on price require different methods to estimate accurately.

## What drives B2B customers' willingness to pay?

WTP is segment-specific and time-sensitive. B2B buyers don't behave like anonymous consumers in retail studies. They operate under budget constraints, ROI mandates, internal politics, and compliance requirements. Willingness to pay varies based on customer demographics and context. You need to map these drivers before you try to measure WTP numerically.

* **Budget cycles and timing**: A Series B SaaS vendor in 2025 with fresh capital has higher WTP for market research than a bootstrapped startup in the same niche. End-of-fiscal-year budget pressure often increases willingness to buy.

* **ROI expectations**: Tools that show explicit ROI (cost savings, revenue uplift, risk avoidance) justify a higher price. A competitive intelligence service that demonstrates deal pipeline improvements commands more than one focused on qualitative reports.

* **Switching costs**: If adopting your product requires data migration, custom integrations, or compliance review, WTP must cover those internal costs. Perceived value goes up when switching pain is high.

* **Regulatory and compliance requirements**: Buyers in healthcare, financial services, and security will pay more for compliance documentation, audit support, and data protection. This shifts customers willingness to pay upward.

* **Competitive alternatives**: Fewer substitutes mean higher WTP. Commoditized markets push WTP down. Exclusive offers can increase WTP by 50%, so differentiation matters.

* **Customer segments within one company**: Different functions have different WTP for the same tool. A product team might pay based on research depth. RevOps cares about cost per seat. Corporate development will pay more for competitive intelligence supporting M&A. Different customer segments inside the same company can have very different ceilings.

* **Emotional connections and brand trust**: Emotional connections can drive 40-70% of B2B buying decisions. If the vendor is trusted, and risk is low (strong SLAs, legal protections, references), buyers will pay more. For research and data services, brand, product quality, and compliance certifications matter heavily.

* **Personalization and context**: Personalization can enhance customer willingness to pay. Context-Based Research accounts for fluctuations in WTP by providing detailed scenarios to respondents, which is why WTP varies depending on how you frame the offer. Dynamic pricing can adjust prices based on demand, and adding features can increase perceived value and WTP.

## Methods to measure WTP: stated-preference B2B techniques

Stated preference means you ask respondents directly, under controlled or designed scenarios, what they'd select or pay. Surveys are commonly used for qualitative WTP measurement. Surveys and focus groups collect qualitative feedback about consumer preferences and price expectations. The Contingent Valuation method asks people to report their WTP for hypothetical scenarios, forming the basis for several structured approaches.

For B2B pricing, [sample sizes of 100 to 500 are typical](https://peppereffect.com/blog/b2b-pricing-strategy?utm_source=openai), so design quality and tight targeting of decision-makers matter more than volume. ### Gabor-Granger pricing method for B2B

The Gabor-Granger pricing method presents a defined offer at a sequence of price levels and asks whether the respondent would buy at each. Direct questioning can gauge initial expectations of cost by asking specific price-related questions. The Gabor-Granger method identifies maximum prices customers will pay by building a simple demand curve from those responses.

* **Use in B2B**: Test a monthly per-seat price for your SaaS analytics tool among 150 qualified buyers in North America and Europe. Same bundle, different price points ($20, $30, $40, $50 per seat).

* **Advantages**: Easy to explain, quick to field, directly tied to yes/no purchase intent, gives clear curves for customers willingness to pay and market demand estimation. You can calculate willingness to pay at each price level.

* **Limitations**: Ignores product features and competitive context. Risks hypothetical bias. Weaker for complex offers with product bundles and contract terms. Doesn't tell you what specific customer values drive acceptance.

Here's how to measure WTP with this method: (1) Define the offer bundle clearly, including features, service level, and contract term. (2) Select 5 to 7 plausible price points based on competitive benchmarking and expert interviews. (3) Recruit qualified respondents in each target segment, prioritizing customer decision-makers. (4) Field an online survey asking whether the respondent would purchase at each price point. (5) Fit acceptance rates versus price to build demand and revenue curves. (6) Compute the revenue-maximizing price per segment by multiplying acceptance rate by price at each level.

### Van Westendorp's price sensitivity meter

The Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter uses four questions to establish an acceptable price range. Van Westendorp's method determines acceptable price ranges through surveys. It's been used since the 1970s and works well for subscription tools and services where buyers have reference frames.

The four questions ask: at what price is the product so cheap you'd doubt its quality, at what price is it a bargain, at what price is it getting expensive but still worth considering, and at what price is it too expensive. Answers are plotted to find the optimal price point and acceptable price corridor.

* **Where it works in B2B**: Categories where buyers have existing mental anchors, like CRM add-ons, dev tools, or research subscriptions in 2024 to 2026. It helps estimate consumer willingness before you invest in heavier methods.

* **Risks**: Hypothetical bias remains. Respondents tend to understate top-end WTP for mission-critical or compliance-sensitive services. Mental price anchors from past purchases skew results. An [empirical study by Müller, Heim, and Matthys](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356917840_Was_sind_Kunden_zu_zahlen_bereit_Ein_Vergleich_der_Open-Ended-_Gabor-Granger-_und_Van-Westendorp-Methode?utm_source=openai) found that Van Westendorp is good for predicting resistance points but incentive-aligned methods perform better against real spending data.

Example: In 2025, you survey corporate strategy teams on the acceptable monthly cost for expert calls. If many say "too expensive" above $2,000 and "bargain" below $500, the acceptable range might be $1,000 to $1,800, with an optimal point around $1,300. That guides your initial list price and discount strategy.

### Conjoint analysis and discrete choice for B2B pricing

Conjoint analysis tests feature-price trade offs to isolate the value consumers place on specific attributes. You present buyers with alternative packages (varying features and price) and ask them to pick one. Statistical analysis of those choices reveals part-worth utilities and marginal willingness for each feature. Conjoint analysis estimates WTP for individual product features, and conjoint analysis reveals valuable product features based on WTP.

Concrete B2B example: a 2026 pricing study for a data platform. Respondents choose between bundles varying by seat count (5, 25, 100), SLA response time (24h standard, 4h priority), integrations (none, 2, all), contract flexibility (annual, monthly), and different price points.

* **Outputs**: Estimated part-worths per attribute level, customer value for each feature, marginal willingness for add-ons (e.g., SSO at +$X/month, dedicated CSM at +$Y/month), and simulated market share at different price points. You get exact willingness data for each component.

* **Pros**: Captures trade offs. Supports scenario testing. Works well for designing a pricing tier structure and add-on menus. An [Artometrix case study](https://artometrix.com/en/work/b2b-saas-pricing?utm_source=openai) found that a saas company using conjoint-based tier redesign saw revenue per customer increase by roughly 31% and enterprise conversion rise by 45%.

* **Cons**: Requires expert design and statistical analysis. Can be time consuming and costly without experienced research partners. Needs careful sampling by customer segments, typically 300+ respondents per segment.

## Revealed-preference methods: using real behavior to validate WTP

Revealed preference means you watch what customers actually do instead of asking what they'd pay. WTP assessments combine direct feedback with real-world testing to determine pricing strategies. Data-Driven Approaches estimate what people will pay using historical sales data and analysis. Auction experiments gauge WTP by observing bidding behavior, acting like a highest bidder mechanism that reveals true maximum price thresholds.

* **A/B live pricing tests**: Offer different price points in real proposals or pricing pages to segments of your prospect pool. Measure conversion rate and deal velocity at each level.

* **Controlled discount experiments**: Randomly assign sales proposals with different list prices or discount floors. Observe which price levels close and which stall.

* **Win/loss analysis tied to price**: Use CRM data to model win probability as a function of quoted price, controlling for customer segment and deal size. Look for the price where win rate drops sharply.

* **Early-access or beta pricing**: Offer early customers a specific price, then measure renewal, expansion, and upsell behavior.

Example: In 2025, a SaaS vendor offers its Pro tier at $900/month to one matched prospect segment and $1,100/month to another. If the higher price group shows only a modest drop in win rate but significantly higher ARPU, the higher price is justified. If churn spikes, you overshot.

The strongest WTP programs pair stated-preference surveys with ongoing pricing experiments. Different methods reinforce each other. A conjoint study gives you the curves. Live tests tell you if those curves hold in the real world. Tracking WTP helps businesses adjust pricing strategies effectively.

## Designing WTP studies around customer segments and value

WTP research is meaningless without clear customer segments and documented value metrics. In B2B, deal sizes, use cases, and buyer sophistication vary widely. Average WTP across all customers hides both high-WTP and low-WTP groups, resulting in prices that fit nobody. You need to understand different demographics and specific pain points per segment.

Define 3 to 5 primary segments before measuring customers willingness to pay. These might be SMB vs. mid-market vs. enterprise, or fintech vs. healthcare vs. industrial clients. Each has a different WTP profile.

Value metrics are the variables that tie price to how target customers measure value: number of users, data volume, number of locations, or number of interviews per month in research services. These link directly to customer value and allow you to scale pricing with usage.

Here's a simple evaluation process: (1) Segment definition using CRM and customer data to identify primary customer types. (2) Qualitative interviews (FieldSignal expert calls) per segment to understand decision criteria, customer expectations, and perceived value. (3) Quantitative WTP survey by segment using one or more methods above. (4) Synthesis into segment-specific price bands or pricing tier structures.

Averaging across segments is the most common mistake. It leaves revenue growth on the table with high-WTP segments and loses deals with lower willingness groups.

## Key metrics to track when you apply WTP insights

WTP research only pays off if you track what happens after you change prices. Connect research outputs to a small set of key metrics that act as sanity checks on your assumptions.

* **Win rate by price band**: If win rate collapses above a certain price in 2024 to 2025 deals, your estimated WTP was too optimistic.

* **Average discount depth**: If discounts are consistently deep, your list price exceeds what customers perceive as fair.

* **ARPA/ARPU by segment**: Track average revenue per account to see if higher prices stick with the right segments.

* **Churn rate by cohort**: If customers on a new, higher price tier churn faster, you've overpriced relative to the customer satisfaction they experience.

* **Expansion revenue**: If upsell rates are low, marginal willingness for add-ons is likely mispriced.

* **Average contract value (ACV)**: The clearest measure of whether optimal pricing has improved revenue.

* **Sales cycle length**: Longer cycles at new prices may signal price sensitivity friction.
