# Growth Equity Research: A Practical Playbook

Growth equity research is the targeted analysis you run before and after a growth equity investment. It's not generic private equity due diligence. It's a structured process for building conviction on whether a proven, high-growth company can sustain its trajectory for the next 3 to 5 years.

Growth equity research analyzes high-growth private companies for capital expansion. The typical target company has $20M to $200M in revenue, a working product, and a clear path to scaling. Capital goes toward entering new markets, hiring, and operational improvements, not rescue financing.

Whether you're a PE/VC associate, a corp dev analyst, or a founder preparing for a raise, the primary goal is the same: answer whether growth is durable, whether the team can execute, and where the potential risks hide. The rest of this article is a practical playbook for doing exactly that.

## How growth equity differs from VC and traditional buyouts

Growth equity sits between venture capital and leveraged buyouts. Understanding where it falls on the spectrum changes everything about how you research a deal.

Early-stage VC and late stage vc funds invest in pre-revenue or early-revenue companies. Product-market fit is still being validated. The risk is binary: the idea works or it doesn't. Growth equity invests in established, high-growth companies, typically 5 to 15 years old, with proven business models and real customers. The risk is about scaling, not about whether the market exists. Traditional buyouts target mature companies with stable cash flow, use heavy debt to generate returns, and take controlling stakes to drive hard cost cuts.

Growth equity investments rarely use leverage. Investors typically acquire minority stakes in growth equity deals, often 15 to 40% ownership, with governance through board seats and protective provisions. Growth equity focuses on scaling operations and market expansion rather than financial engineering. Revenue growth rates typically target companies with over 25% year-over-year growth.

These features shift the research questions. You spend more time on revenue growth quality, unit economics, competitive intensity, and scalability. You spend less time on debt capacity and margin compression.


## The core questions your growth equity research must answer

Every diligence process needs to resolve a short list of high-signal questions. If you can't answer these clearly, you don't have enough conviction to invest.

* Is this growth real and repeatable over the next 3 to 5 years? What are the historic rates by cohort, product, and geography?

* How healthy is the revenue base? Recurring revenue models provide predictable cash flows, but you need to verify retention, expansion, and contraction at the cohort level.

* Customer Acquisition Cost and Lifetime Value analysis ensures sales efficiency. What's the CAC payback period? What's the LTV:CAC ratio?

* Scalability assesses if a business model can exponentially scale revenues. What operational challenges will appear when revenue doubles from here?

* Market Dynamics is a core pillar in evaluating growth equity opportunities. Who are the competitors, what's the pricing power, and how high are switching costs? Don't rely on top-down TAM slides from the CIM.

* Product-Market Fit verifies that a product solves real customer problems. Is the product still differentiated, or are "good enough" alternatives emerging?

* Founder Retention determines if key founders remain involved post-investment. Will the management team stay motivated and aligned?

* Exit Route Analysis focuses on identifying potential liquidity events. Common exit strategies include IPOs, strategic acquisitions, and secondary sales. Is there a credible path to an eventual exit?

You need a deep understanding of all these areas before writing the [IC memo](/blog/investment-memo-template-how-pe-and-vc-investors-structure-them).

## Designing a growth equity diligence plan and checklist

Due diligence assesses financial, operational, and legal aspects. But a growth equity diligence checklist isn't a generic buyout "everything list." It's built around the questions above, organized into workstreams with clear owners and deadlines.

The investment lifecycle includes sourcing, value creation, and exit stages. Your diligence plan covers the middle stretch. Structure it into 4 to 5 workstreams:

* Commercial and market diligence: market sizing, competitive positioning, pricing dynamics, customer behavior.

* Financial due diligence: revenue quality, margins, cash flow, unit economics, scenario modeling.

* Operational and people diligence: org depth, sales efficiency, hiring plans, process maturity. The due diligence process includes evaluating the management team.

* Technology and product diligence: product roadmap, technical debt, infrastructure scalability, security posture.

* Legal due diligence: contracts, IP ownership, regulatory exposure, terms that limit future growth.

Plan a 3 to 5 week sprint. Assign each workstream an owner, define weekly deliverables, and set decision gates before IC. Growth equity investments target companies with proven business models, so your job is to validate, not discover.

## Executing commercial and market research for growth equity

[Commercial diligence](/blog/commercial-due-diligence-a-practical-guide-for-pe-vc-and-corporate-buyers) is where you build or break conviction on future growth. Start with the market, then drill into the target company's position within it.

* Size the market with real numbers. Growth equity firms often use industry reports for market insights. In 2025, [US growth equity activity rose to $234 billion](https://www.rothschildandco.com/en/newsroom/insights/2026/01/ga_growth_equity_update_edition_46/), more than double the prior year, with AI-related deals driving roughly 57% of that value. Europe saw 633 deals raising $49.2 billion. Use these sector benchmarks to pressure-test the "reported TAM" from the target's pitch deck.

* Market analysis includes examining competitors and industry trends. Map 5 to 10 direct competitors. Assess pricing models, feature parity, and go-to-market motions.

* Examine new markets explicitly: is the company expanding into Europe, APAC, or adjacent verticals? What's the track record? Many firms have stumbled on localization, regulatory, and distribution issues in unfamiliar markets.

* Run 10 to 20 targeted expert calls or short surveys to understand how customers buy, why they churn, and how the target compares to alternatives.

Growth equity investments are expected to reach $413.2 billion in 2024, and 151 startups were acquired in Q1 2024 alone. Market trends move fast. Your research must be current.

### Customer and revenue quality analysis

Revenue durability is the single most important thing you're testing. Growth equity targets companies with strong revenue growth rates, but you need to know whether that growth is healthy.

* Break revenue down by cohort, segment, and channel. Track retention, expansion, and contraction over at least 3 to 5 years.

* Key metrics: [net revenue retention (NRR)](https://www.growthspreeofficial.com/blogs/b2b-saas-nrr-grr-net-gross-revenue-retention-benchmarks-2026-by-acv-stage-vertical) for growth-stage B2B SaaS sits at a median of roughly 108%, with top quartile above 125%. Gross Revenue Retention (GRR) should be above 90% for companies over $25M ARR. Best performers hit 95 to 97%.

* Track logo churn, upsell rates, sales cycle length, and CAC payback. Tie each metric to your investment decisions: a rising CAC payback signals trouble.

* Interview current and former customers to test satisfaction, switching intent, and perceived differentiation. This is a core use case for FieldSignal's expert network.

### Competitive and pricing dynamics

Competitive intensity determines how long growth potential lasts. You can't rely on the target's self-assessment.

* Build a structured competitive matrix: 5 to 10 named competitors, price points, features, GTM motions, and margins. Include large strategics that might enter.

* Watch for early signs of commoditization: high feature parity, open-source alternatives, frequent discounting. If customers say multiple vendors are "good enough," pricing power is eroding, and your competitive advantage thesis weakens.

* Use expert interviews with ex-sales leaders and ex-channel partners to learn actual discount levels, win/loss drivers, and upcoming rival product launches. These calls give you a clear picture of the real battlefield.

The expected exit value for growth equity is $413.2 billion in 2024. Strong competitive positioning protects that value at exit.


## Financial and operational due diligence tailored to growth equity

Financial and operational diligence in growth equity is different from a buyout. You're stress-testing growth plans, not optimizing debt structures.

### Building a focused financial due diligence workstream

Financial due diligence examines balance sheets and income statements, but in growth equity you go deeper into revenue quality.

* Review revenue by product, customer, and geography. Examine gross margin trends and unit economics at the customer and cohort level, not just the P&L summary.

* Reconcile KPI definitions between the data room and internal dashboards. Push for raw data. If the company's definition of "ARR" doesn't match yours, flag it.

* Build a light scenario model. Tie downside, base, and upside cases directly to your research findings on pricing, churn, and sales productivity. Don't assume straight-line growth from the management case.

* Assess financial health holistically: cash burn, cash conversion, seasonality, and revenue recognition policies all matter.

### Operational diligence and value-creation planning

Operational due diligence identifies opportunities for performance improvement. Connect findings to a first 180-day value creation plan.

* Key areas to examine: org chart depth, leadership gaps, sales coverage model, support SLAs, implementation capacity, and product roadmap realism.

* Operational support includes recruiting key executives for portfolio companies. If the company needs a VP Sales for North America or a stronger Head of Product, flag it as a Day 1 initiative.

* Growth equity firms often lack majority control to implement changes. That means your creation plan must be collaborative, not imposed. Translate findings into concrete priorities: "restructure partner program," "replatform core product within 24 months."

* Use interviews with former executives via an expert network to get an unvarnished view of culture, execution risk, and leadership quality.

* Common operational challenges in the current market include rising customer acquisition costs, talent retention for GTM leaders, and technical debt that slows feature delivery.

## Legal, technology, and risk-focused diligence for growth investments

Legal due diligence reviews contracts and regulatory compliance. While this workstream is similar across private equity, growth equity investors pay particular attention to terms that limit expansion into new markets or products.

* IP ownership: confirm that the target company owns its critical code and designs, especially where contractors or third parties contributed.

* Technology diligence: assess infrastructure scalability, security posture, data privacy exposure, and technical debt that could delay roadmap commitments. Many firms underestimate the cost of accumulated debt in their tech stack.

* Risk assessment: evaluate concentration risks. Dependence on a single cloud vendor, a major channel partner, or top 5 customers can kill a deal. Regulatory risks, including supply chain management compliance and data localization requirements, also need examination.

* Valuing minority stakes in private companies is complex. Protective provisions, liquidation preferences, and anti-dilution clauses all affect value and need legal review.

* For funds without in-house specialists, outside counsel and technical experts, combined with targeted expert calls, fill gaps without stretching timelines.

### Creating a pragmatic due diligence checklist

Checklists keep growth equity teams disciplined under tight timelines. Without one, something important gets missed.

Group your diligence checklist by workstream:

1. **Commercial:** Confirm data room completeness. Schedule 5 to 10 customer calls. Run independent market sizing. Build competitive matrix.

2. **Financial:** Get historic cohort retention data for 3 to 5 years. Review revenue by product, geography, customer. Stress-test growth assumptions.

3. **Legal:** Review top 20 contracts (vendors and customers). Confirm IP ownership. Check regulatory exposure.

4. **Technology:** Assess architecture scalability. Document technical debt. Review security and privacy posture.

5. **People:** Map org chart. Identify leadership gaps. Evaluate hiring plan realism.

Add a sign-off step: the deal team reviews each category and records go/no-go or mitigation plans before IC.


## Using expert networks and primary research to build conviction

Primary research is what separates informed decisions from educated guesses. In growth investing, the experts who matter most are: former customers (including churned ones), ex-employees in sales or product, systems integrators, resellers, and competitors' alumni. They hold deep expertise that no data room can replicate.

Here's a practical workflow:

1. Define 3 to 5 hypotheses you need to test (e.g., "discounting is increasing," "expansion into APAC will yield lower margins").

2. Draft 10 to 15 targeted questions.

3. Commission 8 to 15 expert interviews or short surveys.

4. Feed qualitative insights back into your financial model, competitive matrix, and IC memo.

## Putting it together: a repeatable growth equity research playbook

Growth equity investments typically involve minority ownership stakes in companies with untapped potential. The firms that generate returns consistently aren't smarter. They're more disciplined about how they research.

Growth equity investors need expansion capital decisions backed by evidence, not narrative. Private investments in high-growth companies demand a structured approach that covers everything from market position to balance sheets to strategic guidance for the management team.

Here's the playbook in five steps:

1. Define your thesis and the 5 to 7 questions that must be answered.

2. Design a diligence checklist tailored to the target company, organized by workstream.

3. Run commercial and financial workstreams in parallel, using primary research to accelerate growth of conviction.

4. Layer on legal and technology diligence to catch risks that don't show up in spreadsheets.

5. Synthesize findings into a value creation plan tied to specific initiatives and a timeline for the first 180 days.

Disciplined research, focused due diligence, and targeted expert input reduce surprises and improve returns in growth equity investments.
