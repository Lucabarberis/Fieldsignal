## How Chemical Industry Research Works

### Step 1: Define Your Research Scope

You start by specifying the decision you need to make. That determines everything else.

Pick your chemical segments: petrochemicals, specialty chemicals, fine chemicals, polymers, agrochemicals, pesticides, rubber, or advanced materials. Define the geography: global coverage or targeted to specific regions. Identify the questions: market entry feasibility, M&A due diligence, competitive analysis, regulatory risk, formulation alternatives, supplier risk, or sustainability strategy.

Then specify expert types. Decision-maker research targets key purchasing decision-makers and improves your ability to identify and reach the right contacts across a product line, while technical research requires former R&D chemists or process engineers. Supply chain studies need procurement managers and distributors. The more precise your brief, the faster and more relevant the expert recruitment.

### Step 2: Expert Recruitment and Screening

The network searches its database and works to maintain a current expert pipeline with direct, relevant chemical industry experience. For very niche roles (say, a catalysis specialist focused on specific polymer applications), external recruitment fills gaps.

Screening includes CV verification, employment history review, and conflict-of-interest checks. Experts must have departed their former employers beyond a buffer period (typically 6–12 months) if they held roles with access to strategy or non-public information. Compliance screening ensures the research process stays safe and compliant: MNPI rules, non-disclosure agreements, and non-compete clauses are all checked before a call is scheduled.

Typical timelines: recruiting a mid-senior technical expert or supply chain director takes 3–10 business days. Extremely niche or senior roles (former C-suite, rare specializations in advanced materials or biotech processes) may take 2–4 weeks.

### Step 3: Conduct Research and Deliver Insights

Research formats include phone or virtual interviews (typically one hour), focused surveys, and panel calls. Focus groups identify critical information about product pricing and competitive positioning. Surveys provide valuable insights into the big picture of market dynamics and buyer behavior.

Deliverables include transcripts, summaries, analysis, and actionable recommendations. Quality control involves cross-checking expert input against published data and technical reality. Market research includes customer insights and competitor analysis, giving you a complete view of your target segment.

Turnaround: 1–2 weeks for a few calls. Larger studies involving 10+ expert interviews, surveys, and multi-geography analysis typically take 3–6 weeks.

## What Makes Expert Networks Different from Traditional Research

The difference comes down to four things:

* **Primary vs. secondary data.** Expert networks give you firsthand information from people who did the work. Traditional reports process existing published data, often 12–18 months old. Data on market trends helps decision-makers identify opportunities and mitigate risks, but only if that data is current.

* **Technical specificity.** R&D scientists who've worked on specific formulations can compare alternative chemistries, assess scalability, and identify processing constraints. Desk research can't replicate lab or production experience. Life cycle inventory databases cover only a tiny fraction of organic chemicals, making many published LCAs incomplete. Experts fill those gaps.

* **Speed.** From briefing to insight, expert network projects deliver in days to weeks. A [case study from CamIn](https://www.camin.com/case-studies/diversification-for-oleochemicals) shows a large specialty chemicals manufacturer completed a full sustainable specialty chemical opportunity assessment in 4 weeks, screening hundreds of molecular families and application areas for $50,000. That was 5x faster than their internal team's projected timeline.

* **Flexible cost structure.** Traditional consulting firms and premium market reports cost tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars, often with annual retainers. Expert networks, particularly mid-tier and boutique options, offer pay-per-project or per-call pricing that makes primary research accessible to firms outside the Fortune 500.

Every expert undergoes conflict-of-interest screening, employment history verification, and MNPI training before a call is scheduled. Compliance scripts are used at the start of each call. Experts with ongoing financial or advisory ties to companies under discussion are excluded.
