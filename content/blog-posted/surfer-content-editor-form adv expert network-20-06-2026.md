## Form ADV Basics for Hedge Fund Managers

Form ADV is the uniform disclosure document under the [Investment Advisers Act of 1940](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/form-adv) that investment advisers file with the securities and exchange commission or state securities authorities. It's the primary disclosure document for any advisory firm providing investment advisory services, including hedge fund managers.

There's a key distinction between fully registered investment advisers and exempt reporting advisers. Most hedge fund managers fall into one of these two buckets:

* **SEC registered investment advisers**: Firms with over $100 million in assets under management must register with the SEC. These advisers file the full Form ADV, including Parts 1A, 2A, and 2B.

* **Exempt reporting advisers (ERAs)**: Hedge fund managers that qualify under Section 203(m) (private fund adviser exemption) or Section 203(l) (venture capital fund adviser exemption) under the investment advisers act rules file a limited version of Part 1A only.

Firms with $100 million AUM must register with the SEC. Below that threshold, investment adviser registration typically happens at the state level with state regulators. When a hedge fund manager's regulatory assets under management cross the SEC threshold, the registration process changes and more detailed form adv parts apply.

The SEC amended Form ADV on August 25, 2016, adding requirements for disclosures about separately managed accounts and other operational details. That amendment expanded the information advisers must provide about their advisory business.

Here are the main parts of Form ADV and what they cover:

| Part              | What It Contains                                                                                                                | Relevant for Hedge Funds?                                     |
|-------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------|
| Part 1A           | Structured data: identifying information, disciplinary history, assets managed, business operations, client types, affiliations | Yes, always                                                   |
| Part 1B           | State-registered adviser supplement                                                                                             | Only if state-registered                                      |
| Part 2A           | Narrative brochure: advisory services offered, fee structure, investment strategies, conflicts, business practices              | Yes, for SEC registered advisers                              |
| Part 2B           | Background on key advisory personnel and individual advisors delivering investment advice                                       | Yes, for SEC registered advisers                              |
| Part 3 / Form CRS | Client relationship summary for retail clients                                                                                  | Rarely, most hedge funds serve institutional advisory clients |
Part 1 includes identifying information and disciplinary history. Part 2A is a narrative brochure detailing services and fees. Part 2B provides background on individual advisors delivering advice. Investment advisers must also disclose social media accounts in Form ADV.

For institutional hedge fund advisers with no retail clients, Parts 1A and 2A carry the most weight. Part 3 (Form CRS) usually doesn't apply. These basics set the stage for understanding where expert network use must be disclosed.

## Which Parts of Form ADV Touch Expert Network Activity?

Form ADV doesn't use the phrase "expert network" anywhere. No check box says "Do you use expert consultants?" But several items clearly cover this research channel.

Here's how it maps at a high level:

* **Part 1A Items 5, 7, and 8**: Cover types of advisory services, methods of analysis, related persons and affiliations, and third-party research arrangements.

* **Part 2A Items 8, 10, 12, and 14**: Cover methods of analysis and investment strategies, other financial industry activities and affiliations, brokerage practices, and client referrals and compensation.

* **Form CRS**: Usually won't mention specific research vendors, but inconsistent language about conflicts between the client relationship summary and Part 2A can create exam issues.

When you reference expert network usage in your firm's form adv, use concrete SEC form item numbers and headings. Don't write vague descriptions like "we sometimes consult external parties." Instead, reference Item 5.G, Item 8.G, Item 10 in Part 2A, and similar specific locations. Examiners compare your detailed disclosures against what's actually happening in your research operation.

## Mapping Expert Network Use to Form ADV Part 1A

Part 1A is structured, check-the-box data. But several items can implicitly reveal expert network usage even if no vendor is named by name.

### Item 5.C and 5.D: Types of Clients and Advisory Services

These items ask about the types of clients you serve and the advisory services you provide. If your hedge fund uses primary research, one-on-one consultations, or expert consultations as a method for delivering its securities advice, these should be reflected here. Consultations typically last 30 to 60 minutes focusing on narrowly defined topics.

Failing to include these methods when expert calls are regular creates a mismatch that examiners will notice.

### Item 5.G: Other Advisory Services or Methods of Analysis

This is where advisers reference "channel checks, expert consultations, and other primary research." It covers the methods of analysis your advisory firm uses to make investment decisions.

If expert network calls are part of your research workflow, say so here. Networks provide access to specialized and niche knowledge not available through public reports. That makes them a material method of analysis. Expert networks connect clients with industry veterans and niche specialists for targeted insights. Investors utilize expert networks to validate their strategies and investment theses.

### Item 7.A: Related Persons and Affiliations

Most hedge funds don't own their expert network vendors. But if they do, or if experts are related persons, that affiliation must be reported. This also matters if expert vendors have compensation ties, referral relationships, or dual roles in your advisory business.

Even if there's no ownership, Item 7.A can flag arrangements where an expert network also provides investor introductions or other services that create conflicts. Control persons at the advisory firm should be aware of these overlapping relationships.

### Item 8.G: Soft Dollar Benefits and Research from Third Parties

This is critical. Item 8.G asks whether you receive research or services from third-party vendors in connection with securities transactions. Expert network payments, commission-paid research, and in-kind research support all fit here.

If you pay for expert consultations using brokerage commissions or soft dollars rather than direct management company funds, that arrangement must be disclosed. Even if expert network fees are paid as standard vendor invoices, they can be construed as "research products or services from third parties" under Item 8.G.

### Exempt Reporting Advisers and Part 1A

Even reporting advisers filing a limited Part 1A must answer many of these core items. Exempt reporting status doesn't hide expert network usage from regulators. ERAs complete Items 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 10, 11, and related sections. If expert networks are part of the research or trading process, relevant items still apply.

## Form ADV Part 2A: Disclosing Expert Network Conflicts and Controls

Part 2A is your narrative brochure. This is where hedge funds have to explain in plain English how they source ideas, manage conflicts, and control risk, including expert calls and channel checks.

### Item 8: Methods of Analysis and Investment Strategies

Item 8 requires you to describe your methods of analysis and investment strategies. If expert calls or external consultations are significant to how you generate investment ideas, describe them here. Include details like:

* How many expert calls you conduct (range, not exact count)

* What topics or sectors they cover

* Whether you use a vendor or source experts independently

* How calls are vetted and supervised

Companies can gain insights into customer experiences and market trends through expert networks. Investors use experts to conduct due diligence on potential acquisitions. These activities should be described as part of your research process.

You must also articulate material risks in this item. That includes the risk of receiving material nonpublic information (MNPI), overreliance on external data, and potential expert bias.

Here's a sample disclosure fragment:

> "We use expert network calls and industry participant interviews as a component of our methods of analysis. These are used alongside public data, financial modeling, and proprietary research. External sources augment our understanding but carry risk: experts may lack full transparency, or inadvertent disclosure of confidential or nonpublic information is possible."

### Item 10: Other Financial Industry Activities and Affiliations

If experts engaged through your network have past employment with issuers, or if the network vendor has relationships with issuers that create conflicts, Item 10 is where those affiliations must appear. Most expert networks operate strictly as facilitators and do not provide strategic recommendations, but the relationships they create still carry conflict potential.

### Item 12: Brokerage Practices

Item 12 covers brokerage practices, including soft dollar arrangements or brokerage commission arrangements used to pay for research or third-party services. If expert network research is financed via commissions, you need full disclosure here.

Sample language:

> "We pay for expert consultation services directly from management fee revenue. We do not use client brokerage commissions or soft dollars to finance expert vendor research."

If you do use soft dollars, describe the arrangement clearly.

### Item 14: Client Referrals and Other Compensation

If you're paying expert network vendors who also make referrals, or expert networks that have "dual roles" providing both research and investor introductions, those compensation flows must be disclosed here.

### Compliance Controls in the Brochure

Your Part 2A description should include a summary of your expert-call compliance program. Expert networks implement compliance protocols to prevent the sharing of non-public information. Your brochure should mirror that by describing:

1. Pre-screening of experts (checking prior confidentiality agreements, employer restrictions)

2. Pre-call compliance briefings or oral reminders

3. Training for investment staff on MNPI

4. Post-call review requirements and transcript/note archiving

5. Vendor due diligence standards

Make sure the language in marketing decks about "primary research" and "expert consultations" matches the detail and tone in Part 2A. If your pitch book says "proprietary primary research program" but your brochure says "we occasionally speak to industry participants," an examiner will notice.

## Exempt Reporting Advisers: Limited Form ADV, Real Exposure

### Who Qualifies as an ERA

Private fund advisers managing less than $150 million in assets under management in the United States can qualify for exempt reporting under Section 203(m) of the Investment Advisers Act. Venture capital fund advisers qualify under Section 203(l). These exemptions let managers avoid full [SEC registration](https://www.sec.gov/about/divisions-offices/division-investment-management/private-fund-adviser-overview) but still require a limited form adv filing.

### What ERAs File

Exempt reporting advisers file a truncated Form ADV Part 1A. This filing still covers core items about private funds, service providers, research methods, and potential conflicts. The expert network story plays out in Item 5, Item 7.B (covering other pooled investment vehicles and private fund details), and relevant Schedule D sections where service providers and conflicts are described.

ERAs do not file Part 2A brochures with the SEC. But many still create a brochure-like document for institutional investors that mirrors adv part 2A content. If expert network use is material and not disclosed in those investor-facing documents, that gap can trigger investor complaints or private actions, even if public disclosure via ADV is limited.

### The Growth Scenario

Here's a common pattern: a small hedge fund ERA starts with occasional expert calls, maybe a few per quarter. The fund grows. Assets managed increase. The firm crosses the SEC registration threshold. Now it must file a full form adv, including Part 2A.

At that point, everything in Part 1A and 2A needs to be revisited. Past disclosures must align with existing practices. If the firm has been running expert calls for two years without formal policies or disclosures, it has to retroactively build and document its compliance program before the initial form adv filing as a registered adviser.

This is why private fund advisers should build compliant expert network processes early, even before full registration requires it.

## Client Relationship Summary (Form CRS): When Expert Work Matters

Form CRS applies to sec registered investment advisers with retail clients. Part 3 was replaced by SEC Form CRS in June 2019, separating it from the main Form ADV. Form ADV Part 3 was separated into Form CRS in June 2019.

Most hedge fund complexes don't serve retail clients, so they don't file a client relationship summary. But some do, particularly those offering thematic funds accessible through wealth platforms or registered investment companies.

Form CRS is short and standardized. It won't name expert networks. But it must accurately capture the types of conflicts and third-party relationships already described in other ADV parts. If your Part 2A describes significant MNPI risk from expert calls but your Form CRS says nothing about third-party research conflicts, that inconsistency creates exam exposure.

If your hedge fund adviser relies heavily on expert calls for retail-facing investment strategies, that fact can influence the conflict language in the CRS. Investment companies and business development companies with advisory relationships face similar considerations.

For institutional-only hedge funds that don't file form crs, the key takeaway is consistency. Internal summaries of conflicts should match what's in Part 2A, even if Form CRS isn't filed.

## Filing Form ADV: Timing, Annual Updating Amendments, and Expert Networks

Expert network usage doesn't change the filing calendar. But it increases the chance that operational changes will trigger amendments between annual filings.

### The Basic Timeline

Filing form adv follows a clear schedule:

1. **Initial form adv filing**: Filed through the investment adviser registration depository (IARD) before advising existing clients or new clients. The SEC has 45 days to approve a new registration request. Advisers must provide Form ADV to clients at the start of the relationship.

2. **Annual updating amendment**: Investment advisors must update Form ADV annually. Advisers must file an annual updating amendment within 90 days of fiscal year-end. Investment advisers must submit annual updates to Form ADV.

3. **Other than annual amendment**: Filed promptly after material changes to the advisor's business, operations, or disclosures.

Form ADV must be filed through the IARD electronic filing system. Form ADV must be filed electronically through the IARD system. There's no paper option unless you qualify for a continuing hardship exemption.

Investment advisers must file SEC Form ADV annually. Form ADV amendments must be filed annually within 90 days of fiscal year-end. Investment advisers must update Form ADV annually within 90 days.

### Expert-Related Changes That Trigger Mid-Year Amendments

These changes can require you to amend form adv before the next annual updating amendment:

* Launching a large-scale expert interview program when you previously had none

* Switching from ad hoc expert calls to a systematic expert-panel product

* Adding a new expert network vendor with different compensation structures

* Expanding expert-call coverage to new jurisdictions or sectors with heavier MNPI risk

* Changing how expert costs are paid (moving from management company payment to fund pass-through or soft dollars)

Compliance teams should treat major changes to research workflows, expert network vendors, or compensation structures as triggers to review all relevant form adv items. When you submit filing updates, make sure the new form adv language reflects actual current practice.

Exempt reporting advisers also file annual updating amendments and must keep their truncated form adv part 1A consistent with current expert-research practices. A new form adv response isn't just a box-checking exercise. It's an initial report on how the business actually operates.

## Common Form ADV Pitfalls for Hedge Funds Using Expert Networks

Regulators now routinely compare hedge fund marketing claims, expert network invoices, and form adv disclosures during exams. Here are the pitfalls that create problems.

### Understating the Role of Expert Research

The most common mistake: calling expert calls "a small supplement" in the brochure when trading records show that expert consultations drive most idea generation. If your portfolio managers are on three expert calls a week and your Part 2A says you "occasionally consult industry participants," that's a mismatch.

Form ADV requires accurate and truthful disclosures to avoid penalties. Vague or boilerplate disclosure language, like "we sometimes speak to industry participants" without detailing frequency, vetting, or MNPI risk, invites regulatory scrutiny.

### Under-Disclosing Conflicts

Using the same expert network to source both investment insights and investor introductions without describing that dual role in Part 2A is a conflict gap. If experts are compensated by both the adviser and the issuers they discuss, that's another undisclosed conflict.

Disciplinary events involving inadequate conflict disclosure are a recurring theme in SEC enforcement. Disciplinary history tied to these gaps can follow an adviser for years.

### Timing Failures

Expanding expert-call coverage to new jurisdictions or sectors with heavier MNPI risk, then waiting until the next annual updating amendment to update disclosures, is a timing failure. An other than annual amendment should be filed promptly.

### Recordkeeping Weaknesses

Missing call transcripts, incomplete expert profiles, and gaps in vendor contracts make it hard to prove to the SEC that the advisory firm followed its own form adv-described compliance process. Expert networks manage scheduling and compensation for consultations between clients and experts. If you can't show that scheduling, vetting, and compensation were handled properly, your disclosures look hollow.

Networks maintain databases of pre-vetted specialists across various sectors. If your network doesn't, and you haven't documented your own vetting, that's a weakness.

## What Regulators Look For: SEC Focus Areas Tied to Expert Calls

Think of this section as an exam-oriented checklist. Here's how enforcement teams connect form adv statements to actual expert network practices.

### Invoice and Record Comparison

The SEC reviews Part 2A conflicts and methods of analysis disclosures alongside expert call logs, email requests for consultations, and invoices from vendors. SEC and state regulators pull invoices and compare them to what's disclosed in ADV to see if vendor relationships are material and whether costs are properly described.

The [SEC's April 2022 Risk Alert](https://www.sec.gov/file/investment-adviser-mnpi-compliance-issues) flagged advisers with weak policies for ensuring experts don't share MNPI. Examiners cited insufficient contracts, lack of documented compliance procedures, and absence of pre-call scripts or compliance reminders.

### Marketing Material Consistency

What you say in pitch books and websites about "primary research" must match what's in Part 2A and what's in actual practice. Registered advisers that claim "proprietary expert-driven insights" in marketing but describe only "public data analysis" in their brochure create an obvious gap.

Form ADV requires disclosures about assets under management and fees. If marketing materials imply a different fee structure or asset class coverage than what's in the filing, examiners will ask questions.

### Sector and Geographic Risk

If expert network vendors are in countries with weaker confidentiality norms, or in sectors with heavy regulation (biotech, medical devices, government contracts), examiners will scrutinize more. This is especially true for private funds focused on these areas.

Disciplinary events from that enforcement action underscored that expert calls are not informal chats. They're regulated research activity.

## Designing a Compliant Expert Network Program that Matches Your Form ADV

The safest approach to form adv disclosures is designing your expert research program around the disclosures, not the other way around.

### Step 1: Define the Research Model

Before you write a word of ADV language, define what your expert research program actually looks like:

* How many calls per month?

* Which asset classes and sectors?

* Who participates (analysts, PMs, compliance)?

* What vendors do you use?

* Are calls recorded or summarized?

Clients submit requests detailing the expertise needed for matching with professionals. Your internal process should mirror that level of specificity.

### Step 2: Embed Compliance Controls

Build controls that match what you'll describe in your brochure:

1. **Expert vetting**: Check prior employers, confidentiality agreements, blacklists

2. **MNPI screening**: Pre-call questionnaire or compliance review of each expert's profile

3. **Sector blacklists**: Restrict calls with experts in sectors where you hold MNPI from other sources

4. **Training**: Regular MNPI training for both investment staff and experts

5. **Structured call notes or transcripts**: Archive every interaction

Expert networks enable rapid decision-making through quick, targeted interactions. Your compliance program needs to keep pace with that speed without cutting corners.

### Step 3: Write ADV Language That Reflects Reality

Once controls are in place, write your Part 2A language to describe them accurately. Here's an example of updating Item 8 and Item 12 wording after moving from informal expert meetings to a formal network relationship:

**Before (informal):**

> "We may speak with industry participants to supplement our research."

**After (formal program):**

> "We conduct regular consultations with paid experts through vetted third-party networks as a primary component of our methods of analysis. These consultations cover sector trends, competitive positioning, and supply-chain dynamics. We maintain pre-call screening protocols, compliance briefings, and archived call notes. Expert fees are paid directly from management company revenue."

Decide in advance whether expert costs are borne by the management company, passed through to funds, or paid via brokerage arrangements. Then describe that clearly in Part 2A. This decision affects Item 12 (brokerage practices) and investor reporting.

## Practical Checklist: Aligning Your Expert Network Use with Form ADV

Use this before your next annual updating amendment. A CCO or junior compliance analyst can run through it in a few hours.

1. **Inventory all expert vendors and informal expert sources.** List every expert network, independent consultant, and informal industry contact used for research. Note frequency, sectors, topics, cost structures, and affiliation risks.

2. **Map each source to form adv items.** For each vendor or expert source, identify where it should show up in Part 1A (Items 5, 7, 8) and Part 2A (Items 8, 10, 12, 14). If a source isn't reflected anywhere, that's a gap.

3. **Review current brochure language.** Read your Part 2A word by word. Compare it against actual call practices, vendor contracts, and invoices. Look for mismatches, especially if expert calls have become a primary research tool rather than an occasional supplement.

4. **Check marketing material consistency.** Compare your pitch book, website, and investor letters against Part 2A. The terms "primary research," "channel checks," and "expert consultations" should mean the same thing in every document.

5. **Evaluate internal controls documentation.** Confirm you have written expert policies, pre-call scripts, MNPI screening processes, training records, and archived call notes. If any of these are missing, your form adv descriptions aren't backed by evidence.

6. **Assess expense and payment structures.** Confirm how expert costs are paid. Are they management company expenses, fund pass-throughs, soft dollars, or commission-based? Disclose the actual structure in Part 2A.

7. **Set an internal review cadence.** Quarterly reviews of expert usage allow you to catch material changes early and file an other than annual amendment before examiners find the gap.

8. **Involve investment staff in disclosure review.** Have portfolio managers and analysts read draft disclosures. Make sure the described "research process" matches how ideas actually get generated with expert input.
