# SPAC Sponsor Research: A Diligence Field Guide

SPAC sponsor research is the process of evaluating the people, incentives, and track records behind special purpose acquisition companies before you commit capital to a deal. It matters more now than it did three years ago, and it matters for a specific reason: the securities and exchange commission has tightened rules on SPAC disclosures since 2024, and courts have shown they'll hold spac sponsors personally accountable for sloppy diligence.

A SPAC is a shell company with no operations. It exists to raise capital through an initial public offering, find a private company to acquire, and bring that target public through a de spac transaction. At peak, SPACs raised approximately $113 billion across 366 IPOs in 2021. By 2023, that number collapsed to roughly $3.85 billion across just 31 IPOs. The spac market in 2025 and 2026 has partially recovered, but with tighter regulatory scrutiny and far more litigation risk baked into every deal.

Sponsors, PIPE investors, and PE/VC funds now treat sponsor diligence as seriously as target company diligence. That's because Securities Act and Exchange Act exposure doesn't stop at the target's financials. It extends to the sponsor's conduct, projections, conflicts of interest, and the quality of the diligence process itself. If you're deploying capital into a SPAC deal, your SPAC research on the sponsor is your first line of defense — and it sits alongside the [commercial diligence](/blog/commercial-due-diligence-a-practical-guide-for-pe-vc-and-corporate-buyers) you'd run on any other target.

## SPAC basics: where sponsor research fits in the lifecycle

Here's the lifecycle in five steps, stripped to what matters for diligence:

1. **Sponsor formation and fundraise.** A sponsor team, typically seasoned industry executives and financial experts, forms an acquisition corp. They provide initial risk capital for underwriting and compliance fees. Spac sponsors typically receive a 20% stake for minimal investment, known as the promote.

2. **SPAC IPO stage.** The shell company files a Form S-1 and lists spac shares on a stock exchange. SPAC IPOs typically have lower disclosure requirements than a traditional ipo because there's no operating business to describe. Spac securities constituting the initial public offering usually include units (common stock plus warrants). The financial industry regulatory authority and exchange rules govern listing standards.

3. **Target search.** The sponsor has 18 to 24 months to find and close a deal. When a spac identifies a target, real diligence begins. Sponsors conduct due diligence to evaluate a private company's viability as a public entity.

4. **De-SPAC transaction.** A de spac transaction involves a proxy statement or Form S-4 filing with detailed disclosures about the target, financial projections, and merger consideration. Investors can redeem shares if they disapprove a merger. Sponsors organize private investment in public equity deals to raise additional capital and validate the valuation. PIPE investment is used to ensure deal completion and validate valuation.

5. **Post-closing.** The acquired company becomes a publicly traded spac successor. Sponsors often secure board seats to help scale the business post-merger.

Unlike a traditional ipo process, where an existing operating company issues shares publicly and goes through months of bank-led roadshows, the risk profile in spac transactions concentrates in the de-SPAC disclosures and projections. That's where sponsor conduct and research quality get scrutinized.

Who cares about sponsor research:

* **PE/VC funds** evaluating PIPE commitments or co-investment alongside the sponsor

* **Hedge funds** deciding whether to hold through the de spac merger or redeem

* **Corporate M&A teams** considering strategic partnerships with de-SPAC'd companies

* **Board members and advisors** who face personal fiduciary duty exposure

* **Retail investors** who lack the resources to run independent diligence

## Regulatory pressure: why sponsor diligence got real after 2021-2024

The SPAC boom from 2019 through the first half of 2021 produced over $100 billion in annual spac ipo volume and triggered attention from both the SEC and the plaintiff bar. Shareholders filed 30 federal class action lawsuits involving SPACs from 2019 to 2021. Over 60 shareholder lawsuits involving SPACs were filed in state courts during the same period. Regulators took notice.

Here are the concrete regulatory milestones that changed the game:

* **April 2021:** John Coates, then Acting Director of the SEC's Division of Corporation Finance, [warned publicly](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2021/05/sec-threatens-to-slap-spacs) that de-SPAC transactions face liability risks similar to traditional IPOs, and that the PSLRA safe harbor for forward-looking statements may not protect SPAC-related projections.

* **July 2021:** The SEC [charged Stable Road Acquisition Corp. and its target Momentus](https://www.sec.gov/enforcement-litigation/distributions-harmed-investors/matter-momentus-inc-et-al) for materially false statements about the target's technology and national security risks. Both sponsor and target were held liable.

* **2021-2022:** The sec advisory committee and multiple SEC commissioners issued guidance on warrant accounting, projection disclosure, and sponsor compensation transparency. The sec's cf disclosure guidance flagged specific areas where SPAC filings were falling short.

* **January 24, 2024:** The SEC [adopted final rules](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/enhancing-investor-protection-relating-spacs-shell-companies-and-projections) titled "SPACs, Shell Companies, and Projections." These rules require heightened disclosure of sponsor compensation and dilution, narrow the PSLRA safe harbor for forward-looking statements in de-SPAC contexts, and introduce co-registrant status for target companies.

* **2024-2026:** The practical effect is that de-SPAC transactions now face IPO-equivalent liability standards. Financial projections need disclosed assumptions. Sponsor economics, side letters, and deferred ipo underwriting compensation must be transparent. The exchange commission continues enforcement actions.

Regulators now examine sponsor incentives, promote economics, and diligence process quality when assessing alleged misstatements under the securities act and the securities exchange act. Mitigating spac litigation risks requires documented, substantive research, not checkbox compliance.

## Core litigation risks tied to SPAC sponsors

Litigation against spac sponsors clusters around three themes:

**Disclosure accuracy.** Plaintiffs allege that financial projections in proxy statements or S-4 filings lacked a factual basis. Revenue forecasts, subscriber counts, technology claims, and past financial performance metrics turn out to be overstated or fabricated. In the Akazoo case, the company went public via SPAC in 2019, and the SEC later found that claims of paying subscribers and revenue were entirely false. The SEC settled for approximately $38.8 million.

**Conflicts of interest.** Spac sponsors typically receive 20% of the equity for minimal investment. That economic interest creates pressure to close any deal rather than the right deal. Warrant packages, side letters, and sponsor compensation structures can misalign sponsors with public shareholders. Courts have found these conflicts severe enough to trigger heightened scrutiny.

**Process failures.** Due diligence failures can lead to shareholder litigation in SPACs. In the MultiPlan case, [Delaware Chancery held](https://www.akerman.com/en/perspectives/spacs-face-enhanced-scrutiny-for-fiduciary-duties-in-de-spac-transactions.html) that the sponsor and board may have breached defendant's fiduciary duties by failing to disclose that the target's largest customer was developing a competing technology. The court applied the entire fairness standard because of the sponsor's acute conflicts.

Performance data makes this concrete. SPACs launched in 2019 had mean returns of negative 12.3%. SPACs had returns of negative 74% in 2022 and negative 67% in 2023. Investors may face dilution from subsequent fundraising efforts. These numbers fuel litigation.

The most recurring allegations against spac sponsors:

* Financial projections presented without disclosed assumptions or supporting data

* Sponsor promote and warrant economics not adequately disclosed, creating hidden dilution for spac investors

* Inadequate investigation into the target's customers, operations, or regulatory profile

* Omission of material risks that would have affected shareholder consent or redemption decisions

Securities litigation includes both federal class action suits (consolidated federal filings in many cases) and state securities class actions in Delaware and other jurisdictions. Modern media acquisition corp and similar SPACs have faced scrutiny where sponsor diligence fell short of what courts expected.


## Legal framework: Securities Act, Exchange Act, and fiduciary duties

You don't need a law degree to understand the liability map. Here's how it works at each stage:

At the spac ipo stage, the sponsor files a Form S-1 for the shell company. Disclosures are limited because there's no operating business yet. Liability under the securities act applies to misstatements in the registration statement, but there's less to get wrong at this point.

At the de-SPAC stage, everything changes. The Form S-4 or proxy statement describes the target company's business, financial performance, projections, and deal terms. Under the 2024 rules, the target becomes a co-registrant, making both sponsor and target jointly liable under Securities Act Sections 11 and 12. Exchange act Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 claims apply when there's scienter, meaning the sponsor or target knew or should have known statements were false. This is where the bulk of associated litigation risks sit.

Post-closing, the combined entity is a public company. Ongoing disclosure obligations apply. If pre-merger statements turn out to have been misleading, plaintiffs can bring claims referencing securities immediately prior to the business combination transaction and the private offering documents.

Key liability concepts:

* **Reasonable investigation defense.** Under the securities act, defendants can argue they conducted reasonable due diligence into the target's claims. Documentation matters. If you ran expert calls, produced memos, and flagged risks in board decks, you have a defense. If you didn't, you don't.

* **PSLRA safe harbor.** The Private Securities Litigation Reform Act historically protected forward-looking statements accompanied by meaningful cautionary language. Under the [2024 SEC rules](https://kpmg.com/us/en/frv/reference-library/2024/sec-finalizes-spac-rules.html), this safe harbor is narrowed or eliminated for SPACs and blank check companies. Financial projections in de-SPAC filings now carry IPO-level risk.

* **Fiduciary duties.** SPAC directors and sponsors owe duties of loyalty and care to public shareholders. Delaware courts have applied the entire fairness standard when sponsor conflicts are acute, meaning the burden shifts to the sponsor to prove the deal was fair in both price and process.

State "Blue Sky" securities laws can add exposure, but most significant suits reference federal statutes and Delaware corporate law. The target ipo stage of the de-SPAC process is where your diligence either protects you or becomes evidence against you.

## What "good" SPAC sponsor diligence looks like in practice

Treat this as a field manual. You have two to three weeks. Here's what to do.

**1. Understand sponsor team history.** Pull the backgrounds of every sponsor principal and board member. Look for prior SPACs, how those deals performed post-closing, any restatements, and any litigation history. Sponsors of SPACs include seasoned industry executives and financial experts, but credentials alone don't guarantee diligence quality. Talk to people who worked with them before.

**2. Map incentives and cap table.** Get the full picture of sponsor economics: founder shares (the promote), warrants, earn-outs, side letters, and any private investment arrangements. SPAC sponsors typically receive 20% of shares for minimal investment. After warrants and earn-outs, effective ownership can shift dramatically. Defensible valuations are typically 2 to 4 times the size of the SPAC's trust account. If the deal falls outside that range, ask why.

**3. Interrogate target quality.** SPAC sponsors focus on private companies with strong fundamentals and growth projections. Your job is to verify those claims independently. Spac sponsors look for businesses with sustainable and predictable business models, but the question is whether the target actually has one.

Artifacts to collect:

* S-1 (SPAC IPO filing)

* S-4 / proxy statement (de-SPAC filing)

* Investor presentation decks

* PIPE term sheets and subscription agreements

* Warrant agreements and charter documents

* Prior audit reports and any financial advisor engagement letters

**4. Test projected value creation.** Don't accept management's financial projections at face value. Run expert calls with former customers, suppliers, and commercial leaders at the target. Cross-reference revenue claims with channel checks. If the target is a private operating company in healthcare or fintech, talk to regulatory specialists about reimbursement or licensing risk.

**5. Assess post-merger governance.** Effective management teams are critical for SPAC target companies post-merger. Who is staying? Who is leaving? What board seats does the sponsor control? Does the combined entity have the infrastructure for achieving public company status, including audit readiness, internal controls, and compliance?

Deal timelines for closing a de-SPAC transaction include addressing redemption risks. SPACs must complete a merger within 18 to 24 months or liquidate. That deadline creates pressure. Your diligence process should account for it without being compromised by it.


## Designing sponsor research to mitigate litigation risks

The connection between field research and risk reduction is direct. Every expert interview you run can test an assumption that would otherwise appear as an unsupported projection in an SEC filing. Here are the workstreams that matter most.

**1. Product and technology validation.** If the target claims proprietary technology or a differentiated product, talk to former engineers, product managers, and competitors. Can the technology do what the sponsor deck says it can? This is exactly what failed in the Momentus case, where technology claims proved false.

Sample questions: What is the actual state of the core product? How does it compare to alternatives? What are the known technical limitations?

**2. Customer concentration and churn.** How many customers generate what percentage of revenue? Are any of them building competitive alternatives? The MultiPlan case turned on exactly this issue. A single large customer developing in-house capabilities was a material risk that went undisclosed. The spac addressed it too late.

Sample questions: Which customers represent more than 10% of revenue? What's the renewal rate? Have any major accounts churned or signaled intent to leave?

**3. Regulatory and reimbursement risk.** For healthcare, fintech, and other regulated sectors, talk to former regulators, compliance officers, or advisory firms that specialize in the relevant regulatory body. Sponsors must ensure compliance with regulatory requirements in deal structures, and regulators don't care about your SPAC timeline.

**4. Operational scalability.** Targets must have scalable financial processes for public market readiness. Can the company handle quarterly reporting, SOX compliance, and the operational demands of public company status? Talk to former CFOs or controllers.

**5. Management integrity and governance history.** Run reference calls on the target's C-suite. Have they been involved in prior restatements, regulatory actions, or governance failures? A financial advisor involved in the deal may provide some of this context, but independent sources are better.

Document your findings so they support a "reasonable diligence process" narrative if disclosures are later challenged. Memos to file, board decks summarizing expert calls, explicit risk-factor edits linked to findings. This documentation is what makes the difference between a defensible position and a losing one.

Strong diligence does not eliminate exchange act or fiduciary duty risk. But it materially improves defenses and gives boards better grounds to decline or renegotiate weak deals.

## Common SPAC sponsor research mistakes (and how to avoid them)

Here are the errors that show up again and again in litigation filings and post-mortem reviews.

* **Overreliance on management projections.** The target's CEO says revenue will triple in two years. You put it in the proxy. Post-merger, revenue is flat. Plaintiffs quote the projections. This is what happened in several high-profile cases, including Stable Road/Momentus. Fix: verify every material projection with independent sources before it goes into a filing.

* **Skipping channel checks.** You reviewed the data room but never called a customer. A few calls to the target's top five accounts would have revealed churn risk or contract disputes. Fix: budget for 6 to 8 expert calls on customer and supplier relationships, even on a tight timeline.

* **Ignoring reference calls on sponsor principals.** The sponsor team has impressive resumes. But nobody called their former co-investors, board members, or operating partners to ask how they actually behave under pressure. Fix: treat sponsor principal references like you'd treat CEO references in a PE deal.

* **Running diligence for optics.** You generated a 200-question checklist, checked every box, but never followed up on incomplete or evasive answers. Courts and regulators look at substance, not volume. Fix: focus on the 10 to 15 questions that could kill the deal and get real answers.

* **Accepting compressed timelines without adjusting scope.** SPACs can complete mergers within 18 to 24 months post-IPO. Near the end of that window, sponsors may rush diligence or accept inadequate answers just to avoid liquidation. Fix: if you have 10 business days before the vote, prioritize expert calls on customers and former commercial leaders rather than expanding a desk checklist.

* **Failing to document findings.** You ran the calls, identified risks, but didn't memorialize them in a format that counsel and the board can use. Fix: produce written summaries of every expert call tied to specific diligence questions, and ensure they feed into risk-factor disclosure drafting.

* **Not testing the sponsor's initial investment thesis.** The sponsor pitched the deal as a growth story. Six months later, the company is cutting costs. Fix: pressure-test the [investment thesis](/blog/investment-thesis-how-to-write-one-that-gets-funded) with independent experts who have no economic interest in the outcome.

Diligence failures don't just create legal exposure. They destroy returns. When the verification successful narrative falls apart post-closing, investors bear the cost.

## Compliance, conflicts, and interview hygiene in SPAC research

SPAC-related expert calls demand strict compliance controls. There's MNPI risk when you're talking to people connected to a target near a de spac business combination vote. There may be active SEC investigations. The stakes are high.

When accessing SEC filings and regulatory databases during spac research, you'll encounter security measures, including security service checks, malicious bots filtering, respond ray id tracking, and verification successful confirmations. These are standard for online regulatory data access, but they remind you that the data you're pulling is being monitored and logged.

Key compliance safeguards FieldSignal maintains:

* Pre-screening experts for current employment at restricted entities before any call

* Clear instructions to experts not to share material non-public information

* Call logging for audit readiness

* Expert identity verification and conflict checks

What your internal team should do:

* Coordinate with counsel before starting any expert call program. Make sure your restricted list is current.

* Align interview guides with what's appropriate under securities laws. Don't ask questions that invite MNPI disclosure.

* Maintain records of who was called, what was discussed, and how findings were used in disclosure drafting or board deliberations.

**Bad interview examples to avoid:**

* Calling a current employee of the target company without confirming they've cleared it with their employer's legal team. This creates MNPI exposure and potential securities act liability.

* Asking a former executive about debt financing negotiations they were party to while still at the company, without screening for whether those negotiations are ongoing and material.

Well-run expert work is additive to counsel's advice, not a substitute. Structure it so findings are usable in disclosure drafting and board deliberations, and so you can point to the process as evidence of diligence if the deal is later challenged.


## Practical sponsor research checklist before committing capital

Use this before signing a PIPE subscription, supporting a proxy, or recommending a de-SPAC deal internally. This sits alongside counsel's legal review. It doesn't replace it.

**Sponsor team and track record:**

* Have we verified the sponsor principals' prior SPAC outcomes (returns, litigation, restatements)?

* Have we spoken to at least two former co-investors or board members who worked with this sponsor on prior deals?

* Do we understand the sponsor's economic interest at every stage: promote, warrants, earn-outs, side letters?

* Has the sponsor had any prior SEC enforcement actions or material regulatory issues?

**Target company fundamentals:**

* Have we spoken to at least three former senior leaders in the target's commercial organization?

* Do we have independent validation of the target's top five customer relationships (retention, satisfaction, competitive risk)?

* Have we verified that the target's past financial performance matches what's presented in the proxy or S-4?

* Is the target a private operating company with scalable processes for public markets, or does it need significant post-merger investment to function as a public company?

**Deal structure and dilution:**

* Do we have a clear picture of effective sponsor ownership post-closing after all warrants, earn-outs, and private offering conversions?

* What is the total dilution from founder shares, warrants, PIPE, and any debt financing converted to equity?

* Are spac securities and merger consideration terms consistent with comparable de-SPAC deals?

* Has the [financial advisor](/blog/m-a-advisory-what-it-is-and-how-to-choose-a-firm) provided an independent fairness opinion, and do we trust its assumptions?

**Litigation and regulatory risk review:**

* Have we reviewed all limited disclosures and risk factors in the S-4 for completeness, particularly around customer concentration, regulatory exposure, and technology claims?

* Have we checked whether the target, its principals, or the sponsor are subject to any pending or threatened litigation?

* Is the company formed in a jurisdiction with clear corporate governance standards?

* Have we assessed whether the target's sector carries specific regulatory risk that could affect financial performance post-merger (reimbursement changes, licensing requirements, export controls)?

This checklist is meant to be printed as a one-to-two-page internal handout. Customize it for your deal. The point is to force specific answers to specific questions before you deploy capital.
