# Conjoint Analysis Explained: When and How to Use It

## Introduction: Conjoint Analysis in Plain English

Conjoint analysis is a survey-based statistical technique used in market research that forces respondents to make realistic trade-offs between product features and price. Instead of asking people to rate features one by one, it presents complete product profiles and asks which one they'd pick. The result is quantified data on what actually drives purchase decisions.

Conjoint analysis is good for three things: prioritizing product features, setting prices, and estimating market share for new or revised products. There are several ways to run these trade-off experiments, including discrete choice designs, conjoint surveys, and menu based conjoint analysis, each suited to different decision contexts.

This article is written for PE/VC associates, corporate strategy teams, and founders who need data for product and deal decisions, not for academic statisticians. If you're preparing for an IC memo, modeling pricing for a new product line, or stress-testing an acquisition thesis, this is for you.

## Conjoint Analysis Explained: Core Concepts You Actually Need

Conjoint analysis originated in academic research during the 1960s. It has since become an essential tool for product and pricing research in commercial settings. Here are the concepts that matter for practical use.

* **Attributes and levels.** Researchers break down a product into its components during conjoint analysis. An attribute is any dimension that can vary, like price, customer support tier, or contract length. A level is a specific option within that attribute, like $49/month, $79/month, or $99/month. For a B2B SaaS product in 2026, your attributes might be price, user seats, API usage cap, support tier, and contract term.

* **Choice tasks and product profiles.** A conjoint analysis survey shows respondents repeated choice tasks between realistic product profiles rather than single-feature ratings. Each choice task presents 2-5 complete product descriptions. Respondents choose their preferred product profiles in each task, and the pattern of choices reveals what they actually value. Conjoint analysis reveals true preferences rather than stated preferences because it forces consumers to make trade-offs between different combinations of features and prices.

* **Utility values.** The data from conjoint analysis is analyzed to determine the part-worth utility of each attribute level. Higher utility scores indicate stronger preferences for product features. Utilities for levels must be evaluated within the context of an attribute, not across attributes. Conjoint analysis quantifies how much consumers value specific features compared to others.

* **Attribute importance.** Preference scores quantify how much each attribute influences choices. This is expressed as a percentage of total decision weight, derived from the range of utility values within each attribute. More on this in the outputs section below.

* **Discrete choice connection.** Most commercial conjoint analysis work uses discrete choice modeling. This means respondents pick from competing profiles rather than rating or ranking them, which mirrors real purchase behavior more closely.

Here's an example. Imagine you're evaluating an EV charging subscription in 2026. You see three options: Plan A is $20/month, 100 kWh, mobile app support, 12-month contract. Plan B is $35/month, 300 kWh, priority support, no contract. Plan C is $25/month, 200 kWh, standard support, 6-month contract. You pick the one that best balances what you need. That single choice, repeated across 10-15 tasks, generates the conjoint data used to estimate utilities.

## When Conjoint Analysis Is Worth It (And When It Isn't)

Conjoint analysis measures the importance of product attributes in the context of real trade-offs. That makes it the right research method for certain decisions and overkill for others.

**Good fits:**

* **Pricing strategy and structure.** A B2B AI SaaS company in 2026 choosing between seat-based, usage-based, or hybrid pricing needs to know how buyers weigh cost predictability against flexibility. Conjoint tells you that directly.

* **Product roadmap trade-offs.** Should you invest in advanced analytics or improved onboarding? Conjoint analysis helps businesses understand how customers value individual features of a product, so you can prioritize what moves purchase intent.

* **New market entry.** If you're entering an unfamiliar competitive set, you need to hit the right bundle of different features and price. Conjoint analysis predicts market share for different product configurations against incumbents.

* **Menu design and bundling.** Subscription media companies deciding which channels to bundle vs. offer à la carte. DTC brands testing [bundle configurations for pricing tiers](https://www.checkbox.com/blog/conjoint-analysis).

* **Cannibalization questions.** When adding a lower-priced SKU, you want to estimate whether it pulls from high-price customers or attracts new ones.

Conjoint analysis is used for optimal pricing, product development, and market segmentation. It allows businesses to effectively target product designs and marketing campaigns based on quantified customer preferences.

**Bad fits:**

* **Simple concept screening.** If you're still brainstorming and don't know your key attributes, run qualitative interviews first.

* **Brand awareness or tracking.** Conjoint can't measure recall, sentiment, or unaided awareness. Those require different survey instruments.

* **Poorly defined attributes.** If you can't describe realistic attribute levels, results won't map to real decisions.

Conjoint surveys are most valuable when you must make a high-stakes decision before a launch, product line expansion, or acquisition, often under time pressure. ## Conjoint Analysis Methods: CBC, ACBC, Menu-Based, and More

There are four types of conjoint analysis used in commercial market research: CBC, ACBC, MBC, and ACA. Here's what each one does and when to pick it.

* **Choice-Based Conjoint (CBC).** This is the default. Also called choice based conjoint CBC or based conjoint analysis CBC. Respondents see multiple full product profiles and pick one, or choose "none." Choice based conjoint analysis is the most widely used method for product, pricing, and brand work in 2026. It generates clean discrete choice data and works well when you have 4-7 attributes with 2-5 levels each.

* **Adaptive Choice-Based Conjoint (ACBC).** Adaptive choice based conjoint starts by probing must-have or deal-breaker features, then generates choice tasks tailored to earlier responses. ACBC is more customized and engaging. It handles [more attributes efficiently](https://skimgroup.com/methodologies/conjoint-analysis/adaptive-choice-based-conjoint/) but costs more per respondent and takes longer to design.

* **Adaptive Conjoint Analysis (ACA).** This older approach combines direct rating of attributes with fewer conjoint trade-offs. Adaptive conjoint analysis customizes the experience for each respondent. It's useful when you have many product attributes but less precise for discrete choice simulation.

* **Menu-Based Conjoint (MBC).** Menu based conjoint analysis models complex consumer choices where respondents build their own offering by selecting features from menus. Think configuring a telecom bundle or choosing enterprise software add-ons. MBC is the right call when your product or service has configurable components.

**Which method to pick by project type:**

* B2B SaaS tiered pricing with 4-6 attributes: CBC.

* Complex enterprise offering with 8+ attributes: ACBC.

* Telecom or subscription media bundles: MBC.

* Medical device with many feature trade-offs and small sample: ACA or ACBC.

* Sample size is very constrained (under 200): simpler CBC with fewer attributes.

These conjoint analysis methods and conjoint analysis techniques cover the vast majority of commercial applications. Traditional conjoint methods like full profile conjoint analysis, where respondents rate one profile at a time, are rarely used today. Volumetric conjoint analysis, which asks respondents how many units they'd buy at each configuration, appears in CPG contexts but is less common than CBC.

## How a Conjoint Study Actually Works: From Attributes to Results

A proper conjoint analysis experiment has four main stages: design, data collection, modeling, and interpretation. Here's how each step works.

* **1. Define attributes and levels.** Researchers break down a product into its components. Start with qualitative input, expert interviews or customer conversations, to list the product attributes and price points that actually matter. Keep it to 4-7 attributes. Define 6 or fewer attributes to avoid overwhelming respondents. Each attribute should have 2-5 realistic levels. Avoid internal jargon. Use vocabulary your survey respondents will recognize.

* **2. Design the conjoint survey.** Choose your method (CBC, ACBC, MBC) based on complexity. Set the number of choice tasks per respondent. A conjoint survey typically includes 8 to 20 choice tasks. Conjoint analysis creates different combinations of features to simulate virtual products. Use design constraints to prevent impossible product concepts, like enterprise features at freemium price points. Conjoint analysis can test thousands of product configurations in one survey.

* **3. Field to the right audience.** Define and recruit your sample. Generic online panels work for consumer products. For B2B or niche markets, you need precisely targeted decision-makers. Use screener questions to ensure you're reaching the economic buyer, not just any employee. For straightforward CBC with 5-7 attributes, [typical sample size is 300-500 respondents](https://quali-fi.com/learn/conjoint-analysis-sample-size). If you want segment splits (enterprise vs. SMB), each segment should have at least 200 respondents.

* **4. Model utility values.** Most commercial work uses hierarchical Bayes estimation to produce individual-level utility scores from the choice data. The modeling step turns raw survey data into preference scores that quantify respondent preferences in conjoint analysis. Conjoint analysis uses preference scores to quantify consumer choices across all tested attribute levels.

* **5. Run market simulator scenarios.** Use the utility estimates to build hypothetical competitive scenarios. Enter your product configurations and competitors' profiles, then simulate predicted market share, revenue, and willingness to pay under different assumptions. This is where the conjoint analysis results become directly useful for board materials and IC memos.

## Key Outputs: Utility Values, Attribute Importance, and Market Simulators

Decision-makers care about three outputs from conjoint studies. Here's what each one means and how to use it.

**Utility values (part-worths).** These are the numeric scores that indicate preference for each attribute level. Higher utility equals higher preference. They're not meaningful in isolation. You compare them within an attribute to see which levels win, and across attributes to build total profile scores. For example, if "$49/month" has a utility of +0.8 and "$99/month" has -0.6, respondents strongly prefer the lower price. Conjoint analysis identifies the most valued product features among consumers through these utility values.

**Attribute importance.** This tells you the relative importance of each attribute as a percentage of the total decision. Attribute importances sum to 100% across all attributes tested. For instance, a cybersecurity SaaS conjoint might show: price 40%, feature set 25%, brand 20%, contract term 15%. This means price is the biggest driver, but brand and features together outweigh it. The relative value of each attribute helps you decide where to invest. A conjoint analysis study for a detergent brand with 363 respondents [found brand was most important at 31%, followed by price at 24%](https://alidasupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/47088640953876-Conjoint-market-simulator-research-example-for-modern-reports).

**Market simulator.** This is a what-if tool where you enter product configurations and simulate market share and revenue under different scenarios. Market simulators predict market reactions to different product configurations. Want to see what happens if you cut price by 15% and drop a feature? The market simulator shows how predicted share shifts across your profile and competitors'. Market simulators predict consumer buying behavior based on the estimated utilities. Advanced simulators estimate willingness to pay and run sensitivity analyses, which is where you get price elasticity and price sensitivity data for each attribute.

Here's a concrete scenario. You're modeling three competing cybersecurity bundles for the U.S. SMB market in 2026. Bundle A is $29/month with basic threat detection. Bundle B is $49/month with advanced threat detection and compliance reporting. Bundle C is $39/month with advanced threat detection only. The market simulator tells you Bundle B captures 42% preference share, Bundle C gets 35%, and Bundle A gets 23%. Now drop Bundle B's price to $39. Share jumps to 58%. That's the kind of insight that changes a pricing decision.

Conjoint analysis helps estimate price sensitivity and willingness to pay at the attribute level, making it possible to find the most profitable combination of features and price points.

## Design Best Practices: Getting Reliable Conjoint Data

Poor conjoint design produces misleading conjoint analysis results. Here are the rules that matter.

* **Limit attributes.** Keep your conjoint design to 4-7 different attributes. More attributes increase respondent burden and reduce data quality. Conjoint studies typically involve 8-20 choice tasks per respondent, which is already significant cognitive load.

* **Use realistic levels.** Price ranges should match what buyers actually see in market. If your price levels are too wide, price will dominate attribute importance artificially. If too tight, it'll appear unimportant. The same applies to feature levels. Avoid "gold-plated" options nobody would actually buy.

* **Pre-work is not optional.** Run 3-10 qualitative interviews or expert calls to refine attributes and levels before committing to a large conjoint analysis survey. This is where market research experts earn their keep. Without pre-work, you risk building a study around the wrong trade-offs.

* **Manage respondent burden.** 10-15 choice tasks per respondent is standard. More than that leads to fatigue, which shows up as random clicking or simplification strategies like always picking the cheapest option. Pilot the survey to measure dropout rates and completion time.

* **Target the right buyer.** If your product is enterprise-grade, target the economic buyer, not the end user. Their customer preferences and trade-offs are different. Use screener questions to verify role, budget authority, and purchase involvement.

* **Common failure modes.** Including "pet" attributes that don't matter to buyers but matter to your product team. Using attribute levels no one would see in market. [Skipping quality checks on survey data](https://redanalytix.com/why-conjoint-studies-fail/) like attention checks and completion time filters. Automated conjoint experiment designs that create structural confounds where attributes move together unnaturally.

* **Avoid premature narrowing.** Don't constrain the conjoint design to avoid tough trade-offs. If the real decision involves a painful price-feature trade-off, the study needs to include it. Otherwise it can't support the decision it was built for.
