# FactSet Pricing: What It Costs in 2026

## Quick answer: What does FactSet cost in 2026?

A typical full factset workstation license is quoted in the $12,000 to $18,000 per user per year range in 2026 before premium add-ons. FactSet subscriptions typically start at $12,000 per year for full access, but most firms see the headline factset cost rise 20% to 60% above the basic price once real time exchange data, alternative data, and API redistribution rights are included.

FactSet pricing varies based on user type and data needs. Two companies of similar size can see very different quotes depending on whether they're licensing full analyst seats or limited read-only access, how much data coverage they need, and how long their contract term runs. If your budget for a core terminal is under roughly $10,000 per user per year, you'll likely need to look at lighter tools or reduce scope rather than expect FactSet to match that number.

## FactSet overview in 2026

FactSet is a global financial data and analytics platform serving portfolio managers, investment bankers, research analysts, and corporate finance teams. It pulls from multiple data sources to deliver company profiles, fundamentals, estimates, and news across asset classes. FactSet provides advanced analytics for portfolio management, risk management, and quantitative research. Its data includes real time and historical market information.

* Data feeds and api access for quants and data engineering teams

* Excel and PowerPoint plugins for financial analysis and pitchbook automation

**Primary user types:**

* Buy-side analysts and portfolio managers

* Sell side research and equity research teams

* Investment bankers building models and comps

* Corporate strategy and M&A teams

For PE/VC associates, corporate strategy groups, and consultants, pricing transparency matters because you need to justify terminal spend against budget constraints.

## How FactSet pricing works: modules, users, and contracts

FactSet does not publish standard list pricing publicly. Instead, it builds quotes from a few core building blocks: user licenses, data and content bundles, geographic coverage, and contract structure.

There are three main license categories. Full factset workstation licenses give analysts and PMs access to the desktop app, excel add in, screening tools, and core data sets. Limited or read-only licenses provide consumption-based access for executives, strategy leads, and IR teams. API and data-feed-only licenses serve quant teams and data engineering workflows that don't need the workstation user interface.

Pricing is modular. "Core" content typically covers global equities fundamentals, consensus estimates, basic fixed income, and news. Optional modules for ESG scores, alternative data, deep fixed income analytics, private company data, and real time market data are priced separately, sometimes per user and sometimes per firm or per exchange.

Contract length makes a real difference. Buyers often achieve better pricing through multi-year commitments, trading flexibility for lower per-seat rates. Multi-year deals (two to three years) are common in 2026. Minimum user counts may also apply. FactSet data bundles and geographic scope (needing global vs. single-region coverage) further move the quote up or down based on your specific requirements.

## FactSet workstation pricing: full vs. limited access

A full factset workstation typically includes the desktop application, Excel and PowerPoint plugins (FactSet offers a PowerPoint plugin for pitchbook automation), core global equities and fundamentals coverage, basic fixed income data, and research tools. FactSet requires physical installation on each machine for its desktop app.

Full workstation licenses are typically quoted in the $12,000 to $18,000 per user per year range before premium add-ons. FactSet's enterprise subscription typically ranges from $15,000 to $40,000 annually, depending on the breadth of modules and data selected. Limited or read-only seats are often priced at roughly 30% to 60% of a full seat, depending on scope.

**Who gets what:**

* Full workstation access: buy-side analysts, PMs, investment bankers running models, comps, and screens

* Limited access: CFOs, strategy leads, IR professionals, legal professionals who mostly consume dashboards and reports

Mixing full and limited seats is one of the primary levers for keeping factset cost within budget constraints. Here are a few example configurations:

* **Small PE firm:** 3 full seats + 5 limited seats. Full seats handle deal screening and modeling. Limited seats give partners and ops staff read-only access. Blended per-seat cost drops materially compared to all-full pricing.

* **Mid-market corporate strategy team:** 5 full seats for analysts + 15 limited seats for department heads and executives. This keeps the total cost well below what 20 full seats would run.

* **Boutique consulting firm:** 2 full seats shared among senior consultants + 8 limited seats for project teams pulling reports. Per-user blended rate stays closer to the low cost end of FactSet's range.

## Premium data, alternative data, and hidden cost drivers

The base workstation quote is rarely your final bill. These premium data categories significantly impact the total factset cost:

* **Real time exchange data:** Feeds from NYSE, Nasdaq, the London Stock Exchange Group, Eurex, and others are charged per user or per device. This is a frequent source of hidden costs. Firms needing minimal latency streaming or Level 2 order book data pay a steep premium.

* **Alternative data:** Credit card spending panels, web-scraped data, geolocation, and supply-chain data are available through FactSet but typically sold as separate high-priced bundles or metered by usage. Third party data providers often set their own pricing on top.

* **Fixed income and derivatives analytics:** Deep fixed income models, risk management attribution, and multi-asset analytics modules each carry additional per-user or per-firm fees.

* **ESG data:** Scoring and screening tools for ESG are optional modules with incremental pricing.

* **API access and redistribution rights:** Firms integrating factset data into internal dashboards, client reports, or data warehouses pay additional licensing beyond the standard "api access included" line. Sometimes this is per query, per usage volume, or a flat redistribution fee.

**Hidden costs checklist:**

* Onboarding and implementation services ($5,000 to $25,000, per [CostBrief estimates](https://costbrief.com/factset-cost-price-ranges-savings/?utm_source=openai))

* Entitlements management setup for complex multi-entity structures

* Historical "deep history" datasets (decades of data for backtesting, often billed separately)

* Real time data feeds for exchanges beyond core US and European markets

* Annual escalators baked into the contract (CPI-plus or content-upgrade-linked increases)

Your factset cost in 2026 is shaped by several variables a FactSet sales team will adjust in building your quote.

* Whether terminals will be shared across shifts or dedicated per person

* Minimum user commitments and penalties for reducing modules mid-term

These variables interact. A firm with specific workflow requirements for real time derivatives data across five exchanges will see a very different quote than a corporate strategy team pulling quarterly comps from a single market.

## Common "hidden costs" in FactSet contracts and how to surface them

Most budget surprises don't come from the workstation line. They come from items you didn't ask about before signing.

**Pre-signing checklist: require written answers to these questions.**

* What are the per-user exchange fees for every real time feed included in the quote?

* Are premium news services (Dow Jones, Reuters, others) bundled or billed separately?

* Which third party alternative data vendors are billed through FactSet, and at what cost?

* Is historical data back to 1990 (or another target date) included, or billed as a separate deep-history add-on?

* What are the per-query or per-MB overage charges for api access beyond the base allocation?

* What does integration with existing systems (data warehouses, BI tools, internal dashboards) cost as a one-time onboarding project?

* Does the contract include auto-renewal with a short notice period? What's the window to opt out?

* Are there mandatory step-ups to higher minimum user counts in years two and three?

* What penalties apply for removing modules or reducing seats mid-term?

Onboarding projects, including custom feeds, permissioning setups, and training, often create one-time costs that don't show up in the core license line. Contract terms like auto-renewal and mandatory step-ups effectively become hidden costs that legal professionals on your team should review before execution.

## Negotiating FactSet pricing: concrete tactics that work

Start the process 90 to 120 days before your renewal or contract start. Engaging 90 to 120 days before renewal aids negotiation outcomes because it gives you time to run a structured evaluation.

**Step-by-step process:**

1. Define every user role precisely. Map each person to full, limited, or API-only access. This prevents over-licensing.

2. Document a target budget band per user and per group before receiving quotes. Anchor on concrete constraints like fund size, cost per AUM, or fee income. This makes discounts more defensible internally for the vendor's sales team.

3. Run a structured evaluation that includes at least one other financial data provider. Buyers can leverage competitive alternatives to negotiate better pricing, and having a Bloomberg Terminal, Capital IQ, or Refinitiv quote on hand gives you a credible reference point without bluffing about an actual intent to switch.

4. Negotiate caps on annual price increases. This can prevent unexpected costs in years two through five that compound well beyond the year-one quote.

5. Request multi-year terms with escalator caps. Buyers often achieve discounts through multi-year commitments, sometimes 10% to 20% off annualized rates.

6. Ask for ramp-up structures where not all users are billed from day one, especially if you're onboarding a new team.

7. Swap unused modules for new ones instead of adding cost. If your team stopped using a particular dataset, trade it for something you actually need.

Some firms use soft dollars to cover portions of their terminal spend. Check with your compliance team on whether this applies to your structure.

* Are premium news services (Dow Jones, Reuters, others) bundled or billed separately?

* Which third party alternative data vendors are billed through FactSet, and at what cost?

* Is historical data back to 1990 (or another target date) included, or billed as a separate deep-history add-on?

* What are the per-query or per-MB overage charges for api access beyond the base allocation?

* What does integration with existing systems (data warehouses, BI tools, internal dashboards) cost as a one-time onboarding project?

* Does the contract include auto-renewal with a short notice period? What's the window to opt out?

* Are there mandatory step-ups to higher minimum user counts in years two and three?

* What penalties apply for removing modules or reducing seats mid-term?

Onboarding projects, including custom feeds, permissioning setups, and training, often create one-time costs that don't show up in the core license line. Contract terms like auto-renewal and mandatory step-ups effectively become hidden costs that legal professionals on your team should review before execution.

## Negotiating FactSet pricing: concrete tactics that work

Start the process 90 to 120 days before your renewal or contract start. Engaging 90 to 120 days before renewal aids negotiation outcomes because it gives you time to run a structured evaluation.

**Step-by-step process:**

1. Define every user role precisely. Map each person to full, limited, or API-only access. This prevents over-licensing.

2. Document a target budget band per user and per group before receiving quotes. Anchor on concrete constraints like fund size, cost per AUM, or fee income. This makes discounts more defensible internally for the vendor's sales team.

3. Run a structured evaluation that includes at least one other financial data provider. Buyers can leverage competitive alternatives to negotiate better pricing, and having a Bloomberg Terminal, Capital IQ, or Refinitiv quote on hand gives you a credible reference point without bluffing about an actual intent to switch.

4. Negotiate caps on annual price increases. This can prevent unexpected costs in years two through five that compound well beyond the year-one quote.

5. Request multi-year terms with escalator caps. Buyers often achieve discounts through multi-year commitments, sometimes 10% to 20% off annualized rates.

6. Ask for ramp-up structures where not all users are billed from day one, especially if you're onboarding a new team.

7. Swap unused modules for new ones instead of adding cost. If your team stopped using a particular dataset, trade it for something you actually need.

Some firms use soft dollars to cover portions of their terminal spend. Check with your compliance team on whether this applies to your structure.
