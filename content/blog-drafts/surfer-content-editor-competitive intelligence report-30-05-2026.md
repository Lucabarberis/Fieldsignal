# Competitive Intelligence Report: How To Go From Raw Data To Deal-Ready Insight

A competitive intelligence report exists to answer a decision, not to summarize the internet. The right question is specific: “Should we enter this market in 2026?” or “Can we win against Vendor X on price?”

The best CI report turns raw data from filings, interviews, tools, financial statements, press releases, marketing materials, and customer feedback into key insights tied to actions.

## What a competitive intelligence report should deliver (answer upfront)

Every competitive intelligence report should drive three outcomes:

* Clearer investment decisions and product decisions, including whether to enter a new market, fund a roadmap bet, or walk away.

* Sharper positioning against named competitors, including where your own business wins or loses on pricing, product offerings, customer loyalty, and speed.

* Fewer surprises from market shifts, competitor moves, regulatory changes, product launches, pricing changes, and macroeconomic trends over the next 12–24 months.

Competitive intelligence is the collection and analysis of openly-available data on competitors, which can include press releases, advertisements, web content, and patent filings, used to develop business strategies that outperform them.

## What is a competitive intelligence report?

A competitive intelligence report is a structured document that analyzes the competitive landscape, tracking how competitors are positioned, what they're prioritizing, and where the market is headed.

It analyzes competitors, market intelligence, customer signals, market trends, and competitive intelligence data to support a specific decision, such as a Series B memo, 2025 product roadmap, Q4 M&A screen, or pre-LOI diligence.

A CI report is one artifact inside a recurring competitive intelligence CI program. Competitive intelligence should be a dynamic, recurring program rather than a one-time project.

It’s different from market research and business intelligence:

| Type                     | Main focus                              | Example question                    |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Market research          | Market size, customers, demand          | “How large is the market?”          |
| Business intelligence    | Internal KPIs, sales data, operations   | “Why did pipeline conversion drop?” |
| Competitive intelligence | External competitors and market changes | “Why is Vendor B winning deals?”    |
A practical example is a 30–40 slide PDF for a 2026 investment committee meeting. It compares 5 target vendors, pricing, win rates, customer satisfaction, market share, competitor performance, and recommended actions.

## Core components of a decision-ready CI report

Most effective competitive intelligence reports include the same core sections:

* Executive summary, with key insights and decisions required.

* Competitor definitions, including direct competitors, indirect competitors, and aspirational or emerging competitors.

* Market shifts overview, covering market insights, industry trends, and regulatory changes.

* Competitor profiles, with product offerings, marketing strategies, pricing posture, and GTM motion.

* Comparative offer and pricing tables.

* SWOT analysis, PEST analysis, or Porter’s Five Forces.

* Recommended actions for decision makers.

Keep the layout skimmable. Use clear section labels, callouts, charts, and no more than 2–3 key insights per page.

### Executive summary with key insights and actions

The executive summary should be 1–2 pages or 2–3 slides. A partner, VP, founder, or corporate strategy lead should understand the situation in 5 minutes.

Include:

* 3–5 key insights, such as “Vendor B is winning 60% of EMEA RFPs because implementation is faster.”

* 3–5 recommended actions, such as adjusting pricing before Q3 or changing the sales team talk track within 30 days.

* Timing, including what changed in the past year, what changed since 2023–2024, and what’s likely in 2025–2026.

* One small visual, such as a table showing market share movement by segment as of Q1 2026.

Avoid long narrative paragraphs. Use concrete competitor names, numbers, and next steps.

### Defined competitor set and segmentation

Competitors can be categorized into Direct, Indirect, and Aspirational/Emerging categories.

* Direct Competitors offer similar products to the same target audience.

* Indirect Competitors offer different products but solve the same customer problem.

* Aspirational/Emerging Competitors are small startups or large adjacent businesses that could disrupt your industry in the future.

Use tiers instead of a flat list:

| Tier   | Who belongs here                                       | Why it matters                 |
|--------|--------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Tier 1 | Core direct competitors                                | Highest deal risk              |
| Tier 2 | Niche or regional competing companies                  | Important in specific segments |
| Tier 3 | market entrants, nearby businesses, adjacent companies | Future disruption risk         |
Effective competitive intelligence gathering requires a strategy that aligns with business objectives, categorizing competitors into direct, important market players, and high-potential new entrants to monitor relevant activities.

### Market shifts and external forces

This section explains the forces changing the competitive environment.

Cover political, economic, social, and technological shifts from roughly 2020–2026:

* Political and regulatory changes, such as privacy rules, export controls, or antitrust scrutiny.

* Economic shifts, such as higher rates, funding slowdowns, inflation, and budget pressure.

* Social shifts, such as remote work, buyer trust, ESG, or changing procurement behavior.

* Technology shifts, such as AI adoption, automation, patent activity, and platform changes.

Connect each shift to competitor actions. For example, a 2023 funding slowdown may push smaller vendors to discount aggressively. AI adoption may push larger companies to buy teams, accelerate product launches, or repackage same products with new claims.

### Competitor profiles and positioning

This is the heart of the CI report. Each Tier 1 competitor should get a structured 1–2 page profile.

Include:

* Company overview.

* Product lines and different offerings.

* Pricing posture.

* Go-to-market model.

* Geographic focus.

* Recent deals or partnerships from 2023–2026.

* Messaging, brand claims, and marketing materials.

Market Positioning compares messaging, value propositions, and target demographics.

Quote real claims from competitor websites, investor decks, trade shows, or public demos. Then explain what those claims signal about target customers, ACV band, and strategy.

### Comparative offering and pricing analysis

This section compares product, pricing, and commercial terms side by side.

Feature & Pricing Matrices lay out competitor pricing tiers alongside your own to visualize cost differences.

Include 1–2 tables covering:

| Comparison area | What to capture                       |
|-----------------|---------------------------------------|
| Core features   | What each vendor offers               |
| Implementation  | Time, services, integrations          |
| Support         | Standard, premium, dedicated support  |
| Pricing         | Bands, tiers, discounts               |
| Contract terms  | Minimums, renewal terms, usage limits |
Tie pricing intelligence to actual quotes, RFPs, customer interviews, or expert calls. Don’t guess. Label caveats clearly.

Flag where your offer is stronger, weaker, or simply different. That’s what turns data analysis into competitive intel.

### SWOT and PEST analysis for your position

Competitive intelligence can be categorized into various frameworks, including SWOT analysis, PEST analysis, and Porter's Five Forces, which help in understanding competitors' strengths, weaknesses, and market dynamics.

SWOT analysis is a framework that evaluates a company's strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats, helping to identify strategic advantages and areas for improvement.

PEST analysis examines external factors affecting a business, categorized into political, economic, social, and technological influences, and is often used alongside SWOT analysis for comprehensive strategic planning.

Porter's Five Forces framework assesses the competitive forces within an industry, including the threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers and buyers, threat of substitute products, and competitive rivalry.

Porter's Five Forces analysis helps gauge how competitive and profitable an overall industry is by examining the competitive rivalry, threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, and the threat of substitute products.

Use these frameworks as synthesis tools, not filler.

### Customer sentiment and ground truth

This section captures what customers, users, partners, and former employees actually say.

Draw from:

* Expert interviews.

* Win-loss interviews.

* Online reviews.

* Survey data.

* CRM notes.

* Third-party review sites.

Customer Sentiment can be assessed by checking reviews on third-party sites like G2, Capterra, or Trustpilot.

CI reports typically pull data from multiple sources, including social media, digital campaigns, customer reviews, and pricing databases, to provide a comprehensive view of the competitive environment.

Use a few short anonymized quotes, but focus on patterns. If a competitor claims great support and reviews show poor onboarding, that gap is useful.

## How to build a competitive intelligence report step-by-step

The competitive intelligence research process involves identifying competitors, determining what data to collect, finding sources of data, analyzing the data for strengths and weaknesses, and transforming conclusions into actionable items.

There are two main types of competitive intelligence: tactical and strategic, with tactical CI focusing on short-term activities and strategic CI analyzing long-term trends and dynamics.

Tactical competitive intelligence involves gathering information on competitors' current activities, such as product launches and pricing changes, to react quickly to competitive threats or opportunities.

Strategic competitive intelligence focuses on analyzing broader trends in the competitive landscape and industry dynamics to identify potential future threats and opportunities.

### 1. Define scope, decisions, and time horizon

Start with the question the report must answer.

Examples:

* “Pre-LOI view of top 4 competitors before July 2026 IC meeting.”

* “Can we enter Western Europe with a mid-market SaaS ACV of $20k–$150k?”

* “Which product gaps are costing us enterprise deals?”

Define:

* Primary question.

* Secondary questions.

* Geography.

* Buyer segment.

* Deal size.

* Time window, usually past 2–3 years and next 1–2 years.

Also define what not to cover. Exclude legacy tech, micro-segments, or nearby businesses if they won’t affect the decision.

### 2. Identify direct, indirect, and emerging competitors

Build the list from reliable sources, not just Google.

Use:

* Customer shortlists.

* RFP documents.

* Analyst notes.

* CRM win-loss data.

* Expert interviews.

* Sales team feedback.

Direct competitors sell similar products to the same buyer. Indirect competitors solve the same problem with a different product. Emerging competitors may be market entrants, adjacent companies, or startups with a new model.

Tag each competitor by priority so your research depth matches the real risk.

### 3. Gather raw data from credible sources

Gather reliable information utilizing both internal sources and external resources.

Internal Data includes reviewing CRM for win/loss patterns, average contract values (ACVs), and common customer feedback.

External Public Data involves monitoring competitor websites, press releases, job openings, and financial filings.

Also collect:

* Product documentation.

* Pricing sheets.

* Conference talks.

* Patent filings.

* Funding announcements.

* Industry reports.

* Publicly available information from websites and databases.

Desk research gives breadth. Primary research gives ground truth. Interviews with former employees, customers, suppliers, and partners often produce the most valuable insights.

### 4. Structure and analyze the data

Don’t organize findings by source. Organize by decision theme.

Use 2–3 frameworks:

* SWOT analysis.

* PEST analysis.

* Porter’s Five Forces.

* Pricing matrix.

* Market positioning map.

Group findings by:

* Product.

* Pricing.

* GTM.

* Customer satisfaction.

* Sales motion.

* Competitor tier.

Turn interview notes, documents, and such data into tables, timelines, and comparative matrices.

### 5. Draft and refine the CI report

Start with the executive summary. Then build the detail sections.

Most deal-ready CI reports should be 20–40 pages, not 100+. Cut repetitive content and weak signals.

Check that every chart supports a business decision. Align terminology, metrics, competitor names, and pricing assumptions.

### 6. Validate with internal teams and experts

Run findings past people who know the market.

Sales Teams need competitor strengths, weaknesses, pricing, and battlecards to close active deals.

Product Teams require deep dives into feature gaps and technology shifts.

Executives require high-level market positioning, market share changes, and potential threats.

If sales says Vendor A never wins enterprise deals, but expert calls say Vendor A is winning quietly through channel partners, treat the disagreement as a signal.

### 7. Schedule CI report updates and alerts

Fast moving markets punish stale reports.

Use:

* Quarterly refreshes for SaaS and AI-heavy sectors.

* Semi-annual refreshes for stable industries.

* Trigger alerts for funding, M&A, product launches, regulatory changes, and pricing changes.

Competitor analysis platforms can automate data collection and send real-time alerts on critical competitor moves.

Maintain a living tracker. Don’t rebuild from scratch every time.

## Best practices for making competitive intelligence reports usable

Best practices for creating a competitive intelligence report include basing insights on credible data, making insights actionable, structuring reports for clarity, and customizing content for the audience.

Usable means a VP can skim the report before a 30-minute meeting and still act confidently.

### Focus on insights, not volume of data

Raw data isn’t competitive intel.

Limit each section to 3–5 key insights. Move backup material into appendices.

Use charts, bullets, and tables to show patterns. Don’t paste every quote, review, or filing excerpt into the main report.

### Make recommendations explicit and prioritized

A CI report without a recommended response is incomplete.

For each finding, state:

| Finding                           | Action                               | Owner    | Timing  |
|-----------------------------------|--------------------------------------|----------|---------|
| Vendor X discounts heavily in SMB | Adjust talk track and approval rules | Sales    | 30 days |
| Competitor Y leads on analytics   | Re-rank roadmap item                 | Product  | Q3      |
| New entrant is gaining in EMEA    | Monitor hiring and customer wins     | Strategy | Monthly |
Rank actions as:

* Do now.

* Test soon.

* Monitor.

### Customize CI reporting by audience

Different teams need different outputs from the same intelligence.

| Audience          | What they need                                     |
|-------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Board or IC       | Market shifts, risk mitigation, scenarios          |
| Executives        | Tradeoffs, capital allocation, strategic decisions |
| Sales team        | Battlecards, objections, pricing proof             |
| Product marketers | Messaging gaps and positioning shifts              |
| Product           | Feature gaps and roadmap implications              |
FieldSignal projects often support multiple teams because the same expert interviews can feed strategy, product, and GTM work.

### Avoid common CI reporting mistakes

Avoid these mistakes:

* Over-reliance on one data source. A pricing page won’t show actual discounting.

* Mixing fact with speculation. Label assumptions.

* Misquoting pricing. Use dated sources and clear caveats.

* Ignoring small entrants. Many large threats looked irrelevant in their first year.

* Overreacting to one press release. Confirm behavior through customers, partners, or experts.

Rigor and transparency matter more than flashy slides.

## Data sources for modern CI reports (and why expert calls matter)

For 2024–2026 CI work, use a mix of public data, internal data, third-party tools, and human-sourced insights.

According to the [Segment8 State of CI 2026](https://segment8.com/research/state-of-competitive-intelligence-2026?utm_source=openai), mature CI programs now use AI workflows for monitoring, summarizing, and alerting. Tools help, but they don’t replace buyer and operator insight.

### Desk research and public data

Public sources are cheap, broad, and useful for trendlines.

Use:

* Annual reports.

* Financial statements.

* SEC and international filings.

* Funding announcements.

* Pricing pages.

* Job postings.

* Patents.

* Conference presentations.

* Trade shows.

* Competitor websites.

The weakness is lag. Public data often won’t show customer experience, true discounting, or why deals are won.

### Customer reviews, RFPs, and win–loss data

Buyer-side data shows what the market believes.

Use:

* Review sites.

* CSAT and NPS surveys.

* Win-loss interviews.

* Internal CRM notes.

* RFP results from 2022–2025.

Aggregating RFP outcomes shows who gets shortlisted, who wins, and why. Handle all customer data ethically and anonymize sensitive details.

### Expert interviews and competitive intel panels

Expert calls are where many reports get sharper.

Ask about:

* Typical pricing bands.

* Reasons for wins and losses.

* Roadmap themes.

* Product weaknesses.

* Buyer objections.

* Sales motion.

* Partner dynamics.

For a focused report, 3–5 expert calls per key competitor tier often gives enough signal. Larger diligence projects may need more.

## Using CI reports across investment, corporate, and startup decisions

Competitive intelligence enables businesses to develop more effective strategies that lead to sustainable growth and success by providing insights into competitors, market trends, and customer preferences.

Competitive intelligence helps organizations anticipate market shifts and plan for future scenarios, allowing them to maintain or advance their competitive position in the marketplace.

Companies that invest in competitive intelligence can expect a significant return on investment, with estimates suggesting that spending $40,000-$150,000 on intelligence can generate or save many times that amount in extra customer revenue or the avoidance of poor investment decisions.

### PE / VC: pre-investment and portfolio support

PE and VC teams use CI reports to test what a target claims.

A 2–3 week sprint may cover:

* Longlist narrowing.

* Competitive threat assessment.

* Pricing sanity checks.

* Customer satisfaction checks.

* Post-close 100-day planning.

The report helps you avoid overpaying when competition is stronger than the CIM suggests. It also helps spot a competitive edge in a segment the market hasn’t priced correctly.

### Corporate strategy and M&A: market entry and buy vs build

Corporate teams use CI to decide whether to enter a region, launch a product, or buy a player.

A market entry report should compare:

* Current competitors.

* Expected new entrants.

* Regulatory risk.

* Technology change.

* Customer switching behavior.

* Likely competitor response.

For global expansion, 2023–2026 changes in AI adoption, privacy rules, and funding cycles matter. This is risk mitigation before large capital allocation.

### Founders and operators: pre-launch and pre-fundraise

Founders don’t need a 100-page study.

A lean CI report can validate:

* Problem selection.

* Pricing assumptions.

* Differentiation claims.

* Buyer urgency.

* Sales objections.

* Substitute products.

Use desk research plus targeted expert calls. You can get useful market insights in weeks, not months.
