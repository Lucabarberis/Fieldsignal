# Pricing Research Without the Pretense of Frameworks

The academic pricing-research frameworks — Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter, conjoint analysis, anchored willingness-to-pay — produce precise outputs. They are also, in B2B contexts, frequently precise about the wrong things.

This piece is about what actually works in B2B pricing research. The frameworks have a place; it's narrower than the literature suggests.

## What the frameworks are good for

Van Westendorp and its derivatives produce a "price sensitivity map" — a curve showing the percentage of respondents who would consider a price "too cheap", "cheap", "expensive" and "too expensive" at each price point. The intersection of these curves identifies "optimal" and "indifference" prices.

The methodology is rigorous and the curves look authoritative. They're useful for:

- Consumer products with broadly homogeneous buyers
- Categories where the buyer's price expectation is well-formed
- Pre-launch testing of price corridors

They're not useful, in my experience, for B2B contexts where:

- Buyers don't have a strong prior on what the price "should" be
- Pricing is negotiated and the list price is largely fictional
- Buyer segmentation is granular enough that aggregate curves obscure the real signal

## What actually works in B2B

Three approaches that I've found materially more useful than Van Westendorp-style frameworks for B2B pricing:

### 1. Behavioural recall questioning

Instead of asking "what would you pay?" (hypothetical), ask "what did you actually pay the last time you bought this category, and how was that price set?"

Buyers can describe specific past behaviour with reasonable accuracy. They cannot reliably predict future behaviour on hypothetical pricing.

### 2. Channel triangulation

Direct customer interviews tell you what the buyer thinks they pay. Channel partner interviews tell you what the buyer actually pays (and what discount discretion exists). The triangulation is often dramatic — the headline price the customer reports is meaningfully different from the transacted price the channel sees.

### 3. Switching-threshold questioning

Instead of asking "what would you pay?", ask "at what price increase would you actively start evaluating alternatives?" This shifts from hypothetical willingness-to-pay to concrete switching-threshold — which is operationally what you actually need to know.

The framing produces materially more reliable signal than Van Westendorp's "too expensive" curve, in my experience.

## Where the frameworks still have a role

I still occasionally commission Van Westendorp or conjoint work — for consumer products, for highly horizontal B2B products, and for pre-launch corridor testing. The frameworks aren't wrong; they're just over-applied.

For most B2B pricing research, the three approaches above — behavioural recall, channel triangulation, switching-threshold framing — produce more useful signal at lower cost.

## The honest summary

Pricing research is hard because buyers' stated preferences diverge systematically from their revealed preferences. The frameworks try to control for this with statistical rigour. The three approaches above sidestep it by asking better questions.

Both have value. The frameworks promise more than they deliver in most B2B contexts.
