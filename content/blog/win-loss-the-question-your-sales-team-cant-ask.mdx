# The Question Your Sales Team Can't Ask

The standard internal win-loss process: your sales rep calls the customer after the deal closes (or doesn't) and asks for feedback. The customer says something polite. The rep writes it up in the CRM. The aggregate findings, three quarters later, show that you win on "product fit" and lose on "price".

You don't lose on price. You almost never lose on price. You lose for reasons your sales team is the last person on earth who could surface honestly.

The reason is structural. Your sales rep has an ongoing commercial relationship with the buyer — sometimes literally hoping to win the same deal a year later. The customer knows this. The customer is professionally polite to your rep. The polite answer is "price". The polite answer is almost always wrong.

## The one question only a third party can ask

Independent third-party win-loss interviews surface what sales-team interviews can't because the structural conflict isn't there. The customer can be candid without risking the commercial relationship.

The single highest-signal question I've used in 300+ win-loss interviews:

> "Walk me through the internal conversation when your team decided to go with [competitor] instead. Who in the room said what? What was the decisive moment?"

A sales rep can't ask this. It's too direct, it forces the customer into the position of having to defend the decision, and the polite social pressure makes it impossible to answer honestly to the rep.

An independent third-party interviewer asking the same question — 30 minutes into a structured call — gets a real answer about 70% of the time. The other 30% you get a polite reformulation, which is still more informative than what the sales rep would have gotten.

## What you learn

When customers answer the question candidly, the actual loss reasons cluster around four patterns:

1. **Perceived risk.** Your offering looked stronger on paper but felt riskier in the buying group. This is invisible to your sales team because they hear the rationalised "price" answer, not the underlying risk perception.

2. **Internal politics.** Someone in the buying group had a relationship with the competitor's account team and pushed quietly. Your sales team rarely surfaces this.

3. **Specific feature gaps.** Your competitor had one specific capability that the buying group cared about more than your team realised. Sales reps tend to under-weight specific gaps because they're trained to handle them as objections.

4. **Process friction.** Your sales process itself created friction (legal terms, procurement timing, security review) that tipped the decision. Reps don't surface this because they can't change it.

## The compounding value of running this independently

The independent third-party programme produces, in my experience, 3-5x the actionable insight per dollar that internal win-loss produces. The compounding effect is significant: after four quarters of structured external win-loss, the patterns become robust enough to drive material changes to sales process, product roadmap, and pricing strategy.

After eight quarters, the programme typically pays for itself many times over through deal economics that wouldn't have existed without it.

After twelve quarters, leadership tends to question whether they could ever go back to internal win-loss. The answer is they could, but they wouldn't want to.
