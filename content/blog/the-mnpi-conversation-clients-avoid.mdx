# The MNPI Conversation Most Buyers Avoid

When buyers procure expert-network services, compliance discussion is usually delegated. Procurement asks the questions on the standard DDQ. The vendor answers. The MSA gets signed. The buyer's analyst team gets to work.

This works most of the time. It also leaves the buyer materially exposed when it doesn't.

Material non-public information (MNPI) risk is not the network's problem alone. The institutional buyer who consumes the call shares the regulatory exposure. The conversation that procurement isn't having is the one the buyer's compliance officer should be.

## What buyers usually ask

Standard DDQ questions on MNPI:

- Do you screen experts for MNPI risk?
- Do you have cooling-off periods?
- Do you have a documented compliance framework?
- Is your framework externally audited?

Answers from any reputable network: yes, yes, yes, yes. The DDQ ticks the box.

These questions are necessary but insufficient. They confirm the vendor has a policy. They don't tell you how the policy operates in the specific cases that matter for your use case.

## What buyers should also ask

Five questions that have, in my experience, much higher signal value than the standard DDQ:

### 1. "Show me a sample audit-trail record from a recent call."

Anonymised, of course. The audit-trail record is the operational artifact of the framework. If the vendor can't produce a sample in 24 hours, the framework isn't operating cleanly.

### 2. "How do you handle the case where the expert is unsure whether something is MNPI?"

This is the genuine edge case. The expert's call: do they pause? Do they have a clear escalation path? Is the call paused while a compliance officer reviews? Or does the call continue and the network deal with it post-hoc?

Reputable answers all involve a clear pause-and-escalate protocol.

### 3. "What's the cooling-off policy in writing, including sector overlays?"

Headline cooling-off is well-documented (6 months for public companies, typically). Sector overlays vary materially. Healthcare KOL work and pharma trial diligence often have longer cooling-off. Defense work has explicit ITAR overlays. The buyer's compliance officer should see this in writing before MSA signature.

### 4. "How do you screen for conflict-of-interest at the expert level?"

The framework should screen for: current employer relationships, board seats, advisory roles, equity holdings above defined thresholds, recent significant transactions in the topic company. If any of these are not in writing, escalate.

### 5. "What happens if MNPI is shared mid-call?"

The honest answer: it sometimes happens. The protocol matters. The expected protocol: immediate call termination, recipient isolation from trading activity, escalation to compliance, documented incident report. Networks that can't articulate this protocol cleanly are unfit for institutional use.

## What this gets you

Procurement asking these five questions produces material differentiation between vendors that look identical on the standard DDQ. The differences map almost directly to operational maturity.

It also gets you the documentation you'll want if you're ever asked to defend a specific call by regulators or internal audit. The buyer's compliance position is "we asked the right questions, received satisfactory answers in writing, and the audit trail bears out the framework". That's the defensible posture.

## What this won't tell you

These questions don't eliminate MNPI risk. No process does. They reduce risk to a level a competent institutional compliance officer can defend.

If your use case generates aggressive MNPI risk (e.g. you're systematically researching publicly-traded companies through expert calls), procurement-level diligence is necessary but not sufficient. You need ongoing compliance partnership with the vendor, with regular sampling and clear incident-response protocols.

For most institutional buyers, that level of investment is justified.
